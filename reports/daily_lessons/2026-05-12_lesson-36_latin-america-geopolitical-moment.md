## 1. The Core Thesis

Latin America is not a geopolitical sideshow. It is a **critical minerals corridor**, a **manufacturing relocation destination**, and an **active battleground** for US-China strategic competition — all simultaneously. Yet most investment frameworks treat it as a homogeneous "emerging market risk" bucket.

That is a mispricing.

The region holds:
- **49.6%** of global lithium resources (the Lithium Triangle: Chile, Bolivia, Argentina)
- **~30%** of the world's copper reserves (Chile, Peru)
- Significant shares of rare earths, nickel, and manganese
- The Amazon basin — the largest remaining carbon sink on Earth, increasingly relevant to climate credit markets

Add nearshoring momentum pulling manufacturing from Asia into Mexico and Central America, and you have a region that structurally matters more in 2026 than at any point since the Cold War commodity booms.

The investment implication is not "buy LATAM ETFs." It is more surgical: understanding which countries, in which sectors, are exposed to which superpower competition dynamics — and how those dynamics affect long-horizon asset pricing.

## 2. Historical Grounding: The Monroe Doctrine to the Washington Consensus

Latin America's geopolitical position has always been defined by what it is *near* and what it has *underground*.

**The Monroe Doctrine (1823)** declared the Western Hemisphere off-limits to European powers. In practice, it was a US sphere-of-influence claim over a region Washington didn't fully control. For the next century, the US enforced this with gunboat diplomacy, CIA-backed coups (Guatemala 1954, Chile 1973), and economic leverage.

**The Washington Consensus (1989–2000s)** was the economic arm of this hegemony: structural adjustment, privatization, capital account liberalization, fiscal austerity. The IMF enforced the playbook. When it failed — Argentina 2001, Venezuela's collapse, Brazil's repeated crises — the political backlash produced the "Pink Tide": a wave of left-wing governments from Venezuela (Chávez) to Brazil (Lula) to Bolivia (Morales) to Ecuador (Correa) in the 2000s.

The Pink Tide didn't end US hegemony. But it created the opening that China would exploit.

**China's entry (2000s–present):** Beijing offered what the Washington Consensus didn't — no conditions on governance, no lectures on democracy, loans-for-commodities deals (oil from Venezuela, soybeans from Brazil, copper from Chile), and eventually infrastructure via Belt and Road. China became South America's largest trading partner in many countries before 2010.

Here is what this history tells us: **Latin American governments are not choosing between values. They are managing dependency.** The key variable for investment is not left/right politics — it is *which dependency is more tolerable given current conditions*. Countries that have more leverage (larger economies, strategic resources) negotiate better. Countries with less leverage get worse deals, from both patrons.

## 3. The Current Configuration: 2026 Snapshot

### 3a. The Lithium Triangle — A Textbook Geopolitical Asset

Chile, Argentina, and Bolivia sit atop nearly half the world's lithium. Lithium is the battery — which means it is the EV, the energy storage grid, the green transition. Whoever controls the lithium supply chain has structural leverage over the decarbonization of the global economy.

**China moved first and moved hard.** Chinese companies (Ganfeng Lithium, Tianqi, BYD, Zijin Mining) acquired equity stakes, long-term offtake agreements, and processing contracts across the triangle. China now controls a significant portion of the refining and battery manufacturing steps downstream, even where it doesn't mine.

**The US moved late, then aggressively.** The IRA (2022) was in part a response to this: onshoring battery supply chains, with FTA-partner preferences that excluded China and pushed LATAM countries to sign bilateral critical mineral agreements with Washington.

**The key tension:** Chile nationalized its lithium sector in 2023, requiring state miner Codelco to hold majority stakes in new projects. This reduced Chinese influence (Tianqi's SQM stake became less decisive) but also complicated US access. Chile is playing both sides, intentionally. Argentina under Milei has taken the opposite approach — deregulation and capital openness — which has attracted both US and Chinese investment simultaneously.

Bolivia remains the hardest case: the largest lithium reserves, state monopoly control, and a history of resource nationalism that has kept the deposits largely undeveloped. Bolivia is the reminder that geology and geopolitics are not the same thing.

### 3b. Mexico — The Nearshoring Wildcard

Mexico is the most important country in the hemisphere for the *manufacturing* dimension of the geopolitical moment. US companies relocating supply chains from China are the primary driver, and Mexico's FTA access (USMCA) makes it the preferred destination.

The complication: Chinese companies are doing the same thing. Chinese EV manufacturers (BYD, SAIC) have been building Mexican facilities partly to access the US market via USMCA. Washington is aware of this and has been pressuring Mexico City to restrict Chinese investment in sensitive sectors. Mexico's response has been careful ambiguity — it needs US trade, but it also needs Chinese capital.

This dynamic is a microcosm of the whole regional picture: **everyone is trying to maximize benefit from both patrons without being forced to choose.**

### 3c. Colombia's Pivot — A Case Study in Realignment

In May 2025, Colombia became the latest country to join China's Belt and Road Initiative — a significant symbolic break for a country historically tight with Washington. President Petro's government has been repositioning Colombia in a multipolar direction while simultaneously maintaining USMCA-adjacent trade relationships. China responded with infrastructure investment and credit lines as part of Xi's $9.2 billion CELAC credit announcement.

Colombia's BRI accession is a leading indicator: countries that feel under-served by US engagement (particularly on climate, development finance, and trade terms) are testing the Chinese alternative not as an ideological commitment but as a bargaining chip.

### 3d. The Political Cycle — Left, Right, and Back Again

Latin American politics is famously volatile. The Pink Tide that peaked around 2010 gave way to a right-wing correction (Bolsonaro in Brazil, Macri in Argentina, Piñera in Chile). That correction gave way to another left turn (Lula back in Brazil, Boric in Chile, Petro in Colombia, Kirchner successors in Argentina). Now Milei in Argentina represents a new radical right experiment.

The investor lesson: **don't bet on the political cycle. Bet on the structural assets.** Lithium is going to be mined regardless of who is in power — the question is terms of access and royalty structures. Copper demand is driven by electrification, not Buenos Aires politics. The political cycle affects entry conditions and regulatory risk, not the underlying thesis.

The one exception: **resource nationalization risk**. Bolivia has demonstrated that a government can lock up strategic assets indefinitely. Mexico's Lopez Obrador and successor Sheinbaum have reversed parts of energy privatization. This is real risk — but it's priced more accurately by watching *institutional quality* than by left/right labels.

## 4. The Answer to the Framing Question

Why haven't Latin American countries simply taken the better Chinese deal?

Several reasons:

1. **The US deal comes with real enforcement.** Dollar access, SWIFT membership, IMF liquidity, US sanctions against adversaries — these are structural dependencies, not just preferences. No country in the hemisphere can easily walk away from dollar-denominated trade and debt.

2. **China's track record has gotten complicated.** Venezuela's loans-for-oil deals left the country in economic ruin when oil prices fell. Ecuador's BRI-financed hydroelectric plant had quality problems and debt terms that looked extractive in retrospect. The "no conditions" framing increasingly sounds like "worse conditions, less visible."

3. **Domestic politics make alignment costly.** In most LATAM countries, the middle class is pro-democracy and broadly US-aligned culturally. A president who goes full China faces domestic opposition (even if business elites want the investment).

4. **They don't have to choose, so they don't.** The US-China competition has actually *increased* their leverage. Playing both sides is the rational strategy when both patrons are bidding.

The investment implication: the *ambiguity* is the stable equilibrium, not a temporary confusion. Build this into your models.

## 5. Investment Implications

### 5a. The Lithium Thesis: Long, with Caveats

**Directional view:** Long lithium as a structural theme, 3–7 year horizon. Electrification demand is not discretionary at this point — it is embedded in policy mandates across the US, EU, and China simultaneously.

**The caveat:** Lithium is notoriously cyclical. The 2022–2024 bust in lithium carbonate prices (-80% from peak) nearly destroyed several junior miners. Battery chemistry innovation (solid-state batteries, sodium-ion) could reduce lithium intensity over time. **The thesis is structural, but the timing is not trivial.**

**Expression:**
- Chile (via ETFs like ECH or copper majors like Antofagasta/Freeport that have LATAM exposure)
- Argentina's regulatory liberalization under Milei creates new entry points for lithium development companies
- Avoid Bolivia — political risk remains unresolvable on a 3-year horizon

### 5b. Copper: The Cleaner Signal

Copper is the better near-term signal. It underpins everything in the energy transition (wiring, motors, transformers) and has no compelling substitutes. Chile and Peru together are ~40% of global supply.

**Directional view:** Long copper, long horizon. Political disruption in Peru (repeated) is the short-term risk, Chile's nationalization framework is medium-term risk. But the demand curve is structural.

**Key risk to monitor:** Peru's persistent political instability affecting mine permitting. When Peru's mines are disrupted, copper prices spike. This is a recurring pattern worth modeling.

### 5c. Nearshoring — Mexico as the Play

The nearshoring thesis is best expressed through Mexico exposure: MXN appreciation against USD (hedged for volatility), Mexican industrial REITs (FIBRAS), or US-listed companies building manufacturing capacity in northern Mexico.

**The geopolitical risk:** USMCA renegotiation in 2026 creates uncertainty. Trump administration pressure on Chinese content rules could accelerate *or* complicate the nearshoring thesis depending on how Mexico responds.

### 5e. The EM FX Overlay

Latin American currencies are highly sensitive to:
- USD strength (dollar index)
- Commodity prices (primary export revenues)
- US rate differentials
- China demand shocks (biggest buyer for most of the region's exports)

This creates a correlation structure worth modeling: when China growth disappears, LATAM commodity exporters face double pressure (lower prices + Chinese demand reduction). When China grows, LATAM benefits — but that same China growth can pressure the USD, compressing the currency gain.

**Actionable:** Track CLP (Chilean peso), BRL, and PEN as leading indicators for the broader commodity/LATAM thesis.

## 9. Market Connection

The theme of this lesson is **geopolitical leverage creates investment asymmetry.** LATAM countries with strategic assets and institutional capacity (Chile, Mexico) have more negotiating power with both superpowers — which means they can extract better terms from both. Better terms = more stable investment environments = lower risk premium = better long-horizon returns.

Countries without leverage (Bolivia) or with institutional degradation (Venezuela) end up with worse deals from whoever they choose. This is not left/right — it is structural. The market systematically underweights this distinction and instead prices "EM risk" as a single factor.

**Our edge:** a country-level framework that distinguishes leverage from political noise. That's what the Databricks political risk classifier is building.
