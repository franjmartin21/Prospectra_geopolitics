## 1. The Physics Foundation — Why Hydrogen Is Hard

Before any investment framework can be built, the physics must be understood. Most hydrogen investment theses are built by people who understand the molecule abstractly but have not reckoned with the physical constraints that determine its economics.

### 1.1 Hydrogen's Core Properties

Hydrogen (H₂) is the lightest element. Its physical properties create both its promise and its persistent challenges:

**The promise:**
- **Energy density by mass:** 33 kWh per kilogram — roughly three times that of diesel (13 kWh/kg). In applications where weight is the binding constraint (aviation, heavy long-distance transport), hydrogen's mass energy density is unmatched
- **Clean combustion:** Burns to produce water vapor; no carbon dioxide
- **Industrial feedstock:** Already used at massive scale for ammonia synthesis (fertilizer) and petroleum refining — hydrogen has industrial demand before the energy transition even begins

**The problems:**
- **Energy density by volume:** At standard conditions, hydrogen is a gas with density of 0.09 kg/m³ — roughly 3,000 times less energy-dense by volume than diesel. This makes storage and transport profoundly difficult
- **Storage requirements:** To achieve practical volumetric energy density, hydrogen must either be compressed to 350–700 bar (expensive, heavy tanks), liquefied to -253°C (energy-intensive, complex infrastructure), or chemically converted to a carrier molecule like ammonia (NH₃) or liquid organic hydrogen carriers (LOHCs)
- **Embrittlement:** Hydrogen degrades steel pipelines — it diffuses into the metal lattice and makes steel brittle. Existing gas infrastructure cannot typically carry pure hydrogen without modification
- **Energy conversion losses:** Every step of the hydrogen value chain loses energy. Electricity → electrolysis (70–80% efficient) → compression/liquefaction (90%/70% efficient) → transport → reconversion to electricity via fuel cell (50–60% efficient). Round-trip electricity-to-electricity efficiency through hydrogen is approximately **30–40%**, vs. **90–95%** for grid-scale lithium-ion batteries

This last point is the core challenge for hydrogen as an energy storage medium. Physics forces a structural cost penalty relative to direct electrification in any application where direct electrification is possible.

### 1.2 The Color Taxonomy — Not All Hydrogen Is Equal

"Hydrogen" as an energy concept conflates radically different production pathways with wildly different economics, carbon profiles, and geopolitical implications. The investor who does not internalize the taxonomy will be systematically misled by announcements.

| Color | Production method | Carbon footprint | Current cost ($/kg) | Status |
|-------|------------------|-----------------|---------------------|--------|
| **Grey** | Steam methane reforming (SMR) of natural gas; CO₂ emitted | ~10 kg CO₂/kg H₂ | $1.0–2.0 | ~95% of all hydrogen today |
| **Blue** | SMR + carbon capture and storage (CCS) | ~1–4 kg CO₂/kg H₂ (disputed) | $1.5–3.0 | Small share, growing slowly |
| **Green** | Electrolysis of water using renewable electricity | ~0.5–1.0 kg CO₂/kg H₂ (embodied) | $3.0–8.0 | <1% of production; target: scale rapidly |
| **Pink / Red** | Electrolysis powered by nuclear electricity | Near-zero | $4.0–7.0 | Tiny; potential growth with nuclear renaissance |
| **Turquoise** | Methane pyrolysis; produces solid carbon | Low-medium | $2.5–4.5 | Demonstration stage |

**The critical market structure point:** 95% of the hydrogen currently used in industry is grey hydrogen. Almost all the hydrogen discussed in the energy transition context is green hydrogen — a product that barely exists at commercial scale. When politicians and CEOs announce "hydrogen strategies," they are almost entirely describing a future state that does not yet exist economically.

### 1.3 The Electrolysis Equation — Where Green Hydrogen's Cost Lives

Green hydrogen is produced by electrolysis — passing electrical current through water to split H₂O into H₂ and O₂. The economics of electrolysis are simple:

**Cost of green hydrogen = (Cost of electricity / efficiency) + (Capital cost of electrolyzer / utilization) + (Operating costs)**

At current technology:
- Producing 1 kg of green hydrogen requires approximately **50–55 kWh** of electricity (accounting for electrolyzer efficiency)
- If renewable electricity costs $30–50/MWh (achievable in Chile's Atacama, Saudi Arabia's Tabuk, Australia's Pilbara), electricity cost alone is $1.65–2.75/kg H₂
- Add electrolyzer capital recovery ($0.80–2.00/kg) and operating costs → total cost $3–8/kg depending on location and utilization

**The "green premium":** Grey hydrogen costs $1–2/kg. Green hydrogen costs $3–8/kg. The competitive gap is real and large. Eliminating it requires either:
1. Dramatic reduction in electrolyzer costs (these have fallen 60% since 2020, driven by Chinese manufacturing scale — more below)
2. Carbon pricing that makes grey hydrogen more expensive (~$60–100/ton CO2 adds ~$0.60–1.00/kg to grey hydrogen cost)
3. Very cheap renewable electricity (the Atacama or Pilbara cost structure doesn't apply everywhere)
4. Long-term power purchase agreements at below-market electricity prices (controversial: does electrolyzer load compete with the grid?)

The IEA's base case: green hydrogen reaches cost parity with grey hydrogen without carbon pricing somewhere between **2030 and 2040**, depending on the region. The optimistic case (if electrolyzer costs continue falling at current rates): 2028–2032 in the best-resource locations.

## 2. The Demand Story — Where Hydrogen Actually Makes Sense

Not all potential hydrogen applications are equally viable. The key analytical discipline is distinguishing applications where hydrogen has a genuine physical advantage from applications where it is being pushed as a solution for political, lobbying, or strategic reasons.

### 2.1 The Stack-Ranked Application Set

**Tier 1 — Near-certain, already working:**
- **Ammonia production (fertilizer):** Already consumes ~55 million tons of hydrogen per year globally (all grey). Replacing grey H₂ with green H₂ in existing ammonia plants is the most straightforward near-term application. The molecule usage doesn't change — only the upstream production pathway does
- **Oil refining:** Refineries use large volumes of hydrogen for desulfurization. As refineries transition (slowly), this remains a near-term market
- **Direct Reduced Iron (DRI) for green steel:** Instead of using coal to reduce iron ore (Fe₂O₃ + 3C → 2Fe + 3CO₂), hydrogen reduces the iron ore directly (Fe₂O₃ + 3H₂ → 2Fe + 3H₂O). The HYBRIT project in Sweden (SSAB + Vattenfall + LKAB) has been producing green steel commercially since 2024. This is the single most important proof of concept that hydrogen works in hard-to-abate industry

**Tier 2 — Plausible, requires cost reduction:**
- **Chemical industry feedstock:** Methanol, ethanol, and other chemicals can be produced using green hydrogen. Green methanol is already being used by Maersk for "e-methanol" marine fuel — the company has 25 vessels ordered to run on it
- **Long-haul heavy trucking:** Fuel cell trucks (Hyundai XCIENT, Toyota, Daimler's Cellcentric) work technically. Economics are marginal against battery electric at current hydrogen prices, but for vehicles running 1,000+ km daily with very long dwell times, hydrogen refueling has time advantages over charging
- **Maritime shipping:** Green ammonia as bunker fuel is technically sound. The first ammonia-fueled vessels are entering commercial operation in 2025–2026 (MAN Energy Solutions' ammonia engine has commercial orders). Economics are challenged; regulation (IMO's carbon levy on shipping) is improving them

**Tier 3 — Overhyped, structurally challenged:**
- **Passenger vehicles (FCEVs):** Toyota Mirai, Hyundai Nexo are excellent cars that the market has largely rejected. FCEVs face a fundamental problem: BEVs are 3–4x more energy-efficient well-to-wheel. Hydrogen for passenger cars is an answer to a question the market hasn't asked. Japan has spent ¥6 trillion since 1993 promoting hydrogen vehicles with negligible market penetration results
- **Grid-scale energy storage:** The round-trip efficiency penalty (30–40% for hydrogen vs. 90–95% for batteries) means hydrogen loses as a grid storage medium except for very long-duration (seasonal) storage where the capacity cost matters more than the conversion losses. Even then, competing technologies (iron-air batteries, pumped hydro, compressed air) are closer to commercialization
- **Residential heating (boiler fuel):** The UK's hydrogen-for-heat pilot in Whitby was quietly abandoned in 2023. The physics don't work: heat pumps are 3–4x more efficient at converting electricity to heat than burning hydrogen (which was made from electricity)
- **Aviation (hydrogen-fueled jets):** Liquid hydrogen aviation faces an engineering challenge that is likely insurmountable for commercial aircraft — hydrogen's low volumetric density requires fuel tanks 4x the volume of equivalent jet fuel, requiring complete aircraft redesign. SAF (sustainable aviation fuel) made with green hydrogen and captured carbon is far more practical

### 2.2 The "Green Hydrogen in Hard-to-Abate" Thesis

The genuine hydrogen investment thesis rests on a specific claim: **hydrogen is the only viable decarbonization pathway for sectors that cannot be electrified directly.** The sectors that fit this description:

1. **Steelmaking via DRI** — proved out
2. **Fertilizer production (green ammonia)** — technically ready, economically challenged but progressing
3. **Chemical industry (methanol, plastics)** — developing
4. **High-temperature industrial heat (cement, glass, ceramics)** — proven in pilots, not yet at scale
5. **Maritime shipping (ammonia or methanol as fuel)** — entering early commercial stage

These five sectors collectively account for approximately **15–20% of global CO₂ emissions**. If green hydrogen can decarbonize all of them, that is a substantial climate contribution. It is also a multi-decade, multi-trillion-dollar market.

The thesis is genuine. The question is whether the timeline, the economics, and the competitive landscape support investment at current valuations.

## 3. The Geopolitical Architecture — Who Is Positioning and Why

Green hydrogen is inherently a geopolitical commodity because its economics require very cheap renewable electricity, which exists only in specific geographies.

### 3.1 The Global Hydrogen Trade Vision

**The basic proposition:** Countries with abundant solar and/or wind resources (Saudi Arabia, Chile, Australia, Morocco, Namibia) can produce cheap green hydrogen and export it to demand centers (Germany, Japan, South Korea, Netherlands) that have dense industrial economies but insufficient renewable electricity.

This would create a new commodity trade structure analogous to LNG — gas is also produced in resource-rich regions and exported to industrial importers. And like LNG, it raises the same dependency questions.

**The envisioned trade flows:**
- **Australia → Japan, South Korea:** Japanese utilities and steelmakers signed hydrogen import MOUs with Australian producers. The hydrogen would likely travel as liquid hydrogen or ammonia
- **Middle East (Saudi, UAE, Oman) → Europe:** Saudi Arabia's Neom project, UAE's Masdar hydrogen initiatives, Oman's Hyport Duqm facility (24 GW electrolyzer target) — targeting European industrial demand
- **North Africa (Morocco, Egypt) → Europe via pipeline:** The EU-Morocco Green Hydrogen Partnership; existing Maghreb-Europe pipeline infrastructure could potentially carry hydrogen blended with natural gas or eventually pure hydrogen after upgrade
- **Namibia, South Africa → Germany:** The Namibian-German green hydrogen partnership (Hyphen Hydrogen Energy); Namibia's Tsau Khaeb region has exceptional wind resources
- **Chile (Patagonia) → Europe, Asia:** The cheapest potential green hydrogen globally (some studies show <$2/kg by 2030) — Patagonia's wind speeds are among the world's most consistent

### 3.2 The Geopolitics of the "Hydrogen Colonies" Problem

There is a serious critique — emerging from African and Latin American scholars and increasingly from governments — that the global green hydrogen trade architecture risks replicating historical colonial patterns:

- **Resource extraction without industrialization:** If Namibia, Morocco, or Chile export hydrogen molecules to Europe, they export energy but don't necessarily build up industrial capacity that uses that energy domestically. The "processing gap" in critical minerals (previous lesson) has a hydrogen parallel: the value is captured where hydrogen is used, not where it is produced
- **Infrastructure built for export:** Electricity grids, electrolyzers, port facilities, and pipelines built for hydrogen export are designed around foreign demand schedules, potentially undermining domestic access
- **Debt and foreign ownership:** Many African hydrogen projects are being financed by European DFIs and private investors — creating ownership patterns that look familiar from resource history

**The counter-argument:** Producer countries with good governance can capture more value than traditional resource exporters by requiring domestic processing mandates (same lesson as minerals), and hydrogen projects at scale bring capital, technology, and skilled labor that benefits domestic development.

**The geopolitical winner/loser assessment:**
- **Winners:** Countries that have exceptional renewable resources, strong governance, and proximity to industrial demand — Morocco, Namibia, Chile, Australia (if they can resolve the domestic energy access questions)
- **Losers:** Countries that have built entire hydrogen strategies around technologies that aren't working (Japan's liquid hydrogen pathway has been repeatedly delayed; hydrogen FCEVs aren't scaling)
- **The Saudi bet:** Saudi Arabia is funding green hydrogen as part of Vision 2030 and post-oil diversification. If green hydrogen scales, Saudi Arabia could remain an energy exporter in a post-carbon world. The Neom delay is a setback but the strategic logic is sound — the desert solar resource is real and permanent. The question is whether the kingdom can execute at scale

### 3.3 The China Dimension — Dominating Electrolyzers

As with critical minerals processing, China has taken the same strategic approach to electrolyzer manufacturing: capture the manufacturing value chain.

Chinese electrolyzer manufacturers (Peric Hydrogen, CSSC, Sungrow, LONGi Green Energy) have reduced electrolyzer costs by 60–70% since 2020 through manufacturing scale and learning curves. The result:
- Chinese alkaline electrolyzers cost approximately $350–500/kW
- European PEM electrolyzers (ITM Power, Nel) cost $1,000–2,000/kW
- Chinese market share in global electrolyzer manufacturing is approaching 60%

**The IRA/FEOC dynamic:** Just as with EV batteries, the U.S. Inflation Reduction Act's $3/kg hydrogen Production Tax Credit (PTC) is designed to stimulate domestic green hydrogen — but with FEOC compliance requirements that exclude Chinese-made electrolyzers and components. This creates the same tension as in EV batteries: the cheapest supply chain (Chinese) is partially blocked; the alternative supply chain (Western) is more expensive and needs time to scale.

**The strategic bet:** If Western electrolyzer manufacturers can achieve Chinese cost levels through their own scale-up (possible — PEM electrolyzers have better performance characteristics and could win on efficiency gains), the strategic calculus changes. If not, Western green hydrogen projects are structurally disadvantaged relative to Chinese-supply-chain projects in third markets (Chile, Morocco, Namibia), which is where the trade routes are being established.

## 4. What Has Proven Out vs. What Is Hype — The Mid-2026 Scorecard

### 4.1 The Great Project Massacre of 2024–2026

Between 2020 and 2023, approximately $1 trillion in green hydrogen projects were announced globally. By mid-2026, the fate of those announcements:

- **Cancelled or indefinitely delayed:** Approximately 40% of announced capacity (>400 GW of electrolyzer capacity)
- **Scaled back significantly:** 25% (projects proceeding at 20–50% of announced scale)
- **On track or ahead of schedule:** 15% (primarily small-scale industrial pilots and ammonia projects)
- **Uncertain / not yet committed:** 20%

**Notable casualties:**
- **Fortescue Future Industries (FFI):** Andrew Forrest's green hydrogen empire announced ~250 GW of projects globally. By 2026, FFI had cancelled projects in Australia, Norway, and the U.S. and refocused on a much smaller set of genuine near-term opportunities. The gap between vision and execution was vast
- **European Hydrogen Backbone (EHB):** A plan by European gas transmission operators to convert 70,000 km of gas pipelines to carry hydrogen by 2040, at a cost of €80–143 billion. Progress is modest; the regulatory framework remains incomplete
- **Germany's 5 GW electrolyzer target by 2030:** Currently tracking at <1 GW commissioned. The 2030 target has been revised down repeatedly
- **H2Global (German auction mechanism):** Designed to bridge the price gap between imported green hydrogen and fossil alternatives via guaranteed purchase contracts. First auction results in 2024 showed prices significantly above what German industry was willing to pay

**What has worked:**
- **HYBRIT Green Steel (Sweden):** SSAB, Vattenfall, and LKAB producing fossil-free steel commercially since late 2024. The world's first — and it works. Customer list includes Volvo and Mercedes-Benz
- **HyStock (Netherlands):** 20 MW electrolyzer producing hydrogen for industrial use in Groningen, operational since 2023
- **Green ammonia at Yara Pilbara (Australia):** Small-scale green ammonia production using solar electricity; demonstrating the industrial pathway
- **Air Products' NEOM delay notwithstanding:** Their Louisiana blue hydrogen facility and Texas green hydrogen projects are proceeding
- **Maersk's e-methanol fleet:** Not pure green hydrogen, but green hydrogen-derived methanol powering real commercial vessels

### 4.2 Why Projects Are Failing

The pattern of project failures reveals the structural problems:

1. **The electricity additionality problem:** Regulators (especially in the EU and U.S.) require that green hydrogen be produced from "additional" renewable electricity — i.e., new capacity built specifically for electrolysis — not from the existing grid. This additionality requirement exists to prevent electrolyzers from diverting renewable power away from grid decarbonization. But it dramatically increases project cost and complexity: you cannot simply buy cheap grid electricity at night and call it "green"

2. **The curtailment window problem:** The cheapest way to make green hydrogen would be to use electricity when renewable generation exceeds grid demand and prices go negative. But curtailment windows are unpredictable and variable — electrolyzers need stable baseload to be cost-efficient. Variable operation increases capital cost per unit of hydrogen produced

3. **The 2030 offtake gap:** Chemical plants, steel mills, and shipping companies want long-term green hydrogen supply contracts starting by 2030 or 2032. Developers cannot sign contracts for supply they cannot guarantee — because they can't finance projects without contracts. Classic chicken-and-egg financing deadlock

4. **The infrastructure gap:** Even if green hydrogen can be produced cheaply, there is minimal transport and distribution infrastructure — pipelines modified for hydrogen, specialized shipping vessels for liquid hydrogen or ammonia, and end-use equipment modifications at industrial sites. The "hydrogen-ready" industrial ecosystem doesn't exist at scale

5. **The "blending" dead end:** Several countries proposed blending 5–20% hydrogen into existing gas pipelines as a near-term deployment path. This is now widely recognized as a poor use of green hydrogen — the hydrogen is diluted so much it provides minimal carbon reduction while creating metallurgical complications — and is being quietly dropped from most strategies

## 5. Investment Framework — Navigating the Hype Cycle

### 5.1 The Hydrogen Investment Hype Cycle — Where We Are

Using Gartner's Hype Cycle framework:
- **2020–2022:** Hydrogen was approaching the "Peak of Inflated Expectations" — every major energy company, government, and bank announced a hydrogen strategy
- **2023–2025:** The "Trough of Disillusionment" — project cancellations, write-downs, disappointing pilot results. Pure-play hydrogen stocks (Plug Power, Ballard Power Systems, ITM Power, Nel, Bloom Energy) fell 70–90% from their 2021 peaks
- **2026:** At or approaching the "Slope of Enlightenment" for specific applications (green steel, green ammonia, green methanol for shipping) while still deep in the trough for broad applications (FCEV cars, grid storage, residential heating)

**The investor implication:** The trough is where assets are cheapest and long-horizon theses should be built — but only in the applications that have proven real demand. The question is discrimination: which hydrogen applications are genuinely on the Slope of Enlightenment?

### 5.2 Tiered Investment Framework

**Tier 1 — Industrial Gas Companies (Structural Winners Regardless):**
The safest hydrogen investment is not a "hydrogen play" at all — it is the industrial gas companies that will be indispensable infrastructure providers regardless of which hydrogen pathway wins.

- **Linde plc (LIN):** World's largest industrial gas company. Already a major hydrogen producer and distributor. Has existing hydrogen pipelines in industrial clusters across North America and Europe. Every industrial hydrogen application — grey today, green tomorrow — runs through their infrastructure. They win if hydrogen scales
- **Air Liquide (AI FP):** Same rationale. French, global industrial gas major. Has a significant hydrogen portfolio and first-mover advantages in hydrogen refueling for heavy transport
- **Air Products (APD):** More concentrated in hydrogen than Linde or Air Liquide. Higher upside but also higher risk — their NEOM project is the showcase for green hydrogen; delays are a real headwind

**Investment thesis for Tier 1:** These companies are like the "picks and shovels" play in the gold rush. They supply infrastructure to hydrogen users regardless of technology, source, or policy outcome. Trading at modest premiums to historical valuations; dividend-paying; appropriate as core long-horizon positions.

**Tier 2 — Green Steel (Hard-to-Abate Industrial Proof Points):**
- **SSAB (Swedish steel company, SSAB SS B):** The HYBRIT partnership is producing commercially. Green steel customers (Volvo, Mercedes) are paying the premium. SSAB has a credible path to being the world's first fossil-free steel producer at scale by 2030
- **Salzgitter AG (SZG GY):** German steelmaker with SALCOS program — hydrogen-based DRI steel. Received €1bn+ in German government support. Earlier stage than SSAB but with committed demand from auto OEMs
- **Voestalpine (VOE AV):** Austrian integrated steel company, green steel transition underway. More cautious timeline but strategically committed

**Investment thesis for Tier 2:** Green steel is proven. The question is whether the green premium can be captured (auto OEMs are paying it for brand differentiation) and whether Chinese steel competition (which is not green) will compress margins. Best case: a structural premium for certified low-carbon steel builds over time as carbon border adjustments (CBAM in the EU) raise the cost of high-carbon imported steel. Risk: carbon pricing remains inadequate and the green premium collapses.

**Tier 3 — Green Ammonia / Fertilizer Companies:**
- **Yara International (YAR NO):** World's largest fertilizer company. Has committed to green ammonia transition at its Pilbara plant and has significant European production. First-mover in agricultural supply chain green credentials
- **CF Industries (CF):** U.S.-based ammonia/nitrogen fertilizer company. Evaluating green hydrogen integration; received significant IRA hydrogen PTC interest from potential customers

**Tier 4 — Electrolyzer Manufacturers (High Risk, Long Horizon):**
This is where the "hydrogen believers" have typically placed capital — and been badly burned. However, from the trough, selective positions in electrolyzer manufacturers could be appropriate if:
- The company has genuine technology differentiation (PEM vs. alkaline)
- A credible path to profitability (not just revenue growth)
- A strong balance sheet that survives another 3–5 years without profitability

The most defensible names from depth of the trough:
- **Linde / Thyssenkrupp Nucera (NCH2 GY):** Thyssenkrupp's electrolyzer subsidiary, recently IPO'd. Has the industrial engineering infrastructure and customer relationships that pure-play startups lack
- **Nel ASA (NEL NO):** Norwegian, PEM and alkaline electrolyzers, has committed customers (primarily Europe and Americas). Deep trough valuation (down 85% from 2021 peak)
- **ITM Power (ITM LN):** UK-based PEM electrolyzers. Has a manufacturing facility in Sheffield. Raised capital at the bottom; operationally challenged but technically credible

**Speculative/Avoid for now:**
- **Plug Power (PLUG):** U.S. green hydrogen company that went from $3 to $75 in 2021 and is now back near lows. Has struggled with execution, margin compression, and a business model that relied on subsidized electrolyzer sales to third parties while operating its own hydrogen supply chain at a loss. Continue monitoring but the operational track record is poor
- **Ballard Power Systems (BLDP):** Fuel cell maker focused on buses and trucks; technically credible but the market for hydrogen commercial vehicles is smaller and slower than anticipated

## 6. Databricks Angle — The Hydrogen Project Tracker

The most valuable analytical contribution the Databricks platform can make to hydrogen investing is **tracking the gap between announced and realized capacity** — a signal that has reliably predicted investment outcomes (companies with large gaps between their announcements and commissioning dates underperform dramatically).

### 6.1 Green Hydrogen Project Status Tracker

**Goal:** Build a database of all announced green hydrogen projects globally, track their stated timelines and capacities, and flag the gap between announcement and delivery.

**Pipeline:**
```
Data ingestion layer:
    - IEA Hydrogen Projects Database (annual, ~1,500 projects)
    - BloombergNEF Hydrogen Projects Tracker (commercial, ~quarterly update)
    - Project announcements via GDELT news monitoring (continuous)
    ↓
Standardization: normalize capacity (MW electrolyzer), timeline (planned FID, commissioning date),
geography, technology type (PEM/alkaline), and stage (announced/FID/under construction/operational)
    ↓
Gap calculator:
    - Original announcement date vs. current status
    - Original planned capacity vs. scaled-back capacity (if applicable)
    - Original commissioning target vs. current commissioning estimate
    ↓
"Hype Ratio" metric:
    - Announced capacity / commissioned capacity (per company, country, technology)
    - Hype Ratio > 5x = extreme caution zone
    - Hype Ratio < 2x = credible execution track record
    ↓
Investment signal:
    - Companies with low hype ratios and commissioning milestones approaching → long signal
    - Companies with high hype ratios and no FID decisions despite claimed timelines → short/avoid signal
```

**Key datasets:**
- IEA Hydrogen Projects Database: freely available annual update; full project list with stage and technology
- IRENA Renewable Capacity Statistics: regional renewable electricity cost curves — critical input to green hydrogen cost modeling
- FRED / World Bank: natural gas prices (sets the competitive benchmark for grey hydrogen)
- LME/CME: no direct hydrogen commodity price (hydrogen is not yet exchange-traded), but ammonia futures (NH3) are a useful proxy — green ammonia is green hydrogen that has been converted for transport

### 6.2 The Hydrogen Economics Dashboard

**Inputs (daily/weekly automated pulls):**
- European TTF natural gas price (→ grey hydrogen cost input)
- Nordic/German electricity spot prices (→ green hydrogen cost in Europe)
- Chilean/Australian electricity price indices (→ green hydrogen cost in export hubs)
- Electrolyzer spot price index (BloombergNEF publishes quarterly)
- Carbon price (EU ETS: spot price → cost adder to grey hydrogen)

**Outputs:**
- **Green Hydrogen Competitiveness Map:** For each major region, the current all-in cost of green H₂ vs. grey H₂ vs. the breakeven carbon price needed to close the gap
- **Arbitrage Alert:** When the TTF gas spike is large enough that blue/green H₂ approaches grey parity even without a carbon price (this happened briefly in 2022 during the Russia-Ukraine energy crisis) → flag as a potential near-term demand inflection

**CEO note on what this pipeline would have caught in real-time:** In Q3 2022, European natural gas prices spiked to €350/MWh equivalent, briefly making grey hydrogen cost > $8/kg in Europe while green hydrogen in Morocco was available at $4–5/kg. The arbitrage was real but lasted only 6 months before European gas prices normalized. A live dashboard would have flagged this window and the specific green hydrogen projects positioned to benefit.

## 7. Reflection Questions

1. **The physics trap for investors:** Hydrogen bulls often cite energy density by mass (33 kWh/kg — three times diesel) as a key advantage. Hydrogen skeptics cite energy density by volume and round-trip conversion losses. How do you resolve this? Construct a table comparing hydrogen vs. direct electricity vs. battery storage across four key use cases: (a) passenger vehicles, (b) long-haul trucking, (c) steelmaking, (d) maritime shipping. In which use cases does the physics clearly favor hydrogen? In which does it clearly disfavor it? What does this tell you about where the investment thesis lives?

2. **The China electrolyzer dilemma:** Chinese electrolyzer manufacturers have reduced costs by 60–70% since 2020 and now dominate global manufacturing. But U.S. and EU policy (IRA FEOC rules, EU critical supply chain regulation) is designed to exclude Chinese electrolyzers from subsidized projects. This creates a structural cost disadvantage for Western green hydrogen that does not exist in other clean energy sectors (solar panels and batteries also face Chinese competition, but at least the markets for those products are established). If FEOC compliance requirements raise the cost of green hydrogen in the U.S. and EU by 20–30%, what is the practical impact on green hydrogen's competitiveness vs. grey hydrogen? Does this mean the hydrogen transition will happen first in markets that can use Chinese supply chains (Chile, Morocco, Southeast Asia) rather than the markets with the most policy support (U.S., EU)?

3. **The "green hydrogen or green electrons" question:** For any given industrial decarbonization problem, there is a choice between electrification (using electricity directly) and "defossilization via hydrogen" (using green hydrogen as a substitute for fossil fuels). Direct electrification is more efficient wherever it is possible. The hydrogen thesis rests on the claim that some industrial processes cannot be directly electrified. Research one industry (your choice from: cement production, glass manufacturing, aluminum smelting, chemical feedstocks) and determine: Is direct high-temperature electrification technically possible? If so, at what cost premium relative to green hydrogen? What does the competitive analysis imply for the size of the genuine hydrogen market?
