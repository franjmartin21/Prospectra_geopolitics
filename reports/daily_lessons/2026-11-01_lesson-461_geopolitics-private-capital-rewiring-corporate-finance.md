## Opening Question

> *If geopolitical fragmentation splits the world into competing blocs, what happens to the global banking system that knits those blocs together — and who fills the void when it retreats?*

Hold that question. The answer has reshaped one of the largest capital markets in the world over the past decade, and understanding it is essential to positioning intelligently in the years ahead.

## Core Concept: The Retreat of Global Banking and the Rise of Private Credit

For most of the postwar era, cross-border corporate finance ran through a small number of globally integrated banks — JPMorgan, HSBC, Deutsche Bank, Citigroup. These institutions could move capital across borders cheaply and reliably because the world's financial plumbing was integrated under a shared regulatory and geopolitical architecture. Basel III capital rules were harmonized. Sanctions lists were aligned. Correspondent banking networks were universal.

That architecture is now fracturing.

The fragmentation is not sudden — it is structural. It operates through three mechanisms:

**1. Sanctions and the Compliance Premium**
As the US and EU have weaponized the dollar and the SWIFT network, global banks have retreated from entire geographies to avoid secondary sanctions exposure. The result: corporations in "grey zone" markets (Turkey, Saudi Arabia, India, UAE, Southeast Asia) increasingly cannot access Western bank credit at competitive prices — not because they are sanctioned, but because their bankers are afraid of being adjacent to something that might become sanctioned.

**2. Regulatory Ring-Fencing**
Post-2008 capital requirements, combined with national-security-motivated regulatory divergence, have pushed major banks to prioritize their home markets. US banks retreat from Europe. European banks reduce Asian exposure. The global balance sheet has been nationalized in slow motion.

**3. Technology and AI Lending**
AI underwriting has dramatically lowered the cost of originating corporate loans at scale, making it viable for non-bank lenders to enter markets that previously required enormous branch networks and local relationships.

The void left by retreating global banks has been filled by **private credit** — a $3+ trillion asset class that has grown from $500 billion in 2015 to become one of the defining investment phenomena of the 2020s.

## Historical Grounding: Three Eras of Corporate Finance

**Era 1 — Bank-Dominated (1945–1990):** Corporate credit was overwhelmingly intermediated by commercial banks. The Eurodollar market allowed cross-border corporate lending at scale, and banks held most loans on their balance sheets. Geopolitical risk was real but largely channeled through sovereign lending (Petrodollar recycling → Latin American debt crisis).

**Era 2 — Market-Mediated (1990–2015):** Securitization and bond markets rose. Banks originated and distributed, rather than hold. The 2008 crisis temporarily shook this model but didn't end it. Post-2008, banks shrunk their balance sheets under Basel III, creating the first meaningful gap for non-bank lenders.

**Era 3 — Fragmented and Private (2016–present):** The acceleration of geopolitical fragmentation — Brexit, US-China decoupling, SWIFT weaponization, Covid supply chain crisis, Ukraine war — has created structural demand for credit intermediaries that are not constrained by geopolitical compliance regimes. Private credit funds, headquartered in Luxembourg, Singapore, or Abu Dhabi, with limited SWIFT dependency, have moved in.

## Concrete Example: Europe's Private Credit Boom (2020–2026)

The clearest real-world laboratory is Europe.

Following Brexit, European mid-market companies lost easy access to UK banking relationships. Post-Covid, European banks under-lent to small and mid-size businesses — their capital ratios demanded it. The Ukraine war further stressed European banks' Eastern exposure and drove wholesale sovereign-bond losses.

Into this gap stepped US-based private credit managers: Apollo, Ares, Blackstone, Blue Owl, and dozens of European-domiciled peers. By mid-2026:
- European private credit had grown to approximately €800 billion AUM
- Direct lending to mid-market borrowers (EBITDA €5–100M) had effectively displaced the syndicated loan market for companies below investment-grade
- CVC's 2026 data confirmed that bank retrenchment and market fragmentation continued to underpin "a durable opportunity set" for private credit across European private equity and credit

The mechanism is geopolitical at its root: regulatory and sanctions fragmentation reduced the effective supply of bank credit, increasing the risk-adjusted return available to non-bank lenders willing to underwrite the same companies.

**The Investment Corollary:** Private credit returned to investors have been partly a structural arbitrage on geopolitical fragmentation — not just a rate premium over public bonds. As that fragmentation deepens, the structural gap may persist longer than the rate cycle would suggest.

## The Systemic Risk Dimension: What Bears Are Watching

Not everyone is constructive. There are two serious risk arguments that deserve intellectual respect:

**Argument 1 — Opacity Risk**
Private credit lacks the price transparency of public bond markets. When a corporate borrower deteriorates, there is no real-time market signal — the distress shows up in quarterly NAVs with a lag. In a geopolitical shock scenario (sudden tariff escalation, energy price spike, regional conflict), mark-to-model portfolios could be carrying losses that won't be visible for 2–3 quarters. The systemic risk question is whether institutional investors — pension funds, insurance companies, sovereign wealth funds — have adequately stress-tested their private credit allocations against correlated geopolitical shocks.

**Argument 2 — Refinancing Cliff**
Much of the 2020–2022 private credit vintage was underwritten at floating rates, to borrowers carrying 5–7x leverage. As those loans mature in 2025–2027, the refinancing environment is tighter. Ray Dalio has specifically flagged this stack — combined with geopolitical chokepoints like the Strait of Hormuz threatening energy prices — as a potential source of contagion from private credit to broader markets.

**CEO Assessment:** The systemic risk is real but probably non-linear. Most private credit stress will be idiosyncratic rather than systemic. The scenario that converts idiosyncratic into systemic requires a geopolitical shock large enough to simultaneously impair a wide cross-section of mid-market borrowers across geographies. That is a tail risk, not a base case — but tail risks in private credit are uniquely hard to see coming.

## Investment Implications

| Theme | Directional View | Asset Class | Timeframe |
|---|---|---|---|
| Private credit structural growth | Long | Managers with strong European and EM origination capabilities (Apollo, Ares, Blue Owl as public vehicles) | 2–5 years |
| Bank retrenchment beneficiaries | Long | Regional banks in fragmentation-adjacent geographies: UAE, Singapore, India-focused lenders | 18–36 months |
| Systemic risk from opacity | Hedge | Long volatility on credit-sensitive indices (HYG, iBoxx) ahead of geopolitical shock scenarios | 6–12 months (tactical) |
| Reshoring finance | Long | Infrastructure and capex-focused private credit, especially in industrial/manufacturing | 3–5 years |
| Public credit mispricing | Watch | Investment-grade credit spreads — if private credit is absorbing risk at tight spreads, IG/HY may be overpriced relative to true geopolitical risk | 12–24 months |

**CEO Portfolio Note:** The primary trade here is not in the private credit instruments themselves (which are inaccessible to retail and most non-institutional portfolios) — it's in the *managers*. Apollo Global Management (APO), Ares Management (ARES), and Blue Owl Capital (OWL) are public vehicles with direct exposure to private credit AUM growth. Their fee streams are driven by fragmentation persisting. The bear case for these stocks is not a recession — it's reintegration of global banking, which looks structurally unlikely over any 3-year horizon.

## Reflection Questions

1. **The Fragmentation Trade-off:** Private credit thrives when banks retreat, which happens when geopolitical risk rises. But high geopolitical risk also stresses the corporate borrowers that private credit lends to. How do you distinguish between geopolitical conditions that are good for private credit managers versus conditions that impair their portfolios?

2. **The Opacity Problem:** Public markets price risk in real time. Private credit does not. In a sudden geopolitical shock, how should an investor who holds both public bonds and private credit think about their true exposure? What signals (other than NAVs) might give earlier warning of private credit stress?

3. **The Regional Hub Question:** Singapore, Dubai, Abu Dhabi, and Riyadh are all positioning themselves as private credit hubs that are geopolitically "neutral" relative to US/EU lenders. Is neutrality a durable competitive advantage in a fragmenting world — or does it eventually become its own geopolitical risk as blocs harden?
