## 1. The Core Framework: War Has a Price Structure

War economics is not one thing. It is a sequence of distinct economic phases, each with a different market structure and a different set of winners and losers.

**Phase 1 — Shock (Days 0–30)**
The moment conflict begins, markets price uncertainty, not outcomes. Equities fall on risk-off. Safe havens spike: gold, USD, CHF, JPY, Treasuries. Commodities surge if the theater intersects with supply chains. Defense stocks spike on contract anticipation. Every asset class reprices simultaneously — not on fundamentals, but on scenario fan-out.

**Phase 2 — Repricing (Months 1–6)**
Markets begin sorting. The economy of the belligerent nations decelerates sharply. Trade routes reroute. Insurance rates on shipping and aircraft in the theater spike. Energy prices either stabilize (if supply continues) or remain elevated (if supply is disrupted). The currencies of belligerent nations devalue. Defense contractors in *non-belligerent* nations begin booking order flow.

**Phase 3 — Structural Reconfiguration (Months 6–36)**
This is where the durable signals live. Supply chains relocate. Energy infrastructure investment realigns. Countries adjacent to the conflict remilitarize. Fiscal spending in allied nations shifts toward defense and industrial capacity. The market cap of the defense sector grows — not just on current contracts, but on the permanent repricing of the security environment.

**Phase 4 — Scarring (Years 3–10+)**
Research analyzing 115 conflicts across 145 countries over 75 years documents this: wars produce large and persistent declines in output, investment, and trade in the belligerent economies, with *no evidence of recovery even a decade later*. The scarring is permanent. The Ukraine-Russia war alone may cost an estimated **$2.4 trillion** and inflict permanent output losses of at least 15% in the belligerent economies.

The investment lesson: Phase 1 is noise. Phase 3 is signal. Phase 4 is where emerging market political risk repricing occurs.

## 2. The Asymmetry Investors Miss

The dominant investor instinct is: "wars are temporary, fundamentals reassert." This is correct for *some* wars. It is catastrophically wrong for *structural* wars.

**What makes a war structural?**
A structural war changes the security architecture permanently:
- It causes remilitarization of allied blocs (NATO's 2% GDP pledge hardening to action)
- It reroutes critical supply chains that take years to rebuild
- It accelerates the decoupling of economic and security alliances
- It imposes permanent insurance and friction costs on cross-theater trade
- It repositions commodity flows on a multi-year basis

The Russia-Ukraine war is structural. The Arab-Israeli conflicts of 1967 and 1973 were structural. The Korean War was structural. The 2001–present Middle East conflicts were *not* fully structural for Western markets — because Western supply chains were not deeply embedded in those theaters.

**The test:** Does this war change the structural map of where capital, energy, and goods flow for the next decade? If yes, it is structural. Structural wars are not dips.

## 3. Anatomy of the Russia-Ukraine Market Response (2022–Present)

This is the clearest modern case study. Use it as a template.

### February 24, 2022 — Invasion Day

**Equities:**
- S&P 500 fell ~3% on invasion day, then largely recovered within weeks. Classic noise.
- European equities took a more persistent hit — DAX and CAC down 15–20% by March 2022.
- European defense stocks: **+12% abnormal returns** within days of the invasion. Markets immediately understood the structural repricing.

**Oil:**
- WTI crude touched **$133.46/barrel** on March 7, 2022.
- Brent crude reached **$139.13/barrel** — the highest since July 2008.
- This was not panic. This was rational pricing of: Russia = 10% of global oil supply + 40% of European natural gas supply. The supply shock was real.

**Wheat and agricultural commodities:**
- Ukraine + Russia = 30% of global wheat exports, 60%+ of sunflower oil.
- Chicago wheat futures surged 50%+ in weeks.
- This was the agricultural supply chain shock previewed in Lesson 17 — live in markets.

**Defense contractors (non-belligerent, 2022–2024):**
- RTX: revenues $64.3B (2021) → $80B+ (2024). +25%.
- Lockheed Martin: revenues up ~6% over period.
- General Dynamics: revenues up nearly **25% from 2021 to 2024**.
- Arms companies averaged **10 percentage points of cumulative abnormal returns** following the invasion.

**Key contrast:** After the Hamas attack of October 7, 2023, arms company CAR was **statistically indistinguishable from zero**. Why? Because the October 7 attack did not change the structural security architecture of NATO or the major defense procurement nations. Ukraine did. This is the signal vs. noise distinction in action.

## 4. The Five Asset Classes — What War Does to Each

### 4.1 — Energy Commodities
War in or near energy-producing regions produces the most direct price impact.
- **Supply disruption** is the primary mechanism: pipelines destroyed, sanctions applied, shipping routes closed.
- The 2019 drone strike on Saudi Aramco's Abqaiq facility disrupted 5% of *global* oil supply and caused a **20% overnight price spike** — resolved in days when production resumed.
- Structural disruption (Russia-Ukraine) sustains elevated prices for months to years.
- **Directional view:** Long energy (especially European natural gas) on structural wars involving major producers.

### 4.2 — Defense Equities
This is the most direct, reliable wartime trade for long-horizon investors.
- Defense spending is a **lagging indicator** — countries remilitarize over 5–10 year cycles.
- The signal is not "war starts, buy defense today." The signal is "war reveals that defense spending has been systematically underfunded, and that will be corrected over a 5–10 year horizon."
- Prime contractors (Lockheed, RTX, BAE Systems, Rheinmetall) capture the backlog.
- European defense rearmament (2022–present) is a multi-year structural shift, not a trade.

### 4.3 — Currencies
**Belligerent currencies collapse.** The Russian ruble fell 40%+ immediately after the invasion (before recovering partially on capital controls). The Ukrainian hryvnia was pegged and devalued in stages.

**Safe haven currencies rally:** USD, CHF, JPY surge on conflict risk-off.

**Commodity-linked currencies diverge by exposure:** AUD and CAD (commodity exporters) can benefit from commodity price spikes driven by war. TRY, ZAR, or currencies of countries exposed to supply disruption suffer.

**Key dynamic:** Capital flight from conflict-adjacent EM economies can be violent and persistent.

### 4.4 — Sovereign Bonds
**Belligerent nations:** Yields spike (risk premium explodes) or bonds become untradable. Russia's sovereign bonds effectively ceased to trade in Western markets after February 2022.

**Allied nations' fiscal spend:** Defense remilitarization expands fiscal deficits. European sovereign spreads widened in 2022 partly because markets priced in the coming years of defense spending increases. This is a multi-year headwind for European sovereign bonds.

**Safe haven bonds:** US Treasuries benefit initially (flight to quality), but the longer-term fiscal impact of war-driven defense spending creates countervailing upward pressure on yields.

### 4.5 — Equities (Broad Market)
The research finding that "wars are buying opportunities" is an artifact of **selection bias on non-structural conflicts**. It holds for:
- Conflicts that do not involve major trading partners.
- Short-duration conflicts that resolve without structural change.
- Conflicts geographically distant from the investor's economic exposure.

For structural wars involving major economic participants, the buying-the-dip instinct is actively dangerous. The DAX was still below its pre-invasion level 18 months later. European energy-intensive manufacturing (chemicals, steel, aluminum) saw permanent input cost repricing.

## 5. The Investor Decision Tree

When a kinetic event occurs, run this sequence:

```
1. GEOGRAPHIC EXPOSURE
   Is this war in or adjacent to a major commodity-producing region?
   → Yes: Energy/commodities likely to spike. Duration depends on supply chain substitutability.
   → No: Limited commodity impact.

2. TRADE EXPOSURE
   Does this war interrupt a major trade corridor or supply chain?
   → Yes: Structural inflation in affected goods. Beneficiaries are alternative suppliers.
   → No: Limited trade disruption.

3. STRUCTURAL TEST
   Does this war change the security architecture for NATO/major powers?
   → Yes: Defense rearmament cycle begins. Multi-year long defense equities.
   → No: Defense spike is noise. Fade it within 6 months.

4. BELLIGERENT CURRENCY
   Are you holding assets in belligerent nations or their close trading partners?
   → Yes: Exit or hedge currency exposure immediately. Sovereign bond exposure needs review.
   → No: Monitor EM spillovers, especially countries with commodity/energy exposure.

5. TIMELINE TEST
   Will this conflict resolve within 6 months?
   → Likely yes (limited geography, clear off-ramp): Treat as temporary shock. Buy dips.
   → Likely no (structural grievance, no clear off-ramp): Do not treat as a dip. Reprice structurally.
```

## 6. The Mispricings That War Creates

Three systematic market mispricings emerge from kinetic conflict:

**Mispricing 1 — Speed premium.** Markets price the *initial shock* as if it will persist, then overcorrect to "it's a dip." Neither is fully right. The structural mid-term repricing (months 3–18) is where mispricing is most severe. Early movers who understand the structural test can position before the consensus catches up.

**Mispricing 2 — Geography compression.** Markets initially treat a war as a single event, then gradually discover the downstream supply chain linkages. Wheat, neon gas for semiconductors (Ukraine), palladium (Russia) — these supply chain discoveries come in waves. Each wave is an investment opportunity for investors with the framework to see them coming.

**Mispricing 3 — Defense spending lag.** Markets price defense contractor order books, but systematically underprice the *political durability* of defense spending commitments. Politicians announce defense budget increases during crisis, then historically cut them when public attention shifts. Post-Ukraine, for the first time in 40 years, that pattern may be breaking. NATO countries are converting pledges to contracts. This is a multi-year misvaluation in European defense equities.
