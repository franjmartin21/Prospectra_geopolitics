## Opening Question

For 40 years, the global consensus was clear: governments should stay out of markets. Free trade, comparative advantage, deregulation, privatization. The Washington Consensus ruled. Then — almost simultaneously — the United States passed the CHIPS Act and Inflation Reduction Act, the EU launched its Green Deal Industrial Plan, China doubled down on Made in China 2025, India rolled out Production-Linked Incentive schemes, and Japan began subsidizing semiconductor fabs.

**Here is the question that should stop you cold:**

*If every major power is now practicing industrial policy simultaneously — picking winners, subsidizing strategic sectors, weaponizing trade — does "free trade" still exist? And if it doesn't, what are the rules of the new game?*

---

## 1. The Death and Resurrection of Industrial Policy

### The Washington Consensus Era (1980–2015)

After the stagflation of the 1970s, the Reagan-Thatcher revolution established a durable orthodoxy: markets allocate capital more efficiently than governments. Industrial policy — government subsidy of specific industries — was associated with failure: the Soviet command economy, Japan's deflating bubble, Latin America's import-substitution disasters.

The prescription was universal:
- Liberalize trade (WTO, NAFTA, TPP)
- Privatize state-owned enterprises
- Reduce capital controls
- Let comparative advantage determine what each country produces

This was not ideology alone. From 1990 to 2008, global trade expanded from 39% to 61% of world GDP. China's integration into the global economy lifted hundreds of millions out of poverty. The model *worked* — by its own metrics.

### The Three Shocks That Killed the Consensus

**Shock 1 — China's Mercantilist Success (2001–2020)**
China joined the WTO in 2001 but never played by Washington Consensus rules. It subsidized state champions, manipulated its currency, required technology transfers, and used state banks to flood global markets with cheap steel, solar panels, and electronics. The result: the "China shock" — between 2 and 2.4 million US manufacturing jobs lost in a decade. The West got cheap goods; China got industrial capability.

**Shock 2 — COVID Supply Chain Collapse (2020–2022)**
When COVID hit, the US discovered it couldn't manufacture PPE. Taiwan produced 90% of leading-edge semiconductors. China dominated rare earth processing. Decades of "efficiency-maximizing" supply chains turned out to be single points of failure at geopolitical risk. The lesson: efficiency and resilience are not the same thing. The market optimizes for the former; geopolitics demands the latter.

**Shock 3 — Russia's Invasion of Ukraine (2022)**
Europe's dependence on Russian natural gas — a product of 30 years of "economics over security" thinking — became a strategic catastrophe overnight. Germany, the most committed adherent to market logic in the G7, found itself funding Russia's war machine through energy payments while simultaneously opposing it militarily. Energy transition, previously framed as climate policy, became national security policy overnight.

**The synthesis:** By 2023, every major power had concluded that strategic sectors — semiconductors, clean energy, critical minerals, pharmaceuticals, food — could not be left to market forces alone. Industrial policy was back.

## 2. The New Industrial Policy Landscape

### The United States: Mercantilist by Statute

The Biden administration passed two transformational acts:

**CHIPS and Science Act (2022) — $52.7 billion**
Direct semiconductor manufacturing subsidies plus $200B in science funding. The goal: reduce dependence on Taiwan (TSMC) and prevent Chinese access to cutting-edge chips. Result by 2026: TSMC, Samsung, and Intel all building US fabs. The US went from zero advanced chip manufacturing to multiple facilities under construction.

**Inflation Reduction Act (2022) — ~$369 billion in climate/energy incentives**
The largest climate investment in US history, structured primarily as production tax credits for clean energy manufacturing. Key provision: domestic content requirements (the "Buy American" clause) meant clean energy subsidies went to companies manufacturing in the US or friendly-nation supply chains. This triggered immediate protests from EU and allied nations who saw their own industries disadvantaged.

**The Trump Continuity Paradox:**
When Trump returned in 2025, the expectation was reversal. The reality was more complex. Trump *kept* most CHIPS Act funding (too many Republican districts benefiting from fabs). He *gutted* most IRA clean energy credits. But he added something new: **tariffs as industrial policy**. By April 2025, the average US tariff rate hit 27% — the highest since the 1930s. By April 2026, it had moderated to 11.8% after negotiations and legal challenges — but the signal was clear. Eurasia Group named "State Capitalism with American Characteristics" their #7 Top Risk of 2026.

The US had moved from market fundamentalism to explicit economic nationalism. Not in one direction — Biden's approach (subsidize friends) and Trump's approach (tax enemies) are different tools — but both are industrial policy.

### The European Union: Strategic Autonomy Under Pressure

The EU's challenge is structural: 27 member states with different industrial interests, no common fiscal policy, and a rule-book (state aid rules) designed to prevent exactly the kind of subsidies now being deployed globally.

The EU's response has evolved in three phases:

**Phase 1 — Reactive (2022–2023):** The IRA passed and European clean-tech companies began relocating to the US for the subsidies. EU panicked. Loosened state aid rules temporarily. Created the Net Zero Industry Act and the Critical Raw Materials Act.

**Phase 2 — Strategic (2024–2025):** The European Green Deal Industrial Plan (GDIP) consolidated the approach. The EU set binding targets: 40% of clean tech deployed in Europe must be made in Europe by 2030. European Chips Act: €43 billion to double EU semiconductor market share from 9% to 20% by 2030.

**Phase 3 — The Industrial Action Agenda (2026):** The EU's most assertive industrial policy yet. Key mechanism: mandatory domestic/trusted-partner content for state-funded energy and infrastructure projects. Rather than competing dollar-for-dollar with US subsidies (impossible without a common fiscal capacity), the EU is using *demand-side* policy — essentially requiring public procurement to favor European supply chains.

**The strategic bind:** Europe needs the US security umbrella (NATO), is competing with the US on industrial subsidies, and is trying to reduce dependence on China while China remains a major trade partner. This three-way tension defines EU strategic autonomy in 2026.

### China: The Original Industrial Policy State

China never abandoned industrial policy. Made in China 2025 (announced 2015) targeted ten strategic sectors for domestic dominance: advanced manufacturing, AI, aerospace, robotics, semiconductors, and more. The West largely ignored it. They shouldn't have.

By 2026:
- China dominates solar panel production (>80% global share)
- China dominates EV manufacturing — BYD overtook Tesla globally
- China produces ~70% of global battery cells
- China controls ~60% of global rare earth processing
- China is closing the gap in advanced semiconductors (still 1-2 generations behind TSMC)

The West's response — export controls on chips to China, CHIPS Act to re-shore production, IRA to build domestic EV supply chains — is explicitly an attempt to reverse Chinese industrial policy victories. This is the core geopolitical competition of the 2020s: not a military war (yet) but an **industrial capabilities war**.

### India and the Global South: Late Movers, Big Ambitions

India's Production-Linked Incentive (PLI) scheme, launched 2020, offers cash incentives to companies that meet production targets in 14 target sectors: electronics, pharmaceuticals, textiles, food processing, and more. Apple moved significant iPhone production to India. Samsung expanded. The logic: be the "China +1" destination that supply chain diversification creates.

By 2026, India has attracted meaningful manufacturing investment, though execution remains uneven. The geopolitical logic is clear: India is positioning itself as the democratic world's alternative manufacturing base.

## 3. The Investment Architecture of Industrial Policy Competition

This is where the lesson connects directly to portfolio construction.

### Framework: Who Wins in an Industrial Policy World?

Industrial policy creates **guaranteed demand** and **subsidized supply** in specific sectors. For investors, this is a signal — but it requires distinguishing between:

- **Direct beneficiaries:** Companies receiving subsidies or guaranteed contracts
- **Indirect beneficiaries:** Suppliers to subsidized industries
- **Displaced losers:** Companies competing against subsidized incumbents
- **Arbitrage plays:** Companies positioned to capture multi-jurisdictional incentives

### Sector-by-Sector Investment Implications

**Semiconductors — Long domestic producers, long equipment**
The global fab-building spree requires enormous capital equipment. ASML (Netherlands, monopoly on EUV lithography), Applied Materials, Lam Research — these are picks-and-shovels plays on every government's chip ambitions. Long-term: cautious on firms 100% dependent on TSMC/Taiwan supply chain without diversification strategy.

**Clean Energy Manufacturing — Multi-year structural tailwind**
US IRA (partially), EU IAA, and global electrification targets create a 10-15 year demand curve for solar, wind, batteries, and grid infrastructure. The *location* of manufacturing matters enormously for subsidy capture. Watch for re-shoring announcements as leading indicators of which companies are positioning to capture incentives.

**Defense — The NATO dividend**
European rearmament is industrial policy by another name. Germany's 2% GDP defense spending target, Finland/Sweden NATO membership, the EU's defense industrial base investment — these are 10-year procurement cycles. European defense primes (Rheinmetall, Leonardo, Thales, Saab) have structural tailwinds the US market underestimates.

**Critical Minerals — The choke point**
Every industrial policy program runs through critical minerals. Lithium for batteries. Cobalt for EVs. Copper for electrification. Rare earths for defense systems. The Critical Raw Materials Act (EU) and IRA domestic content requirements create demand for non-Chinese mineral supply. Australia, Canada, Chile, and select African producers are the long-term beneficiaries. Mining and processing companies serving Western supply chains are structurally undervalued relative to the demand curve governments are creating.

**Traditional Manufacturing — Tariff winners vs. losers**
With average US tariffs at 11.8% (and selective tariffs much higher), the calculus of where to manufacture has shifted. Companies with US domestic production capacity are insulated; companies with China-dependent supply chains face structural cost headwinds. In equities, this argues for sector neutrality and position focus on supply chain positioning, not just industry exposure.

### The Macro Overlay

Industrial policy at scale is **inflationary**. Here is why:

1. **Subsidies misallocate capital** into sectors that wouldn't otherwise attract it at prevailing returns — this bids up input prices (labor, materials, land) in those sectors
2. **Tariffs raise input costs** directly — the Tax Foundation estimates average US household tariff burden of $1,500/year in 2026
3. **Domestic content requirements** prevent the cheapest global supplier from winning — by definition, adding cost
4. **Competing subsidy races** mean capital is globally redirected toward subsidized sectors, potentially starving others

For portfolio positioning: industrial policy argues for **higher structural inflation** in the West than the 2010s baseline. This means:
- Shorter duration on fixed income than traditional allocations suggest
- Commodity producers as long-term inflation hedges
- Real assets over nominal assets
- Caution on businesses with heavy import dependencies and limited pricing power

## 4. The Second-Order Effects — Where Most Investors Miss the Trade

The obvious trade (buy defense, buy semiconductor equipment, buy critical mineral miners) is already widely understood. The alpha is in second-order effects:

**1. The Fiscal Math**
The US IRA was estimated at $369B. Actual projected cost by 2026: closer to $1 trillion due to uncapped tax credits and faster-than-expected adoption. EU and US industrial policy *combined* represents the largest peacetime government intervention in Western economies since the Marshall Plan. Who finances this? Bond markets. Watch sovereign spreads — the countries with the most aggressive industrial policy without fiscal space to support it are building sovereign risk.

**2. WTO Death Spiral**
Every industrial policy program technically violates WTO principles. The US IRA triggered formal EU disputes. China has filed dozens of cases against US/EU measures. The WTO's dispute resolution mechanism has been functionally broken since the US blocked appellate body appointments in 2019. In a world without enforceable trade rules, bilateral deals and strategic alliances determine market access — not legal frameworks. This is a structural risk for any company or country that assumed rule-based trade was permanent.

**3. The Friend-Shoring Map**
The US "friend-shoring" initiative (trade only with trusted partners) is redrawing global supply chains along political rather than economic lines. The winners: Mexico, India, Vietnam (carefully), Southeast Asia generally, and select EU partners. The losers: any economy that can't clearly align with either bloc. Middle powers — Turkey, UAE, Saudi Arabia — are playing both sides, creating their own arbitrage opportunity.

**4. The Competitiveness Trap**
When every major power subsidizes the same sectors (chips, EVs, batteries, defense), the risk is global overcapacity. We saw this with solar: the US and EU subsidized domestic production; China subsidized at even greater scale; the result was a price collapse that bankrupted most Western solar manufacturers despite the subsidies. The subsidy race can destroy the very industries it aims to protect if coordination fails.

## 5. Signal vs. Noise: Evaluating Industrial Policy Announcements

Not all industrial policy is equal. Framework for assessing whether an announcement is investable signal or political noise:

| Factor | Strong Signal | Weak Signal |
|---|---|---|
| Funding mechanism | Statutory tax credits (hard to repeal) | Annual appropriations (easily cut) |
| Timeframe | 10+ year commitment | Subject to next election |
| Private capital leverage | $1 public → $5+ private | Pure government spending |
| Strategic necessity | Addresses clear vulnerability | Politically motivated |
| Enforcement mechanism | Domestic content requirements | Voluntary targets |
| Cross-party support | Bipartisan | Single-party, close vote |

The CHIPS Act scores high on most metrics (bipartisan, long-term, private leverage). The IRA scores high on tax credit mechanisms but low on political durability — Trump gutted the EV credits within months of taking office. **Distinguish between statutory industrial policy (durable) and executive-order industrial policy (reversible).**

## Market Connection

The return of industrial policy is not a cycle — it is a regime change. The 40-year Washington Consensus era is over. We are now in a world where **governments are active co-investors in strategic industries**, where **geography of production matters as much as cost of production**, and where **trade policy is foreign policy**.

For the Prospectra investment thesis: this is the macro architecture within which all our other analysis sits. Every lesson we've delivered — energy geopolitics, critical minerals, semiconductor geopolitics, currency wars, sanctions architecture — is a chapter in this larger story. Industrial policy is the binding document.

**The CEO's investment thesis update:** In an industrial policy world, the most durable positions are in sectors where government demand is statutory, the private capital leverage ratio is high, and the strategic necessity is non-negotiable regardless of which party holds power. Defense, semiconductor infrastructure, and critical mineral supply chains outside China meet all three criteria. This is where the portfolio's structural long positions should concentrate.

- Industrial policy is back because three shocks (China's mercantilist success, COVID supply chain failure, Russian energy war) proved markets alone cannot deliver strategic resilience

- The investment implications are multi-layered: direct beneficiaries (defense, semiconductor equipment, critical minerals), macro overlays (higher structural inflation, shorter duration), and second-order plays (friend-shoring geography, WTO erosion, fiscal risk)
