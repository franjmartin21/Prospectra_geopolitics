**What's driving it, per the reporting:** a continuation of the de-escalation narrative running since the Lake Lucerne/Bürgenstock roadmap (L132): the IMO secured safety guarantees for over 11,000 seafarers stranded in the Gulf to begin transiting out, and markets are reading "more than 11,000 seafarers stranded... will begin exiting" as a leading indicator that physical shipping is normalizing. OPEC+'s May 3 decision to add a symbolic 188,000 bpd from June — explicitly framed by the group itself as "ready to raise supplies once the war stops," not a response to current demand — adds a second, independent bearish input: supply-side positioning for a war that traders are pricing as more over than the physical evidence confirms.

**What's still contested, per the same protocol that built L132:** Iran's Khatam al-Anbiya Central Headquarters re-declared the Strait fully closed on June 20 (the L131 event); as of today, live tracking shows roughly 23 vessel transits per 24 hours against a ~93/day pre-war baseline — physical throughput at barely a quarter of normal, even as price action behaves as if the risk premium is unwinding. That gap — price moving on a *process* signal (seafarer evacuation logistics, OPEC+ positioning) while the *physical* signal (actual transit counts) remains depressed and contested — is precisely the Tier-1-evidence-vs.-Tier-3-assertion distinction TRACE-V was built to keep separate. Today's price move is real and tradeable; today's "Hormuz is reopening" narrative underneath it is not yet confirmed by the hardest evidence the project has access to.

## Section 2: The Historical Precedent — Markets Price the Resolution Before the Resolution Arrives

This pattern has a clean, two-decades-plus precedent that is worth holding next to today's move: the 1991 Gulf War.

Oil spiked through the second half of 1990 after Iraq invaded Kuwait, on fears that the conflict would spread to Saudi production or close Gulf shipping lanes entirely. Then, on January 17, 1991 — the day the U.S.-led coalition began its air campaign against Iraq — oil prices collapsed roughly 33% in a single trading day, the largest one-day percentage drop in the modern oil market's history at that point. Iraq still physically occupied Kuwait that day. No tanker had moved. No pipeline had reopened. What the market priced was not a resolved war; it was a *high-confidence read that the coalition's air superiority would prevent Iraq from successfully threatening the Gulf's oil infrastructure* — a process judgment about capability, made well ahead of the physical confirmation (which came five more weeks later, when the ground war ended the occupation on February 28).

The 1991 case is the bullish version of this pattern: the market's anticipatory read turned out to be correct, and traders who waited for physical confirmation paid a much higher average price than the one-day-after-the-air-campaign sellers. But the same anticipatory-pricing mechanism has an equally well-documented failure mode — the market pricing a resolution that then stalls or reverses (this project's own June 18–20 whiplash, where transit data signaled a reopening that Iran reversed within 48 hours, is a miniature version of exactly that failure mode, graded honestly in L131). The historical lesson is not "markets are right" or "markets are wrong" about pricing ahead of confirmation — it's that anticipatory pricing is a bet on a *specific causal claim* (air superiority prevents Gulf disruption; seafarer evacuations confirm de-escalation), and the discipline is to identify what that claim is and what would falsify it, not to treat the price move itself as the evidence.

## Section 3: Running Today's Number Through Each Precommitted Condition

**P01 — Long Energy (oil majors/LNG).** Kill switch requires *three* conditions simultaneously: a formal documented Lebanon ceasefire, confirmed IRGC stand-down, AND Brent posting three consecutive weekly closes below $80. L129 (June 20) scored this "0 of 3 legs met." Today's print, if it holds through Friday, would be the *first* of the three required weekly closes on the price leg alone — but the other two legs (Lebanon, IRGC) remain exactly where L129 left them: unmet, with Netanyahu's own language ("the fire has ceased because they stopped attacking us... if they attack again, we will respond") explicitly falling short of a formal ceasefire. **A falling price cannot single-handedly close this position; the conjunction (AND, not OR) is the whole point of writing it that way.** Friday's close matters for one-third of one trigger, not the trigger.

**Gold.** Kill switch requires Brent below $75 AND 10-year TIPS real yield above 3% for 60 days, simultaneously. Today's Brent print (\$74.73, CNBC) is — for the first time since this kill switch was written — arguably through the $75 leg, depending on which quote you trust. But the TIPS leg is nowhere close: the 10-year TIPS real yield closed at 2.25–2.27% as of June 23, more than 75 basis points below the 3% threshold, with no 60-day clock even started. **One leg of a two-leg AND condition moving does not move the position.** Gold's HIGH conviction, last confirmed at L127 against the Warsh FOMC hawkish read, is unaffected by today's oil move and should be reported as such, explicitly, rather than left ambiguous.

**EM energy-importer underweight (TUR/EWY).** Stop-loss: Brent below $80, *sustained for six weeks*, triggers a 50% reduction. Today is data point one of a hypothetical six-week clock — if Friday confirms a sub-$80 close, that clock starts now, not before. This is the one condition among the three where today's move is unambiguously a step toward the trigger rather than a partial or contested one — but "a step toward" is not "triggered," and the six-week window means the earliest this could legitimately fire is early August. Flag it; do not act on it.

**Tankers (FRO/STNG/DHT) — not a kill-switch position, but a live test of the June 22 decoupling claim.** The L131/weekly-briefing thesis explicitly predicts tanker equities should *not* track Brent down, because the rerouting-premium and dividend dynamics run independently of the oil-price level. Today is the largest single-day Brent drop since the L129 episode — a real stress test. Same-day moves: STNG roughly flat-to-down fractionally (-0.91%), DHT up modestly (+0.96%), FRO holding near its June 18 all-time high (~$40.93). **The decoupling thesis held today** — tankers did not sell off in sympathy with the sharpest oil move of the week. One day is not proof, but it is the kind of evidence the position's own framework asked for, and it should be logged as such rather than left unobserved.

## Key Concepts Covered

- Anticipatory pricing: markets price a causal judgment about how a conflict resolves *before* physical evidence confirms it — illustrated by the 1991 Gulf War's 33% one-day oil collapse on the air campaign's start, five weeks before the ground war's physical resolution
- Conjunctive (AND) vs. disjunctive (OR) kill-switch design: why writing "Brent <$X AND condition Y" instead of "Brent <$X OR condition Y" is a deliberate discipline against single-variable overreaction, and why a partial move on one leg of an AND condition should be reported as partial, not rounded up to "triggered"
- The price-vs-physical-evidence gap as a live TRACE-V application: today's de-escalation-priced rally against a contested, still-depressed (~23 vs. ~93 ships/day) transit count
- Distinguishing "a stop-loss clock has started" from "a stop-loss has fired" — the EM underweight's six-week sustained-condition structure
- Treating a thesis's own predicted decoupling as a falsifiable, checkable claim on a specific stress-test day, not just a narrative
