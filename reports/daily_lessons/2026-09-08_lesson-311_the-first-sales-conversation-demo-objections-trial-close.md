## Opening Question

*Lesson 310 filled the top of the funnel. You have a qualified list of 10 names. You have the outreach architecture. You have the email template. And one of them replied: "Sure — I have 30 minutes next Thursday."*

**"You have a 30-minute call booked with a PM at a $800M systematic macro fund who manages meaningful EM exposure. He replied to your cold email about Mexico's GRI spike. Before you open the screen share — what is the single most important thing you are trying to learn in this call?"**

Most founders answer this wrong. They say: "I want to show them the product." But a 30-minute call with a sophisticated institutional buyer is not a demo slot. It is a diagnostic conversation. The demo exists to confirm a hypothesis — not to create one.

The most dangerous failure mode in institutional sales is a founder who runs a great demo, gets genuine interest, and then walks out without a next step because they didn't structure the call to close.

This lesson tells you how to run the call, what to ask, what to say when objections land, and exactly how to move from interest to trial commitment before hanging up.

## I. The Structure of a 30-Minute Institutional Sales Call

A 30-minute call with a senior institutional buyer has zero tolerance for wasted time. They do not want to hear your backstory, your vision, or how hard you worked to build the signal. They want to know if the data is relevant to their mandate, if the methodology holds up, and whether the signal has predictive content. Period.

Here is the only structure that works:

### Minutes 0–5: Context, Permission, Agenda

Open by confirming the reason for the call and asking permission to diagnose before demoing. This takes 60 seconds.

> *"Thanks for taking the time. I sent that Mexico GRI note because I thought the score movement was specifically relevant to EM macro — your fund's mandate based on [what you read about them]. Before I show you anything, I'd rather spend the first few minutes understanding how you currently process geopolitical risk. Is that okay?"*

This does three things:
1. It names the specific thing that earned the call (the signal you sent them), so they remember why they said yes.
2. It signals that you did research on their mandate — not a generic pitch.
3. It inverts the usual dynamic: most vendors open with a pitch. You are opening with a question. This immediately reads differently.

### Minutes 5–15: The Diagnostic

Ask three questions, in this order. Do not shortcut them. Take notes.

**Question 1: The process question**
> *"When a geopolitical event happens — say, a sovereign-level political shock in one of your EM positions — what's your current process for assessing the investment impact? Walk me through it."*

You are listening for: latency (how fast they can react), depth (how far their current analysis goes), and pain (what's frustrating or incomplete about the current process). The answer to this question tells you whether your product solves a real problem for them — or whether they have already built something internally that you are competing against.

**Question 2: The coverage question**
> *"What's your heaviest EM exposure right now? Not the specific positions — just the geographies. Where are you most sensitive to political risk?"*

You are listening for: which countries they care about most. This tells you exactly which GRI data to show them in the demo. A PM who says "Turkey, Mexico, Brazil" needs to see GRI scores for Turkey, Mexico, Brazil — not a generic dashboard.

**Question 3: The signal question**
> *"Is there a recent situation where you wish you'd had a better leading indicator on political risk — something that cost you or that you moved on too late?"*

This is the highest-value question in the call. When a PM answers this question honestly, they are describing the exact use case for your product in their own language. Their answer is better marketing copy than anything you will write. Take it verbatim. Use it in your follow-up email. If they cannot answer this question, it may be because they do not actively manage geopolitical risk — and that is a qualification failure you need to know before investing further.

### Minutes 15–23: The Demo

You now have three critical inputs: their current process, their coverage universe, and a specific historical failure point. The demo exists to show your product solving all three simultaneously.

**What to show (in order):**

1. The GRI weekly score for one of the countries they named — specifically the chart showing movement over the past 90 days. Let them read it for 10 seconds before you say anything.

2. One event annotation: the point on the chart where the score moved sharply, and the specific event that drove it (with source). This demonstrates that the score is not a black box.

3. The methodology card: one slide or one screen, not a whitepaper. The three factor categories (political stability, economic stress, conflict/security), the data sources, the update frequency. Under 90 seconds.

4. If they named a recent political event as their failure point: show the GRI chart for that country, pinpointed to the event. If the GRI moved in advance of the market — even by days — this is your most powerful demo moment. Do not dramatize it. Just show it and let them do the math.

**What NOT to show:**
- The full platform feature list
- Pricing or tiers (unless they ask)
- The Databricks integration architecture (save for Enterprise conversations)
- Coverage of 80+ countries if they only care about 6

### Minutes 23–28: The Close

This is where most founders fail. They finish the demo, get positive signals — nodding, questions, "this is interesting" — and then say "great, well, let me know if you have any questions." And the call ends without a next step.

The close is not a pitch. It is a specific ask for a specific thing.

> *"Based on what you described — your Mexico and Turkey exposure, the gap in your current process on early-warning political indicators — I think the most useful thing I can do is get you a trial pack. It's 28 days, no contract, no commitment: you get the full weekly GRI scores for your coverage universe, the event annotation feed, and direct access to me for any questions on the methodology. We do a check-in call at day 14 and you decide from there. Does that make sense as a next step?"*

Two important mechanics in this sentence:
1. It references what *they* told you (Mexico, Turkey, the gap in their process) — it is not a generic offer.
2. The ask is specific and bounded: 28 days, no contract, a named day-14 touchpoint. It is easy to say yes to because the downside is zero.

### Minutes 28–30: Logistics

If they say yes: confirm their email address, confirm the coverage universe for the trial pack, and tell them when they will receive their Day 0 onboarding materials (within 24 hours). Do not hang up until the follow-up is scheduled.

If they say "let me think about it" or "I need to check with [someone]": ask the only question that matters before ending the call:

> *"Totally understand — what's the biggest open question for you at this point? Is it the methodology, the coverage, the timing, or something else?"*

Their answer tells you what the real objection is. Every other response from you in the call was designed to surface objections before they go silent — this is the last chance to hear it while they are still on the phone.

## II. The Seven Institutional Objections — and What They Actually Mean

Sophisticated institutional buyers do not say "this is too expensive" or "I'm not interested." They say things that sound like questions but are actually objections. Knowing the real meaning behind each one gives you control of the conversation.

### Objection 1: "How does this compare to X?"
*(X = MSCI ESG, Eurasia Group, Oxford Analytica, Bloomberg country risk, a bank's geopolitical desk)*

**What it means:** They have an existing relationship and they are asking whether you are a replacement or a complement. Replacement requires budget displacement. Complement is an easier yes.

**The answer:**
> *"Different tools solve different problems. Eurasia Group and Oxford Analytica are narrative — they give you qualitative geopolitical analysis, which is high-quality judgment. The GRI is a quantitative signal — a numeric score, updated weekly, built for systematic integration and backtesting. They are not competing with each other. If you use Eurasia Group, the GRI is additive because it gives you a way to track the quantitative direction of the dynamics they describe narratively."*

Never position against a vendor they already trust. Position as additive.

### Objection 2: "We build our own models internally."
**What it means:** Either (a) they genuinely have a quant team that could build this, in which case the question is speed-to-quality and ongoing maintenance; or (b) it is a polite deflection, which means the real objection is elsewhere.

**The answer:**
> *"Most sophisticated shops can build something like this — the question is whether it's the best use of the quant team's time. The GDELT pipeline alone is 2–3 months of engineering to do well, and it requires ongoing maintenance as the data source evolves. The trial costs you nothing. If your team can do it better, you will know in 28 days and you will have a useful benchmark to evaluate their build against."*

### Objection 3: "Can you show me backtested performance?"
**What it means:** They want evidence that the signal has predictive content. This is a reasonable ask and a positive signal — they are thinking about how to use it, not whether to dismiss it.

**The answer:**
Show the specific validation study if you have it. If you do not yet have a published backtest:
> *"The backtest documentation is in progress — I'll have a methodology note with country-level validation by [date]. What I can show you today is the event annotation log: every significant GRI movement in the past 12 months, with the triggering event and the subsequent market outcome. It's not a formal backtest, but it lets you see the signal's track record in plain language."*

Do not promise a backtest you cannot deliver. Give them what you have.

### Objection 4: "This looks interesting but we're not set up to use another data vendor right now."
**What it means:** This is a timing objection, not a substance objection. It usually means: (a) budget is committed, or (b) the data operations team has a vendor onboarding queue.

**The answer:**
> *"Understood — what's the right timing for you? The trial is designed to be zero-friction operationally: it's a weekly CSV or a Delta Share, no API integration required. The trial itself is not a vendor relationship — it is a data sample with a 28-day window. If the signal is useful, you have 28 days of evidence before any procurement conversation starts. If it's not useful, there was no setup cost."*

### Objection 5: "Who else is using this?"
**What it means:** Social proof is missing. They want to know they are not the first institutional buyer to trust an unknown vendor with a new product.

**The answer:**
When you have customers: name them (with permission) and describe their mandate briefly.

When you do not yet have customers (at the time of your first trial):
> *"We're in the first trial cohort stage — you would be among the founding institutional users. That comes with a specific advantage: the pricing for founding users is locked in permanently, the methodology is validated through your use case before it scales, and you have direct access to me on methodology questions that later customers won't have. The risk is that we are early — the signal's live track record is being built now, which is exactly why the first trial is free."*

Being early is a risk — own it. But it is also a feature for the right buyer.

### Objection 6: "How do you handle data quality / GDELT noise issues?"
**What it means:** This is a technical objection from a sophisticated user. They know GDELT. They know it has noise. They are asking whether you have engineered around it.

**The answer:**
> *"GDELT is a firehose — you are right that the raw event feed is noisy. The GRI is not a GDELT pass-through. The signal uses GDELT as one of several inputs, with a three-step cleaning process: [brief methodology]. The validation case is the event annotation log — every GRI movement above a specific threshold has a documented trigger. If the noise were dominant, you would not see the event-to-score correlation. I'm happy to walk through the methodology notebook if you want to look under the hood."*

### Objection 7: "Send me more information."
**What it means:** This is the softest possible rejection that sounds like engagement. "Send me more information" is almost never a step toward a sale. It is a polite ending to a call they are not ready to continue.

**The answer:**
Do not just send more information.
> *"Happy to. So I send you something useful rather than generic — what's the one open question that, if I answered it, would make it clear whether the GRI is worth a trial for you?"*

Force the real objection to the surface. If they say "I don't know — just send the deck," send the deck, note them as low-engagement, and move to the next name on your list.

## III. The Follow-Up Email (Sent Within 2 Hours)

Every call ends with a follow-up email sent within 2 hours. This is not a recap — it is an activation document.

**Subject:** `GRI trial pack — [their name], [fund name]`

**Structure:**

1. One sentence noting something specific they said in the call: "You mentioned the Turkey political risk situation in Q3 — I pulled the GRI chart for Turkey from January through now; sending it separately."

2. The trial confirmation or next step: "As we discussed, here's the trial pack setup: [coverage universe they named], 28-day window starting [date], weekly GRI scores delivered by [method]. I'll send Day 0 materials tomorrow morning."

3. One specific thing you are doing for them this week that they did not ask for: "I'll also run the GRI movement analysis for Brazil for the past 90 days since you mentioned you're watching the Lula administration — I'll include it in tomorrow's materials."

4. The frictionless close: "If anything changes or you have questions on methodology before Day 0, reply directly. No other steps needed."

The follow-up does not summarize the call. It moves the next step forward.

## IV. The Call Log: What to Document After Every Conversation

After every sales call, write a 5-line log entry in `prospectra.gold.prospect_pipeline`:

1. **What they care about most:** primary geography, risk type, investment mandate element
2. **Their current process gap:** what they said they lack or do poorly
3. **The objection they raised:** even if you handled it successfully
4. **Their language for the problem:** the specific words they used — these are your best marketing copy
5. **The agreed next step and deadline:** trial start date, follow-up date, or decision date

This documentation is not bureaucracy. It is the institutional memory of your sales process. When you revisit a prospect in 90 days, you will know exactly where you left off and exactly what mattered to them.

## Investment Implications

### The Institutional Sales Process as a Framework for Evaluating B2B Data Companies

The mechanics of institutional data sales have direct implications for how to value and assess early-stage data businesses — whether you are thinking about Prospectra or evaluating a potential investment.

**The demo-to-trial conversion rate as a product-market fit signal.** In institutional data sales, a conversion rate from demo to trial above 40% is strong evidence of product-market fit. Below 20% suggests the product is either not solving a real problem or the demo is not connecting the product to the buyer's specific pain. When evaluating early-stage data companies, ask for this conversion rate — it is a more honest signal than a pipeline count.

**The objection log as a competitive intelligence map.** The seven objections above are not random — they reflect the real competitive dynamics in the institutional data market. "We build our own" (Objection 2) is the single most common reason an otherwise-qualified prospect does not buy external data. The market for systematic geopolitical signals is still small enough that most sophisticated funds do not have a dedicated geopolitical quant — which is why the objection is partially dishonest: they have the capability but not the focus.

**The social proof gap at launch.** Every institutional data business faces a chicken-and-egg problem at launch: buyers want social proof, but you cannot generate social proof without buyers. The solution is not to fake it — it is to reframe early adoption as a structural advantage for the buyer (founding pricing, direct methodology access, the signal being calibrated to their specific use case). The companies that navigate this gap successfully always have at least one reference customer who is willing to say publicly that they use the product. That first customer is worth 10x their ARR in marketing value.

**Durability of sales relationships at the PM level.** When a PM at a fund buys your data product, they own that decision personally. If they move to another fund — which happens frequently in the hedge fund industry — they are a warm referral at the new shop. The relationship follows the PM, not the fund. This creates a specific compounding dynamic in institutional data sales that is structurally different from enterprise SaaS: your reference customer network grows faster than your account count because PMs move.

## Databricks Angle

**Build: `prospectra.gold.call_log`**

The call log is not just CRM hygiene — it is a dataset. When you have 50+ calls logged, the patterns that emerge are analytically useful:

```sql
CREATE TABLE IF NOT EXISTS prospectra.gold.call_log (
  call_id STRING,
  prospect_id STRING,           -- FK to prospect_pipeline
  call_date DATE,
  call_duration_minutes INT,
  primary_geography_mentioned STRING,
  mandate_detail STRING,
  objection_type STRING,        -- 'comparison', 'internal_build', 'backtest', 'timing', 'social_proof', 'technical', 'info_request', 'none'
  objection_resolved BOOLEAN,
  outcome STRING,               -- 'trial_agreed', 'follow_up_scheduled', 'declined', 'ghosted', 'info_requested'
  trial_start_date DATE,        -- populated if outcome = 'trial_agreed'
  prospect_language STRING,     -- verbatim language they used to describe the problem
  call_notes STRING
)
USING DELTA
COMMENT 'Sales call log — every conversation with a qualified prospect, structured for pattern analysis';
```

**The analytics query that compounds over time:**

```sql
-- Which objection types correlate with trial conversion?
SELECT
  objection_type,
  COUNT(*) AS total_calls,
  SUM(CASE WHEN outcome = 'trial_agreed' THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS trials_agreed,
  ROUND(100.0 * SUM(CASE WHEN outcome = 'trial_agreed' THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) / COUNT(*), 1) AS trial_rate_pct,
  SUM(CASE WHEN objection_resolved = true THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS objections_resolved,
  ROUND(100.0 * SUM(CASE WHEN objection_resolved = true THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) / COUNT(*), 1) AS resolution_rate_pct
FROM prospectra.gold.call_log
GROUP BY objection_type
ORDER BY trial_rate_pct DESC;
```

After 20+ calls, this query tells you which objections — when resolved — most reliably convert to trials. This allows you to improve the demo by front-running the highest-impact objections before they are raised.

**The full funnel view:**

```sql
-- Full funnel: from prospect identification to trial agreement
SELECT
  pp.source,
  pp.mandate_type,
  pp.qualification_score,
  COUNT(DISTINCT pp.prospect_id) AS prospects,
  COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN pp.stage IN ('conversation','trial','converted') THEN pp.prospect_id END) AS reached_conversation,
  COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN cl.outcome = 'trial_agreed' THEN cl.prospect_id END) AS trials_agreed,
  ROUND(100.0 * COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN cl.outcome = 'trial_agreed' THEN cl.prospect_id END)
        / NULLIF(COUNT(DISTINCT pp.prospect_id), 0), 1) AS overall_conversion_pct
FROM prospectra.gold.prospect_pipeline pp
LEFT JOIN prospectra.gold.call_log cl ON pp.prospect_id = cl.prospect_id
GROUP BY pp.source, pp.mandate_type, pp.qualification_score
ORDER BY overall_conversion_pct DESC;
```

**Dataset needed:** `prospectra.gold.prospect_pipeline` (Lesson 310) feeds `call_log` via `prospect_id`. The two tables together give you the complete sales analytics stack.

## Key Concepts Covered

1. **The diagnostic-first call structure** — 30 minutes divided into context (5), diagnosis (10), demo (8), close (5), logistics (2)
2. **The three diagnostic questions** — process (how do they currently work?), coverage (which countries?), signal (what did they miss?) — and what you are listening for in each
3. **The demo as hypothesis confirmation** — show the GRI data for their specific coverage, not a generic dashboard
4. **The trial close language** — a specific, bounded ask that references their own words and eliminates perceived downside

6. **The 2-hour follow-up email** — structure, what it must contain, and what it must not

Your goal is not to pitch them. Your goal is to practice listening to the answers. -- Which countries come up most in sales conversations?
SELECT
  primary_geography_mentioned,
  COUNT(*) AS mention_count,
  SUM(CASE WHEN outcome = 'trial_agreed' THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS converted_calls

ORDER BY mention_count DESC
LIMIT 15;

This is the feedback loop between sales and product: prospects tell you what they care about, and the data tells you whether what they care about is converting.
