## 1. Scenario Planning vs. Forecasting: A Critical Distinction

Most investors — and most analysts — treat geopolitical analysis as a forecasting exercise. They ask: *what will happen?* They assign point estimates. They build models around base cases. The problem is that geopolitics is not a domain where point estimation works. The range of outcomes is too wide, and low-probability tail events are too consequential to ignore.

**Forecasting:** "Russia has a 40% chance of invading Ukraine in Q1 2022." You are predicting a single variable. You are right or wrong. This approach is essentially useless for portfolio construction — a 40% probability doesn't tell you how to size the position.

**Scenario Planning:** "Here are three plausible futures for the Russia-Ukraine situation over the next 12 months, with distinct asset price implications for each. I will size positions so that I benefit from at least two of the three, with limited downside in the third." You are not predicting. You are mapping and positioning across a distribution of futures.

This is the methodology used by:
- Shell Oil (pioneered scenario planning in the 1970s to survive the oil shock)
- The US military (war gaming goes back to the Prussian *Kriegsspiel* of the 1820s)
- The best geopolitical hedge funds (Bridgewater, Kyle Bass, George Soros)
- National intelligence agencies (the CIA's structured analytic techniques, developed after the 9/11 failure)

The key insight: **scenario planning is not about being right. It is about not being catastrophically wrong, and extracting value from the scenarios you prepared for.**

## 2. The Core Methodology: The 2×2 Scenario Matrix

The most battle-tested scenario planning tool is the **2×2 matrix**, developed by Shell's Pierre Wack in the 1970s and refined by scenario planning practitioners over 50 years.

### Step 1 — Identify the Two Driving Uncertainties

For any geopolitical situation, identify the **two variables** that are:
1. Most uncertain (you genuinely do not know which way they go)
2. Most consequential for your investment thesis

These become the axes of your matrix.

**Example: U.S.-China Tensions over Taiwan (2025–2027)**

Two driving uncertainties:
- **Axis 1 — Xi Jinping's political calculus:** Does Xi decide that the 2027 CCP Party Congress requires a demonstration of strength, or does internal economic pressure make military adventure politically toxic?
- **Axis 2 — U.S. deterrence credibility:** Does the U.S. maintain credible extended deterrence (deployments, carrier groups, explicit defense commitments), or does political dysfunction erode the credibility of the security guarantee?

This gives you a 2×2 matrix:

|  | **U.S. Deterrence: Credible** | **U.S. Deterrence: Eroded** |
|---|---|---|
| **Xi: Risk-Tolerant** | Scenario A: Confrontation managed — elevated tension, no kinetics | Scenario B: Full Taiwan Strait crisis |
| **Xi: Risk-Averse** | Scenario C: Status quo + economic competition | Scenario D: Coercive pressure without military action |

### Step 2 — Write Each Scenario as a Narrative

Each quadrant gets a full narrative: what happens, what the sequence of events looks like, what the world looks like in 24 months. Scenarios without narratives are not useful — they do not generate actionable positions.

**Scenario A — Confrontation Managed:**
Xi attempts to consolidate territorial claims through gray-zone operations (coast guard, economic coercion), but U.S. carrier group deployments and explicit Congressional red lines prevent escalation to kinetics. Taiwan remains de facto independent. Semiconductor supply chains remain intact but are diversified aggressively by U.S. allies.

**Scenario B — Taiwan Strait Crisis:**
Xi orders military blockade or limited strike. U.S. responds with sanctions and naval deployment but not direct military engagement. TSMC production disrupted for weeks to months. Global semiconductor supply shock. Taiwan dollar collapses. Regional EM equities crash. Safe-haven flows to USD, gold, US Treasuries.

**Scenario C — Status Quo + Economic Competition:**
Internal economic pressures (deflation, property market, youth unemployment) make Xi focus on domestic stabilization. Taiwan tensions simmer but do not escalate. Economic competition continues in technology, EVs, and critical minerals. A "cold economic war" without hot phase.

**Scenario D — Coercive Pressure Without Kinetics:**
U.S. deterrence erodes, but Xi judges direct military action too costly. Instead: aggressive economic coercion of Taiwan's trade partners, cyber operations on semiconductor infrastructure, diplomatic isolation campaign. Markets initially interpret this as "no war" relief, missing the slow strangulation dynamic.

### Step 3 — Assign Probability Weights

This is the hardest step, and the one most analysts skip. Do not skip it. Forcing yourself to assign probabilities does two things: (1) it reveals what you actually believe vs. what you say, and (2) it allows you to calculate expected value across scenarios.

**Example probability assignment (as of mid-2026):**
- Scenario A: 35% — elevated tension, managed
- Scenario B: 15% — full crisis
- Scenario C: 40% — status quo with economic competition
- Scenario D: 10% — coercive but non-kinetic pressure

Note: your probabilities do not need to be precise. The discipline of assigning them is the point. A 15% probability of a Taiwan Strait crisis is not a prediction — it is an explicit statement that you believe this is a real, non-trivial risk that deserves a portfolio hedge.

### Step 4 — Map Asset Price Implications per Scenario

For each scenario, run the asset class impact framework from Lesson 25.

| Asset Class | Scenario A | Scenario B | Scenario C | Scenario D |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| TSMC / Semiconductor equities | Volatile, elevated | Crash −40%+ | Recovery, upside | Slow pressure, −10%–20% |
| Gold | Elevated (risk premium) | Spike +20%+ | Drift lower | Modest bid |
| USD | Elevated | Flight-to-quality spike | Gradual decline vs. EM | Elevated |
| Taiwan Dollar | Weak | Collapse | Stable | Gradual weakening |
| Defense equities (US/Asia) | Long (structural demand) | Spike + structural | Modest | Long (structural) |
| Oil | Moderate disruption risk | Spike (if Strait closed) | Moderate | Limited impact |
| China equities | Suppressed | Crash | Recovery possible | Range-bound |

### Step 5 — Construct the Expected-Value Portfolio

Now you can do the thing that separates scenario planning from guessing: calculate expected value.

**Expected value example for TSMC:**
- Scenario A (35%): −10% (volatility suppresses multiple)
- Scenario B (15%): −45% (supply shock, geopolitical risk discount)
- Scenario C (40%): +20% (recovery + AI demand tailwinds)
- Scenario D (10%): −20% (slow structural pressure)

**E(return) = 0.35(−10) + 0.15(−45) + 0.40(+20) + 0.10(−20)**
**= −3.5 − 6.75 + 8.0 − 2.0 = −4.25%**

On an expected-value basis, an unhedged long TSMC position has negative expected return given geopolitical risk. This is information. You now need to either: (a) buy a tail hedge for Scenario B, (b) reduce position size to reflect the skew, or (c) find a position that has positive expected value across more scenarios.

## 3. Historical Case Study: Shell's Scenario Planning and the 1973 Oil Shock

The scenario planning methodology was not invented by investors. It was invented by oil company executives at Shell in the early 1970s — and the case study is the single best proof of its power.

### Background
In 1971–1972, virtually every energy company, government, and bank was forecasting continued cheap, abundant oil. The prevailing assumption was that OPEC was a loose cartel with limited coordination capacity, and that Saudi Arabia would not sacrifice oil revenues for political goals.

**Shell's planning team, led by Pierre Wack, disagreed.** They did not predict the 1973 oil embargo. They built a scenario in which OPEC successfully coordinated a supply shock. They called it "the year of the sheikh."

### What They Did
Wack's team worked backward from the scenario: *if* this happened, what would it mean for Shell's assets, supply chains, refinery locations, and capital allocation? They did not change anything in 1972. They prepared.

When OPEC embargoed oil shipments to the U.S. and Netherlands in October 1973 and the oil price quadrupled, Shell was ready. Every other major oil company — Exxon, BP, Chevron — was caught flat-footed. Shell had pre-positioned refining capacity, diversified supply contracts, and hedged currency exposures. Within two years, Shell moved from being the industry's weakest competitor to the second-most-profitable oil major in the world.

### The Lesson
Shell did not win because they predicted 1973. They won because they had already run the thought experiment and built the portfolio of responses. When the low-probability scenario arrived, they were not surprised. They were prepared.

**This is the investment analog:** the investor who has already built the scenario analysis and sized positions accordingly does not need to react quickly when the event occurs. The preparation is the edge.

## 4. The War Game Format: Running a Structured Geopolitical Exercise

Professional scenario planning often uses structured "war game" exercises — structured debates where teams argue for different geopolitical outcomes, pressure-test assumptions, and identify the "tells" that would confirm one scenario over another.

For a solo investor or small team, the war game is simplified:

### The Three-Question Exercise

For any geopolitical situation, force yourself to answer three questions:

**1. What would have to be true for the worst-case scenario to occur?**
This is the critical question. Most investors dismiss tail risks because they seem implausible. Forcing yourself to articulate the conditions under which the worst case materializes makes it concrete and credible. For Taiwan Strait crisis: what would have to be true? (Xi's domestic approval collapsing below a threshold where foreign adventure becomes necessary; U.S. distracted by domestic political crisis; miscalculation in a gray-zone incident triggering escalation.)

**2. What are the leading indicators — the "tells" that a scenario is materializing?**
Scenarios do not arrive without warning. They have precursors. For the Taiwan Strait: PLA naval exercises in the strait exceeding 2022 patterns; TSMC foreign investment acceleration (executives voting with capital); Taiwan defense budget exceeding 3% GDP; diplomatic communique language hardening.

**3. If the worst case materializes, what is the first thing I need to do in the first 24 hours?**
This is the pre-commitment. Decide now, before you are in the fog of crisis, what action you will take at what threshold. "If Taiwan Strait exercises expand to a formal blockade declaration, I will: (1) execute the Taiwan dollar short that is already sized in my book, (2) reduce semiconductor equity exposure by X%, (3) increase gold and USD exposure by Y%."

This is exactly what professional risk managers call a **pre-mortem** — working backward from the bad outcome to identify both the path and the response.

## 5. Probability Updating: Bayesian Thinking in Real Time

A scenario plan is not a document you file and forget. It is a living framework that gets updated as new information arrives. The tool for this is Bayesian thinking — formally or informally updating probability weights as evidence accumulates.

**The basic logic:**
- You start with a prior probability (your initial scenario weights)
- New information arrives (an election result, a military deployment, a diplomatic statement)
- You update your probability weights based on how much more or less likely that information makes each scenario

**Example:**
You started with 15% probability on Scenario B (Taiwan Strait crisis). Then:
- PLA announces new maritime exclusion zones around Taiwan's eastern coast (+updates Scenario B up)
- Xi Jinping makes surprise state visit to Europe for G7 meeting (−updates Scenario B down, signals diplomatic preference)
- TSMC announces $15B additional investment in Arizona fabs (+weakly updates Scenario B — executives hedging)

You don't need formal Bayesian math. You need the discipline of *explicitly asking* how each data point changes the scenario weights — and updating your positions accordingly.

**The critical discipline:** most investors update their narratives but not their positions. They say "tensions are rising" and then do nothing. The scenario framework forces you to translate the narrative update into a portfolio action.

## 6. Constructing the Geopolitical Scenario Portfolio

Given the scenario framework, how do you actually build and size a geopolitical risk portfolio?

### The Three-Layer Architecture

**Layer 1 — Core Long-Horizon Positions (60–70% of risk budget)**
These are positions that have positive expected value across *most* scenarios — not all, but most. They reflect structural secular trends that the scenario analysis consistently supports.

Example: Long European defense equities. This position benefits from Scenarios A, B, and D in the Taiwan framework above. It benefits from continued Russia-Ukraine structural rearmament regardless of Taiwan outcome. It has positive expected value across a wide range of geopolitical paths.

**Layer 2 — Scenario-Specific Hedges (20–30% of risk budget)**
These are positions that may have negative carry or modest expected returns in the base case, but provide large payoffs if a specific tail scenario materializes.

Example: Long gold and USD. Negative carry (opportunity cost) in Scenario C (status quo), but strongly positive in Scenario B (crisis). You are paying an insurance premium. The question is whether the premium is fair given your probability weight.

**Layer 3 — Signal-Triggered Opportunistic Positions (0–10% of risk budget)**
These are positions you take when leading indicators confirm that a specific scenario is beginning to materialize — not fully, but partially. These require pre-commitment (the 24-hour response plan from the war game exercise) and discipline.

Example: You have pre-committed that if TSMC foreign investment acceleration exceeds a threshold AND PLA maritime exclusion zones expand, you will open a short position on Taiwan semiconductor supply chain equities. The trigger arrives. You execute — not because you are now certain of crisis, but because the probability weight has shifted enough to change the expected value calculation.

### Position Sizing Under Scenario Uncertainty

The Kelly Criterion (from information theory) provides a principled approach to sizing:
- **Full Kelly** = (Edge/Odds) — sizes based on probability-weighted expected return
- In geopolitics, where probabilities are uncertain and outcomes are fat-tailed, use **fractional Kelly** (0.25–0.5x) to avoid ruin from probability estimation errors

The simpler rule for practitioners: **size so that if your worst-case scenario materializes, you can survive it and add to your best-positioned assets.** The investor who is not ruined by the tail scenario is the investor who can exploit it.

## 7. Why Most Institutions Fail at This

Return to the opening question: how did Western institutions *know* Russia might invade Ukraine and still fail to price it?

Three systemic failures:

**Failure 1 — Careers punish tail-risk preparation.** An analyst who recommends hedging for a 15% probability event that doesn't materialize looks wrong 85% of the time. The incentive is to stay close to consensus and be wrong with everyone. This is the **career-risk distortion** that systematically underweights tail scenarios in institutional portfolios.

**Failure 2 — "Base case" culture dominates.** Investment committees vote on base cases. Scenario B (15% probability) never makes it to the investment committee because "it's not the base case." The scenario framework is explicitly designed to fight this — it forces low-probability scenarios into the explicit, actionable analysis.

**Failure 3 — Analysts separate from portfolio managers.** Geopolitical analysts at major institutions write reports that go into a folder. Portfolio managers manage money based on models that don't incorporate geopolitical scenarios systematically. The scenario framework only works if the analyst and the portfolio manager are the same person — or if there is an explicit translation process from analysis to position.

For a small, integrated operation like Prospectra, this is actually an **advantage**. You are the analyst and the portfolio manager. There is no translation layer where geopolitical insight gets lost.

## Reflection Questions

1. Shell's scenario planning gave them an edge over Exxon and BP in 1973 — but by 1985, Exxon and BP had both adopted scenario planning methodologies. Shell's edge largely disappeared. What does this tell you about the nature of edge in scenario-based investing? Is the methodology itself the edge, or is the quality of the scenario construction the edge? How do you maintain an edge when the methodology becomes widely adopted?

2. In the Taiwan scenario matrix, we assigned 40% probability to "Status Quo + Economic Competition" (Scenario C). But notice: the portfolio positions that benefit from Scenario C (long China tech recovery, long Taiwan equities, reduced gold hedge) are essentially the *opposite* of the positions that protect against Scenario B. This is a genuine tension. How do you resolve it — do you run separate books for base case and tail risk, or do you accept that hedging Scenario B structurally reduces your Scenario C returns? What is the right framework for this decision?

3. The lesson argues that pre-commitment (the 24-hour response plan) is essential for avoiding fog-of-crisis decision errors. But pre-commitment also removes optionality — you might be wrong about which leading indicators matter, and a pre-committed response might be triggered by a false positive. How do you calibrate the specificity of a pre-commitment rule? What is the cost of being too specific vs. too vague?
