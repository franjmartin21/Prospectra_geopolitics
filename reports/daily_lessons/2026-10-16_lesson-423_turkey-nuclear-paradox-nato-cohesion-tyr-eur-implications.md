## Opening Question

Lesson 422 asked you to hold one unresolved problem: Turkey.

Saudi Arabia has two structured paths to nuclear capability. Iran crossed the threshold and paid with airstrikes. Pakistan is already a nuclear state and now a formal security partner of a Gulf monarchy. Every regional nuclear calculation runs through these three actors — and the investment implications, however large, are tractable. You can map them. You can build pipelines to monitor them. You can position for specific scenarios.

Turkey is different. Turkey is a problem that, if it moves, does not create a regional crisis. It creates a **systemic one**.

Here is the question: **Recep Tayyip Erdogan stood before the United Nations General Assembly in September 2019 and said the following, on the record, with cameras rolling: "Some countries have missiles with nuclear warheads, not one or two. But [for developing countries], we can't have them. This, I cannot accept."**

That statement was made seven years ago. Turkey at the time was a NATO ally, hosted approximately 50 US B61 nuclear gravity bombs at Incirlik Air Base, and had no known indigenous nuclear weapons program. Since then: the post-Midnight Hammer regional order has collapsed, Iran has been struck, Saudi Arabia has signed a nuclear cooperation agreement that bypasses the gold standard, and the world's nuclear architecture has been structurally disrupted.

**Has Turkey moved closer to acting on that statement — and what does it mean if it does?**

## 1. Turkey's Nuclear History: Closer Than You Think

Turkey's relationship with nuclear weapons is not theoretical. It is operational, active, and structural — and it has been so since 1961.

### NATO Nuclear Sharing: The B61s at Incirlik

Under NATO's nuclear sharing arrangement, the United States forward-deploys approximately **100 B61 nuclear gravity bombs** across five European countries: Belgium, Germany, Italy, Netherlands, and Turkey. Of these, roughly **50 are stationed at Incirlik Air Base** in southern Turkey, approximately 100 kilometers from the Syrian border and roughly 1,000 kilometers from Tehran.

This is not a Cold War artifact. The B61s at Incirlik are operational. They have been upgraded to the B61-12 variant — a precision-guided, dial-a-yield thermonuclear weapon with selectable yields from 0.3 to 50 kilotons. They are maintained by US Air Force personnel, controlled by US Permissive Action Links (electronic locks), and require a dual-key authorization — US and host nation — before they can be released.

**The structural implication:** Turkey currently hosts more US nuclear weapons than any other NATO ally, and those weapons sit on aircraft — Turkish F-16s and potentially F-35s — that Turkish pilots are certified to deliver. Turkey does not *own* nuclear weapons. But it is trained to deliver them, it hosts them on its soil, and its government has political leverage over their safety and accessibility.

During the July 2016 coup attempt against Erdogan, **Incirlik Air Base was briefly cut off from external power supply and the Turkish government suspended US air operations there**. For approximately 24 hours, the US nuclear weapons at Incirlik were inaccessible to US forces and surrounded by a situation of uncertain political control. The US military's response was not publicized. But the episode demonstrated, unambiguously, that Turkey has structural leverage over American nuclear weapons on its soil — leverage it has never formally exercised but demonstrably possesses.

### Erdogan's Nuclear Statement in Context

The September 2019 UNGA speech was not an accident. Erdogan is not a leader who speaks carelessly on sensitive matters. He was making a calculated point in a specific context: the NPT's inequity. His argument was structurally correct — the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty enshrines a permanent hierarchy between the five recognized nuclear-weapons states and everyone else, with no credible pathway to equalization.

The statement was diplomatically ignored by NATO partners. That was a mistake. Erdogan was signaling a framework: **Turkey views itself as a great power whose second-class nuclear status is a political injustice, not a permanent condition.**

In the years since, the signals have accumulated:

- **2020:** Turkey signs a nuclear cooperation agreement with Russia for the Akkuyu Nuclear Power Plant — a four-reactor, 4,800 MW facility being built by Rosatom on Turkey's Mediterranean coast. This gives Russia physical infrastructure inside Turkey and gives Turkey civilian nuclear experience and dual-use knowledge. Akkuyu Unit 1 is expected to begin commercial operations in 2026.

- **2022:** Turkey declines to join Western sanctions on Russia after the Ukraine invasion — and increases energy cooperation with Moscow, including nuclear.

- **2023:** Turkey acquires S-400 air defense systems from Russia, triggering US removal of Turkey from the F-35 program. This is directly relevant to nuclear delivery: without the F-35, Turkey's nuclear delivery capability for NATO weapons depends on aging F-16s. But it also forces Turkey to develop or acquire alternative delivery systems — which could include systems not integrated with NATO.

- **2025:** Post-Midnight Hammer, Turkey condemns the Israeli strikes on Iranian nuclear facilities in the strongest terms of any NATO member, calls for an emergency UN Security Council meeting, and publicly describes the strikes as "an act of state terrorism that destabilizes the entire region." Turkey simultaneously accelerates Akkuyu construction and quietly expands its uranium exploration activities in central Anatolia.

- **2026 (ongoing):** Turkey has not signed a 123 agreement with the United States — it has not sought one. Instead, Rosatom will provide nuclear fuel for Akkuyu under a Russian state contract. Turkey is building civilian nuclear expertise and fuel-cycle familiarity entirely outside the US-controlled nonproliferation architecture.

**The pattern is one of deliberate structural separation from US nuclear influence — while maintaining the formal NATO membership that gives Turkey the political cover to avoid US pressure.**

## 2. The NATO Nuclear Sharing Dilemma: Turkey as Leverage

Turkey is simultaneously the problem and the leverage.

As a NATO member, Turkey participates in the Nuclear Planning Group — the alliance body that develops nuclear doctrine and strategy. Turkey has formal input into NATO's nuclear posture. It sits at the table where nuclear use guidelines are discussed, where alert levels are set, and where extended deterrence commitments are made.

**This creates an extraordinary asymmetry.** If Turkey were to withdraw from NATO's nuclear sharing arrangement — or worse, to acquire indigenous nuclear weapons — it would not simply be leaving a security alliance. It would be:

1. **Removing 50 US nuclear weapons from a strategically critical forward position**, potentially requiring their relocation under politically sensitive conditions
2. **Breaking NATO's nuclear consensus**, which operates on unanimity — a Turkey that has gone nuclear independently is a Turkey that can veto any NATO nuclear decision
3. **Demonstrating that the NPT is unenforceable against a state that is simultaneously a NATO member**, which would accelerate every other latent nuclear program globally
4. **Activating Article 5 ambiguity** — if Turkey is attacked *because* it acquired nuclear weapons, are other NATO members obligated to defend it?

The Cold War logic of nuclear sharing was partly deterrence and partly **nuclear coupling** — by placing nuclear weapons in allied territory and training allied pilots to deliver them, the US created co-ownership of nuclear risk that bound allies to the American strategic position. Turkey's nuclear sharing participation was designed to prevent exactly the independent deterrent ambition that Erdogan expressed in 2019.

**That logic is breaking down.** The coupling mechanism works when allies believe American extended deterrence is credible. Turkey watched the US fail to prevent Israel from striking Iran — a NATO-adjacent military operation against a neighboring country. It watched the US conduct the Qatar episode. It received the message that American security guarantees have conditions, limits, and political override clauses that Washington controls unilaterally.

## 3. Turkey's Two Paths — An Echo of Saudi Arabia

Just as Saudi Arabia has two paths to nuclear capability, Turkey has a structural mirror image:

### Path A: The Russian/Chinese Nuclear Partnership

Turkey is building Akkuyu with Rosatom. A second proposed nuclear plant (Sinop, on the Black Sea coast) was initially awarded to a Japanese-French consortium but has stalled. Turkey has approached Chinese companies about Sinop. If Sinop goes to Chinese state nuclear enterprises, Turkey will have nuclear infrastructure from both Russia and China — outside any US or Western oversight framework.

**Akkuyu itself is dual-use capable at the margins.** The reactors are light water reactors that produce spent fuel with plutonium. Under current IAEA safeguards, Turkey cannot reprocess that plutonium. But the reprocessing knowledge gap — the gap between "has operating nuclear reactors" and "can separate weapons-grade material" — is much smaller than the gap between "no nuclear program" and "operating nuclear reactors."

The Rosatom relationship also gives Turkey access to Russian nuclear weapons design knowledge informally, through the dense web of Rosatom engineers and staff who will operate Akkuyu for its initial decades under a "build-own-operate" contract. This is not a weapons transfer. But it is a technology environment.

### Path B: Breakout from NATO Nuclear Sharing Knowledge

Turkey's air force personnel at Incirlik have, for decades, been trained to handle, maintain, and deliver US nuclear weapons. This is not weapons design knowledge — the weapons themselves are black boxes with US-controlled locks. But **weapons handling, delivery aircraft operation, and strike planning** are transferable skills. A Turkish indigenous weapons program would benefit from this operational experience even without direct technical access to US weapon designs.

The critical missing piece for Turkey is **fissile material** — highly enriched uranium or plutonium. Turkey has no indigenous uranium enrichment capability and no reprocessing facility. This is the binding constraint. Without fissile material, nuclear weapons are not achievable regardless of design knowledge.

**But Turkey has a path to fissile material that no other regional proliferant has:**

Turkey could withdraw from the NPT (90 days notice, as North Korea did in 2003), expel IAEA inspectors, and begin enriching uranium in facilities whose existence has not yet been publicly confirmed — or use Akkuyu's spent fuel if it acquires reprocessing technology.

The NPT withdrawal scenario is the extreme case. Short of that, Turkey could pursue a **threshold strategy**: develop enrichment capacity, stop short of weapons-grade, and use the latent capability as leverage against both NATO and regional adversaries. This is the Iranian model — and it worked for over two decades before the military response.

## 4. The Investment Implications: TRY, EUR, European Defense, and NATO Cohesion

This is where the Turkey nuclear question becomes directly investable — not because Turkish weaponization is imminent, but because **the risk premium for NATO cohesion collapse is priced at near zero and the fundamentals suggest it should not be.**

### Turkish Lira (TRY)

Turkey's nuclear ambitions are inseparable from its broader geopolitical positioning, which has persistently damaged TRY. The currency has lost more than 80% of its value against the dollar over the past decade — driven by monetary policy dysfunction, current account deficits, and political risk premium.

The nuclear dimension adds a specific new layer:

- **Sanctions risk:** Any confirmed Turkish move toward indigenous nuclear capability triggers the CAATSA (Countering America's Adversaries Through Sanctions Act) framework. Turkey is already in tension with CAATSA over the S-400 purchase. A nuclear signal would activate full sanctions — worse than anything Turkey has experienced.
- **IMF access restriction:** A sanctioned nuclear-threshold Turkey cannot easily access IMF emergency liquidity. Turkey's external financing needs are structural and large.
- **FDI collapse:** Western FDI into Turkey — significant in manufacturing and tourism — would freeze under sanctions conditions.

**TRY view:** Already a structurally challenged currency. Nuclear signaling is a tail risk that adds a potential step-down of 20–35% in a sanctions scenario. Not a position for ordinary risk tolerance; worth holding in mind as a macro hedge if Turkish nuclear signals intensify.

### Euro (EUR)

The EUR impact of Turkish nuclear acquisition would be indirect but powerful — running through three channels:

**1. NATO fragmentation premium:** A Turkey that has gone nuclear outside NATO is a Turkey that either stays in NATO and destroys its decision-making, or leaves NATO and creates a southeastern flank vacuum. Either outcome is bad for European security architectures. Markets price European security uncertainty into EUR — the 2022 Ukraine invasion demonstrated this with the EUR/USD move from 1.13 to parity. A NATO cohesion crisis centered on Turkey would be larger.

**2. Refugee and migration pressure:** Turkey is the primary transit and origin point for Middle Eastern migration to Europe. Under conditions of regional instability associated with Turkish nuclear signaling, migration pressure increases — and European political fragmentation increases with it, which is persistently negative for EUR.

**3. German and French defense spending acceleration:** Already elevated post-Ukraine, European defense budgets would surge further under a scenario where NATO's southeastern flank is in question. Defense spending is economically positive for industrial output but fiscally negative — it requires either debt issuance or tax increases, both of which are growth-negative at the margin.

**EUR view:** Turkey nuclear risk is an underpriced EUR tail risk. Not a position today, but a scenario worth stress-testing in any EUR-denominated portfolio with a 3–7 year horizon.

### European Defense Equities

The paradox: Turkish nuclear ambition is fundamentally positive for European defense company valuations, for the same reason that any NATO threat is. If Turkey defects from NATO's nuclear architecture, the remaining NATO members face a more exposed threat environment and accelerate rearmament.

**Beneficiaries in a Turkish nuclear signaling scenario:**
- **Rheinmetall (RHM GY):** Germany's primary defense industrial champion; ground systems, ammunition, future combat vehicles
- **Airbus Defence and Space (AIR FP):** Eurofighter Typhoon, A400M, MRTT — the European air defense backbone
- **Leonardo (LDO IM):** Italian defense, radar systems, electronic warfare
- **Thales (HO FP):** Missiles (with MBDA), sensors, electronic warfare
- **BAE Systems (BA/ LN):** UK-based but the largest European defense company by revenue; Typhoon, destroyer programs

**The NATO nuclear architecture crisis scenario is an accelerant on top of an already elevated European defense spending trend.** The base case for European defense is already positive; Turkish nuclear ambition is a tail-risk accelerant.

### Turkish Sovereign Debt

Turkey's sovereign bonds (both local TRY-denominated and Eurobonds in USD/EUR) face a specific risk from nuclear signaling: **the sanctions-driven freeze scenario**. CAATSA sanctions applied to Turkey would restrict access to dollar funding markets, force roll-risk on near-term Eurobond maturities, and create spread widening events.

Turkey's Eurobond spreads are already wide (historically 300–500 basis points over UST). A nuclear signal scenario would widen them by a further 150–300 basis points at minimum — comparable to the spread widening Turkey experienced during the 2018 currency crisis.

**Do not hold duration in Turkish Eurobonds without explicit scenario pricing for nuclear signal risk.**

## 5. What Has Actually Changed Since 2019 — A Status Assessment

Erdogan's 2019 speech was a signal. Seven years later, where does Turkey actually stand?

| Dimension | 2019 Status | 2026 Status | Direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stated nuclear ambition | Publicly declared (UNGA) | Maintained; not retracted | Unchanged — ambition is structural |
| Civilian nuclear program | Akkuyu in construction | Akkuyu Unit 1 entering commercial operation | Progressing — dual-use knowledge accumulating |
| Nuclear delivery capability | F-16 certified for B61 delivery under NATO sharing | F-16 certification maintained; removed from F-35 program (2020) | Weakened on Western platform; not replaced |
| Fissile material capability | None; no enrichment or reprocessing | None confirmed; uranium exploration activities underway | Zero to early-stage indigenous exploration |
| NATO nuclear sharing participation | Full participant | Full participant | Unchanged formally |
| NPT status | Signatory in good standing | Signatory in good standing | Unchanged formally |
| Russia nuclear cooperation | None | Rosatom operating Akkuyu; fuel supply contract signed | Deepening |
| Geopolitical incentive for nuclear | Moderate (Iran threat; Kurdish autonomy pressures) | Elevated (post-Midnight Hammer; Saudi path visible; Iran unresolved) | Increasing |

**Assessment:** Turkey is not on a weapons timeline. It is on a **leverage accumulation** timeline. Every step increases Turkey's ability to make a credible nuclear threat without taking the catastrophic political step of formal weaponization. This is a rational strategy for a middle power that wants great-power treatment.

The investment implication: **you do not need to price in Turkish nuclear weapons. You need to price in sustained, escalating Turkish nuclear ambiguity as a structural feature of European security.** That ambiguity is worth a premium in European defense stocks, a discount in Turkish sovereign assets, and a floor under EUR volatility.

## 6. The NATO Cohesion Paradox — A Framework for Understanding All Future NATO Events

Turkey's nuclear question is not isolated. It is the most extreme expression of a structural problem that will dominate NATO analysis for the next decade: **how does an alliance hold together when its members have divergent threat assessments, divergent strategic interests, and divergent tolerance for American leadership?**

The framework has three variables:

**1. Threat consensus:** The alliance works when members agree on the primary threat. During the Cold War, consensus was Soviet. Today: Russia (for eastern members), China (for US), Turkey/migration/Islam (for some southern members), domestic politics (for Hungary/Slovakia). No shared threat → no coherent strategy.

**2. American leadership credibility:** Extended deterrence requires allies to believe the US will actually fight for them. The Qatar episode damaged this. The failure to prevent Midnight Hammer damaged this. Every US action that suggests conditions on commitment weakens the alliance's logical foundation.

**3. Member cost-benefit calculus:** Each member continuously evaluates whether NATO membership provides more security than it costs in sovereignty constraints. Turkey's nuclear ambition is the clearest expression of a negative calculus: "NATO's nuclear umbrella does not give us the deterrent we need, and its constraints prevent us from getting one ourselves."

**When all three variables move in the same negative direction simultaneously** — as they are now — the alliance faces structural fragmentation risk that markets systematically underprice because NATO has never actually collapsed and therefore the base rate is zero.

**The base rate for NATO collapse is zero. But base rates are useless for structural change events that have no historical precedent.** Use the framework, not the base rate.
