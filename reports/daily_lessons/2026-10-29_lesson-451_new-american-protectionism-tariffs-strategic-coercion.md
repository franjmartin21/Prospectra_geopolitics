## Opening Framing Question

*Here is a puzzle for you before we begin:*

If tariffs are economically inefficient — they raise prices for consumers, reduce trade volumes, and invite retaliation — why do great powers keep using them? Why would a country absorb $900 per household in annual costs, legal rulings against its tariff authority, and diplomatic friction with allies, and then *redouble* the policy?

The naive answer is that politicians are economically illiterate. The strategic answer is more interesting: **they may be doing it on purpose, and for reasons that have nothing to do with economics.**

That's the lens we're going to build today.

## Core Concept: Tariffs Are Not Trade Policy — They Are Power Policy

### The Economics vs. The Strategy

Economic theory since Ricardo (1817) has been unambiguous: free trade raises aggregate welfare. Comparative advantage means every country benefits from specialization. Tariffs distort this — they protect inefficient domestic producers at the expense of consumers and export competitiveness.

And yet: tariffs have been central to every rising and defending power since the industrial age.

The United States itself industrialized *behind* tariff walls — average rates of 40–50% throughout the late 19th century. Alexander Hamilton's *Report on Manufactures* (1791) explicitly called for infant industry protection. Friedrich List, the German economist, argued in 1841 that free trade is the policy of the strong when they want to prevent the weak from catching up.

This is the core insight: **free trade is the policy choice of the hegemon. Tariffs are the policy choice of challengers and defenders.**

When the US adopted high tariffs in 2025–2026, it was not a beginner's mistake. It was a structural shift in how America defines its national interest — from the post-WWII Bretton Woods order (where US openness underwrote alliance loyalty) to a mercantilist logic where trade is *leverage*.

### The Three Functions of a Modern Tariff

**1. Revenue (the fiscal function)**
At a 7.2% average effective rate on $3+ trillion in imports, tariffs generate real fiscal revenue — roughly $200–250 billion annually. In a fiscal dominance regime (which we've been studying since Lesson 427), this matters. Tariffs are a politically easier revenue source than income tax hikes. They tax foreigners and import-consuming Americans, and the political cost falls disproportionately on cosmopolitan consumers rather than manufacturing-district voters.

**2. Industrial Policy (the structural function)**
Tariffs on semiconductors, EVs, steel, aluminum, and strategic materials are attempts to shift supply chains back to the US. The Inflation Reduction Act and CHIPS Act used subsidies; tariffs add the stick. The goal is to create domestic industry by making imports uncompetitive, then scale that industry until it can survive without protection. Whether it works is empirically contested. That it's being attempted at scale is not.

**3. Coercion (the strategic function)**
This is the most underappreciated function. Tariffs are *negotiating leverage*. The Trump administration from 2025 onward used tariff threats as a primary diplomatic tool: with Canada and Mexico over immigration and fentanyl, with the EU over digital services taxes, with China over technology transfer, with Japan and Korea over burden-sharing. The tariff itself may never be the end state — it's a threat that can be suspended, waived, or weaponized depending on the target's behavior. This is tariff as coercion architecture, not trade policy.

## Historical Grounding: The Three Modern Eras of US Tariff Policy

### Era 1: The Liberal Order (1945–2016)
Post-WWII, the US used trade openness as a tool of alliance management. The GATT system, then the WTO, lowered global tariffs systematically. The US accepted trade deficits in exchange for allies hosting US bases, accepting the dollar as reserve currency, and maintaining the Western coalition against the Soviet Union. Openness was *strategic generosity* — you get access to our market; we get geopolitical loyalty.

**Key data:** US average effective tariff rate in 2000: ~1.6%.

### Era 2: The Disruption (2016–2021)
Trump 1.0 broke the consensus. Section 232 tariffs on steel/aluminum (national security authority), Section 301 tariffs on China (~$370bn of goods), USMCA renegotiation. The political logic: the costs of openness had been concentrated in manufacturing communities (the Rust Belt) while the benefits diffused broadly. This created an asymmetric political economy where the losers from trade were more mobilized than the winners.

**Key data:** US average effective tariff rate peaked at ~3.4% in 2019 under Trump 1.0.

### Era 3: Structural Protectionism (2025–present)
The Biden administration *kept* most Trump tariffs and added more (EVs, solar, semiconductors). Trump 2.0 from 2025 escalated sharply — proposing universal baseline tariffs of 10–20%, 60%+ on China, and sector-specific rates of 25–100%. After IEEPA tariff authority was partially struck down by the Supreme Court in 2026, the administration shifted to Section 232, Section 301, and new legislation to preserve as much of the architecture as possible.

**Key data (2026):** Average effective tariff rate at ~7.2%, up from 2.3% in January 2025. ~54% of US goods imports covered. Household cost: ~$900/year.

The ideological consensus has collapsed: there is now no major US political faction arguing for a return to the 1945–2016 liberal trade model.

## Concrete Example: The China EV Tariff Arc

In September 2024, the Biden administration raised tariffs on Chinese electric vehicles to 100%, on solar cells to 50%, and on certain semiconductors to 50%. The explicit rationale: Chinese state subsidies had made competition impossible on cost alone.

This was not a trade dispute. It was a **structural technology war fought through trade instruments.** The US argument: China's EV industry (BYD, SAIC, CATL) was scaling on the back of $230 billion+ in state subsidies. Allowing these vehicles unrestricted access to the US market would destroy a domestic EV industry the US had just spent $370 billion in IRA subsidies to build.

**The strategic logic:**
- Protect the IRA investment from being rendered worthless by subsidized Chinese competition
- Force Chinese automakers to build in the US (Mexico routing risk: USMCA contained provisions to prevent tariff circumvention through Mexico)
- Use tariff threat to extract technology transfer conditions or IP concessions

**The investment implications played out as follows:**
- **Winners:** US EV manufacturers (TSLA, GM EV division), US battery material suppliers, domestic solar manufacturers
- **Losers:** Chinese automakers with export ambitions, US consumers buying EVs (higher prices), US auto dealers
- **Complex:** European automakers building in China (BMW, VW) faced secondary exposure

## Investment Implications

### The Tariff Regime as a Structural Feature

The most important investment insight from this era: **tariffs are no longer episodic events to trade around; they are a structural feature of the investment landscape.** The question is not "will tariffs go away?" but "how do I position for a persistent high-tariff world?"

### Directional Views by Asset Class

**1. US Industrials / Reshoring Beneficiaries — LONG (6–18 month horizon)**
Companies benefiting from supply chain repatriation: US steel producers (Nucor, Steel Dynamics), defense manufacturers, domestic semiconductor fabs (Intel, Micron for domestic CHIPS facilities), US chemical producers. The tariff wall makes their products structurally more competitive. Risk: productivity gap vs. Asian producers is real; these companies remain less efficient in many cases.

**2. Emerging Market Exporters — Cautious, Selective**
Countries in the "tariff crossfire" (China, Mexico for some sectors) face headwinds. But the story is nuanced: Vietnam, India, and Mexico (for non-tariffed goods) are benefiting from supply chain diversion. The framework is not "EM bad" — it is "which EM wins the supply chain diversion?"

**3. US Consumer Discretionary — Structural Headwind**
A $900/household annual tax on imports is not trivial. Lower-income households spend a higher fraction of income on tariffed goods. The inflation pass-through is real. This is a structural drag on discretionary consumption, compounding the fiscal dominance / high interest rate environment.

**4. Commodities — Mixed but Important**
Section 232 tariffs on steel and aluminum created a domestic price premium — US hot-rolled coil steel traded at a significant premium to global prices. Raw material inputs to tariffed sectors (iron ore, metallurgical coal, alumina) have complex second-order effects depending on sourcing geography.

**5. USD — Ambiguous**
Tariff theory predicts dollar appreciation (tariffs reduce import demand, trade deficit narrows, USD strengthens). Empirically, this is complicated: retaliatory tariffs, capital flow shifts, and confidence effects can offset. The 2025–2026 experience showed limited tariff-driven dollar appreciation, partly because the fiscal deterioration we covered in Lessons 427–440 was a larger countervailing force.

### The CEO Portfolio Note

In a persistent protectionist regime, the analytical framework must add a *tariff exposure layer* to every position. Before buying any company with significant international supply chains or export revenues, ask: (1) How exposed is their cost base to tariffed inputs? (2) How exposed are their revenues to retaliatory tariff risk in foreign markets? (3) Are they a beneficiary (domestic producer) or a victim (import-dependent, export-dependent)?

Companies that pass all three screens cleanly are scarce — and that scarcity premium is real.

## Databricks Angle

**Dataset:** USITC (US International Trade Commission) tariff schedule and trade data, combined with HS code-level import/export flows from Census Bureau. FRED has US effective tariff rate history.

**Pipeline Idea — Tariff Exposure Scoring Engine:**
1. Ingest USITC tariff schedule and HS code tariff rates
2. Map public company filings (10-K supplier disclosures, geographic revenue segments) to HS codes
3. Build a "tariff exposure score" per company: weighted average tariff rate on inputs vs. revenue exposure to retaliating markets
4. Track this score over time as tariff schedules change — companies with rising exposure scores are at structural margin risk

**Feature Engineering:** A company's tariff exposure delta (change in exposure score quarter-over-quarter) could be a leading indicator for margin compression or expansion — testable against earnings surprise data.

This is a genuinely useful pipeline that no commercial data provider does well at scale.
