## Opening Question

> *You are the finance minister of Chile. Beneath your country's Atacama Desert sits 35% of the world's known lithium reserves. The global EV transition has just made that lithium worth ten times more than it was a decade ago. Foreign mining companies — mostly Australian, American, and Chinese — are extracting it under contracts signed when lithium was worth almost nothing. What do you do?*

Hold that question. The answer to it is being played out right now across every mineral-rich country on earth. And the way states answer it will determine a significant portion of the investable commodity universe for the next two decades.

## 1. What Resource Nationalism Actually Is

Resource nationalism is not simply nationalization. That's the blunt, 1970s version. Modern resource nationalism is a toolkit of policy instruments that governments use to capture a larger share of economic rents from extracting natural resources — while keeping enough foreign capital and expertise in the game to maintain production.

**The toolkit, from soft to hard:**

| Instrument | Example | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Progressive royalties | Chile lithium royalty: 8–26% | Captures upside during price surges |
| Export restrictions | Indonesia cobalt/nickel/rare earths ban | Forces domestic processing; creates scarcity |
| State equity mandates | Chile National Lithium Company majority stake | Redirects future cash flows to state |
| Domestic content requirements | DRC insisting on in-country refining | Captures value-add, not just raw material |
| Export quotas | DRC cobalt 96,600 tonne annual cap | Controls global supply; manages price floor |
| Outright nationalization | Bolivia's 2023 lithium nationalization | Full state ownership; foreign capital exits |

The trend is toward the **middle of the toolkit** — not outright nationalization (which scares all capital away) but progressive rent capture mechanisms that say "you can mine here, but the terms have changed."

**Why now?** Three structural forces converged simultaneously:

1. **The Energy Transition demand shock** — EV batteries require lithium, cobalt, copper, nickel, manganese, and rare earths in quantities the world has never needed before. BP's Energy Outlook projects a 4–6x increase in critical mineral demand by 2040. Countries sitting on these reserves suddenly have leverage they didn't have in 2010.

2. **Deglobalization weakens the WTO discipline** — In the post-Cold War globalization era, resource nationalism was constrained by WTO rules, bilateral investment treaties (BITs), and the threat of capital flight. As that framework erodes (see Lesson 48), the political cost of tearing up contracts and rewriting terms has fallen dramatically.

3. **The China precedent** — China's state-directed industrial policy in rare earths (it controls ~60% of global processing capacity despite not holding the majority of reserves) demonstrated what resource control can do politically and economically. Every mineral-rich middle-income country took note.

## 2. The Historical Baseline: Three Waves of Resource Nationalism

This is not new. Understanding the prior waves tells you how the current one ends.

**Wave 1 — The Great Nationalizations (1950s–1970s)**
The post-colonial wave. Iran nationalized Anglo-Persian Oil (1951). Venezuela seized American oil assets (1970s). OPEC was formed (1973) as collective resource nationalism by oil producers. Saudi Aramco became fully state-owned by 1980.

*How it ended:* In most cases, production collapsed or stagnated because the nationalizing states lacked technical expertise. Eventually they opened up again — with better terms for themselves, but better terms for capital too. The lesson was: full nationalization kills the golden goose.

**Wave 2 — The Latin American Left Turn (2000s)**
Venezuela under Chávez forced Exxon, ConocoPhillips, and others to accept joint ventures giving PDVSA majority stakes. Bolivia nationalized gas infrastructure. Ecuador renegotiated contracts. Argentina re-nationalized YPF.

*How it ended:* Venezuela's oil production collapsed from 3.3 million b/d in 1998 to under 700,000 b/d by 2020. Ecuador's renegotiations scared off investment. Bolivia got better deals but grew dependent on Brazil's demand. The pattern: political wins in the short term, production declines in the medium term, capital famine in the long term.

**Wave 3 — The Critical Minerals Nationalism (2020s–present)**
This wave is structurally different from the first two in one crucial respect: the countries applying it have learned from the previous waves. They are *not* nationalizing outright. They are raising royalties, mandating state equity positions, restricting exports of unprocessed ore, and requiring domestic refining — all while keeping foreign operators in place to maintain production volumes.

This is more sophisticated and harder to counter. And unlike oil nationalism (which created OPEC as a coordination mechanism), the critical minerals wave has *no equivalent to OPEC* — yet.

## 3. The Current Wave: Five Case Studies

### Case 1 — DRC: Cobalt as a Choke Point
The Democratic Republic of Congo produces roughly **73% of the world's cobalt**. In February 2025, it suspended cobalt exports entirely — sending spot prices spiking. By September 2025, it replaced the outright ban with a quota system: 96,600 tonnes per year for 2026–2027.

The message was not subtle. Cobalt prices surged to ~$25/lb in 2026, up 50%+ from 2025 averages. The DRC government demonstrated that it *can* move global prices unilaterally. It now has leverage in renegotiating foreign mining contracts that didn't exist before.

**Investment implication:** DRC cobalt supply is a structural risk premium that must be priced into battery manufacturers (Tesla, BYD, Panasonic supply chains), recycling economics (second-life cobalt becomes more attractive), and cobalt-free battery chemistry development (LFP batteries).

### Case 2 — Chile: The Lithium Renegotiation
Chile holds 35% of global lithium reserves. In 2024, the Boric government implemented:
- Progressive royalty rates of **8–26%** on large-scale mining operations
- A requirement that the state-owned *National Lithium Company* hold a **controlling stake** in all new lithium projects

SQM (NYSE: SQM) and Albemarle (NYSE: ALB) — the two dominant foreign operators in the Atacama — now operate under materially different economics than their original contracts envisioned. SQM's share price fell ~40% from peak before stabilizing as markets priced in the new regime.

**Investment implication:** "Royalty creep" is the correct mental model for Chilean lithium. Project economics for new investment are less attractive; existing producers have partially grandfathered terms but face renegotiation risk at every contract renewal.

### Case 3 — Indonesia: The Processing Mandate
Indonesia's nickel nationalism is the most successful case study in the toolkit. In 2020, it banned exports of unprocessed nickel ore. The result: **$33 billion in downstream nickel investment** poured into Indonesia in three years, building smelters, processing facilities, and battery supply chain capacity. Indonesia turned from a raw material exporter into a nickel products exporter — a fundamental shift in value capture.

In November 2024, Indonesia extended this logic to 12 more minerals (cobalt, copper, coal, bauxite, silicon), and in 2025, raised nickel royalties from 10% to a floating 14–19% tied to market prices.

**Investment implication:** The Indonesia model is being copied aggressively. Any country with significant raw mineral reserves and ambitions to climb the value chain is studying Jakarta. The downstream processing sector — battery component manufacturers, smelters, refiners — increasingly must locate in resource-holding countries to access raw material supply. This reshapes the geography of the battery supply chain fundamentally.

### Case 4 — Kazakhstan: Uranium Progressive Taxation
Kazakhstan is the world's largest uranium producer (~45% of global supply). In 2025, it raised uranium mining base tax rates from 6% to 9%. From 2026 forward, a dual progressive tax system tied rates to both production volume and market price — the higher uranium goes, the more the state captures.

Kazatomprom (the state producer) retains strategic control while foreign joint venture partners (Orano, China CGN, and others) face improved state revenue capture.

**Investment implication:** Uranium supply economics are under quiet but sustained pressure. Kazatomprom's JV partners face rising cost structures at the exact moment nuclear energy demand (driven by AI data center power needs and energy security concerns) is creating a multi-year bull case for uranium prices. Net: uranium supply cost inflation may lag uranium price appreciation — still constructive for the thesis, but margin dynamics at JV level need monitoring.

### Case 5 — China: Rare Earths as Weapon, Not Just Commerce
China's approach to rare earths is the most strategically intentional of any case. In April 2025, China restricted U.S. exports of seven rare earth elements: scandium, yttrium, samarium, gadolinium, terbium, dysprosium, and lutetium. These are not obscure materials — they are essential for F-35 jet engine magnets, wind turbine generators, EV motors, and missile guidance systems.

China doesn't hold a majority of rare earth reserves (that distinction goes to Brazil and Vietnam). But it controls ~60% of global *processing* capacity — the refineries and separation facilities that turn rare earth ore into usable materials. That processing monopoly is the chokepoint.

**Investment implication:** This is explicitly geopolitical weaponization, not commercial resource nationalism. The lesson is that supply chain exposure to Chinese processing creates tail risk that is non-economic in origin — it cannot be hedged by price. The response (US, EU, Australian investment in domestic processing capacity) will take 5–10 years to bear fruit. Until then, the restriction represents a structural supply vulnerability in defense electronics, renewables, and EVs.

## 4. The Investment Framework: Navigating Resource Nationalism

Resource nationalism creates a systematic investment framework across multiple asset classes.

### Framework: The Resource Nationalism Risk Matrix

```
              LOW STATE CAPACITY          HIGH STATE CAPACITY
              (can't run it alone)        (can manage themselves)

HIGH          PARTIAL RISK               FULL RISK
RESERVES      (renegotiation likely,      (nationalization viable,
              operator still needed)      capital flight possible)
              → Chile lithium             → Gulf state oil
              → DRC cobalt               → Kazakhstan uranium

LOW           LOW RISK                   MODERATE RISK
RESERVES      (not worth the fight)      (export bans, taxes,
                                         processing mandates)
              → Minor EM mines           → Indonesia nickel
                                         → Zimbabwe lithium
```

**Investing through the matrix:**

1. **Long processing geography diversity** — Whoever builds the refining capacity *outside* China and politically-risky jurisdictions earns a structural premium. Australia's rare earth processing buildout (Lynas Corp), US lithium refining investments, and EU critical minerals partnerships are the clearest expression of this.

2. **Long recycling and circular supply chains** — Every ton of cobalt, lithium, or rare earths recovered from battery recycling is a ton that does not require navigating DRC, Chilean, or Indonesian resource nationalism risk. Companies building battery recycling infrastructure (Li-Cycle, Redwood Materials, Umicore) are implicitly hedging resource nationalism risk for their customers.

3. **Short mid-tier foreign miners in high-nationalism jurisdictions** — Companies with concentrated exposure to single high-nationalism countries (a copper miner with 80% of reserves in Bolivia, a cobalt company entirely DRC-exposed) carry political risk that is difficult to model and tends to be mispriced in the upside phase of a commodity cycle.

4. **Watch sovereign spread widening as early warning** — Before a government formally announces a mining renegotiation, bond markets often price in political risk through widening credit spreads. A widening spread in a mineral-rich country in a politically turbulent period is sometimes a leading indicator of resource nationalism escalation.

5. **Long commodity prices where nationalism is creating supply constraints** — The DRC cobalt quota, Kazakhstan uranium tax escalation, and Indonesian nickel export bans all create supply-side pressure that is *policy-driven* rather than geology-driven. Policy can be reversed, but reversals are slow. In the interim, supply constraint + energy transition demand = sustained price pressure. The investable expression is long commodity futures or royalty companies with low jurisdiction concentration.

## 5. The Investor's Structural Question

Here is the core paradox that makes resource nationalism a durable investment theme rather than a one-time adjustment:

**The energy transition requires massive increases in critical mineral supply. But the policies that generate the most political support for mineral-rich developing countries (resource nationalism) tend to constrain supply or raise costs. These forces work against each other simultaneously.**

The net result is not that either force wins cleanly. The result is persistent price volatility and supply uncertainty across the critical minerals complex — which creates mispricing opportunities on both sides. The investor who has a systematic framework for mapping resource nationalism risk by country and commodity is operating with information that spot commodity prices don't fully capture.

## Key Concepts

- **Resource nationalism toolkit**: royalties, export bans, state equity mandates, processing requirements, nationalization (hard end)
- **Three historical waves**: post-colonial nationalizations, Latin American left turn, critical minerals nationalism
- **Processing monopoly vs. reserve monopoly**: China's rare earth power comes from processing control, not reserve ownership — a crucial distinction
- **The Indonesia model**: export ban → force downstream investment → become a value-added exporter (being emulated globally)
- **Supply-transition paradox**: energy transition increases demand for minerals at the same time nationalism increases supply constraints

## Market Connection

The current wave of resource nationalism is **already affecting commodity prices and mining equity valuations**. Cobalt's 50%+ price move in 2026 is a live example. Lithium companies with Chilean exposure trade at a persistent valuation discount to peers with Australian/Canadian reserves. These are not temporary distortions — they reflect a structural repricing of jurisdiction risk that will compound over the energy transition decade.

**The investor who builds a systematic country-by-country, commodity-by-commodity resource nationalism risk model has an edge over consensus mining sector analysis**, which typically focuses on geology and production costs but underweights the political rent-capture dynamic.
