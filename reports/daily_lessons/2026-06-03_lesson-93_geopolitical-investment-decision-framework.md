## Opening Question

February 24, 2022. Russia invades Ukraine at 5:00 AM Moscow time.

By market open, European equity markets are down 4–5%. Brent crude is up 8%. European natural gas (TTF) is up 40% intraday before circuit breakers trigger. European energy equities — Equinor, TotalEnergies, BP, Shell — are selling off with the broad market despite having just received the most powerful near-term earnings catalyst in a decade.

Here is the question: **Do you buy European energy equities on the invasion news — or do you sell?**

If you're operating purely on valuation, you buy. They were cheap before the invasion; now they're cheaper; their underlying commodity just surged. The earnings math has just dramatically improved.

If you're operating purely on geopolitics, you're paralyzed. You don't know whether this is a 3-day war or a 3-year war. You don't know whether sanctions will hit energy exports. You don't know whether the conflict will escalate into NATO. You don't know anything with enough certainty to act.

If you're operating on *noise avoidance*, you do nothing — you wait until the picture is clear.

**None of these responses is correct.** The right response requires a structured decision framework that weighs geopolitical signal against valuation signal, assigns uncertainty appropriately, and produces a *sized position* — not a paralyzed analysis.

This lesson is that framework.

## 1. The Core Problem: Two Languages That Don't Translate

Investment analysis has two primary signal languages:

**The Valuation Language:** Price-to-earnings. Free cash flow yield. EV/EBITDA. Book value. Discount rates. Margin of safety. These metrics are precise, quantifiable, and time-tested. They answer the question: *Is this asset cheap or expensive relative to its fundamental value?*

**The Geopolitical Language:** Power dynamics. Regime change. Escalation probability. Supply disruption. Sanctions architecture. Political capture. These signals are qualitative, probabilistic, and context-dependent. They answer a different question: *Is the environment in which this asset operates stable or disrupted — and in what direction is it moving?*

Most investors operate fluently in one language and awkwardly in the other. Value investors are fluent in valuation but often dismissive of geopolitical signals ("noise"). Macro investors are fluent in geopolitics but often vague about specific asset implications ("bearish on the region"). Traders are neither: they read price action and call it geopolitics.

The Prospectra framework demands bilingualism. **But bilingualism without a translation protocol produces cacophony, not clarity.** When both signals are present and they point in different directions — which happens constantly — you need an explicit rule for which signal dominates and by how much.

That rule is the Geopolitical Investment Decision Framework.

## 2. The 2×2 Matrix: The Starting Point

The framework begins with a diagnostic matrix. Every investment situation sits in one of four quadrants defined by two dimensions:

**Dimension 1 — Geopolitical Regime:** Is the geopolitical environment for this asset *stable* (no threshold-crossing events, incremental backdrop) or *disrupted* (active conflict, sanctions, regime change, supply shock)?

**Dimension 2 — Valuation Signal:** Is the asset *cheap* (below historical multiples, wide discount to intrinsic value) or *expensive* (premium multiple, narrow margin of safety)?

```
                    VALUATION
                 CHEAP    |   EXPENSIVE
               ───────────┼────────────
STABLE         QUADRANT 1 | QUADRANT 2
               BUY-GRADE  | HOLD/AVOID
               ───────────┼────────────
DISRUPTED      QUADRANT 3 | QUADRANT 4
               DECISION   | AVOID/SHORT
               POINT      |
```

### Quadrant 1 — Stable + Cheap: Buy-Grade
The ideal setup. Geopolitical backdrop is stable, asset is undervalued. This is classic value investing with geopolitical confirmation. The decision is straightforward: deploy capital according to conviction and sizing rules. The only question is *how much* — not *whether*.

**Example (2020):** European defense equities in early 2020 were trading at 10–12x forward earnings — cheaper than the broad market — while NATO spending commitments created a structural multi-year revenue tailwind. Geopolitical backdrop: stable (no hot war, incremental buildup). Result: Rheinmetall, Leonardo, BAE Systems all compounded at 25–40% CAGR over the subsequent four years as the thesis ran. Classic Q1 setup.

### Quadrant 2 — Stable + Expensive: Hold/Avoid
Expensive assets in stable environments are an investor's least interesting problem: you either don't buy them (new capital), or you hold and reassess your exit thesis (existing position). Geopolitics adds nothing here except the need to monitor for a shift to Q3 or Q4.

### Quadrant 3 — Disrupted + Cheap: The Decision Point
This is the hardest quadrant. It is where the most money is made and lost. The asset is cheap — sometimes spectacularly cheap — but a significant geopolitical disruption is either underway or imminent. Every instinct says "buy the dip." Every risk manager says "wait for clarity." Both are partially wrong.

This is the quadrant that requires the full framework.

### Quadrant 4 — Disrupted + Expensive: Avoid/Short
The easiest quadrant after Quadrant 1. If an asset is expensively valued *and* faces a major geopolitical headwind, there is no case for being long. The disruption can compress both the multiple *and* the earnings. Double compression is the most destructive force in investing.

**Example (2021):** Chinese technology equities in Q1 2021 were trading at 40–60x earnings — expensive by any measure — as the regulatory crackdown on the platform economy was just beginning. Geopolitical backdrop: actively disrupted (CCP asserting control over private capital, data sovereignty laws, Didi IPO crackdown). The Hang Seng Tech Index fell 70% peak-to-trough by March 2022. A pure Q4 setup: the right answer was never "how cheap can I get it?" — it was "why am I here at all?"

## 3. Quadrant 3 in Depth: The Disrupted-and-Cheap Decision

The real work of the Geopolitical Investment Decision Framework lives in Quadrant 3. This is where the Russia-Ukraine February 2022 question sits. Let's resolve it properly.

### Step 3A: Classify the Disruption Type

Not all disruptions are equal. The first task in Q3 is to determine what *kind* of disruption you are facing:

| Disruption Type | Characteristic | Asset Impact Pattern | Duration Expectation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Supply shock** | Sudden reduction in commodity or product supply | Immediate price spike; sustained if structural | Months to years |
| **Demand destruction** | Conflict, sanctions, or regime change destroys economic activity | Revenue/earnings collapse for exposed firms | Quarters to years |
| **Regime change** | Political/regulatory environment fundamentally altered | Discount rate re-pricing; structural earnings revision | Years (often permanent) |
| **Tail event with mean reversion** | Severe but non-structural shock; environment returns to prior state | Sharp drawdown followed by recovery | Weeks to months |

**The February 2022 case:**
- For European energy equities, the invasion was a **supply shock** — not a demand destruction or regime change. The underlying demand for energy was unchanged; the supply of Russian gas to Europe was being disrupted. Energy companies that were not Russian-facing were *net beneficiaries* of the supply shock (higher commodity prices → higher earnings).
- For European industrial equities with high energy input costs, the invasion was **demand destruction** — higher input costs, lower margins.
- For Russian equities (Sberbank, Gazprom, Lukoil), the invasion was **regime change** — the sanction and asset-freeze regime altered the fundamental investment framework so completely that these became uninvestable in Western portfolios regardless of price.

The same event — one invasion — produced three different disruption types for three different asset classes. Getting this classification right determines whether Q3 is a buy, a hold, or a sell.

### Step 3B: Assess Disruption Duration

A supply shock that lasts 6 weeks has very different investment implications than one that lasts 6 years. Duration assessment is the most uncertain part of the framework — but the most consequential.

**The duration assessment tool:**

Ask three questions:
1. **What would need to be true for this disruption to resolve in 3–6 months?** Name the specific conditions (ceasefire, diplomatic agreement, substitute supply identified, policy reversal).
2. **What is the probability of each condition being met within that timeframe?** Force a number, even if it's uncertain.
3. **What is the structural residual if resolution is reached?** Even if the acute disruption resolves, does something permanently change about the asset's operating environment?

**Applied to February 2022 European energy:**
1. *Resolution conditions:* Ceasefire; Russian gas flows resume; sanctions lifted. Probability of all three within 3–6 months: *~5%* (structural military conflict with NATO involvement already signaled).
2. *12–24 month probability of major de-escalation:* *~15–20%* — possible but not base case.
3. *Structural residual:* Even if the conflict resolved, Europe's energy independence initiative was permanently launched. The 30-year structural shift away from Russian gas was a political decision that would survive a ceasefire. This is the key: the *structural baseline had changed* regardless of conflict duration.

**Conclusion for European energy equities:** The supply shock was likely to last 18–36 months minimum; the structural residual (energy independence buildout, LNG infrastructure, nuclear restarts) would benefit energy companies for 5–10 years. The Q3 assessment was: *buy on the disruption, hold through the structural*. The February 24 selloff was a gift.

**What happened:** Equinor went from NOK 245 on February 23 to NOK 390 by June 2022 — a 59% gain in 4 months. TotalEnergies rose 28% in the same period. The Q3 framework called it correctly.

### Step 3C: Assess the Earnings Impact Vector

The disruption's *direction* of earnings impact determines whether Q3 is a buy or a defensive exit:

- If the disruption **raises** the earnings power of the asset (commodity company during supply shock, defense company during conflict escalation, gold miner during monetary credibility crisis): **Q3 = HIGH-CONVICTION BUY**
- If the disruption **has ambiguous** earnings impact (banking sector during geopolitical uncertainty, diversified conglomerate with some exposure): **Q3 = STAGED ENTRY, SMALL SIZE**
- If the disruption **lowers** the earnings power of the asset (energy importer during oil shock, tourist economy during conflict, country with sanction exposure): **Q3 = DO NOT BUY THE CHEAPNESS; WAIT OR SHORT**

This is the core insight: **cheapness in a disrupted environment is only investable if the disruption is a *catalyst*, not a *headwind*, for the specific asset's earnings.** Buying Turkish banks in 2021 because they were "cheap" missed that the disruption (political capture of the central bank, 85% inflation) was actively destroying their earnings power. The cheapness was not a floor — it was a mirage.

## 4. The Signal Hierarchy: When Geopolitics Overrides Valuation, and Vice Versa

Now we can state the framework's decision rules explicitly.

### Rule 1: Threshold-Crossing Geopolitical Events Override Valuation — For 90 Days

When a geopolitical event is classified as **threshold-crossing** (Lesson 89 Stage 1 vocabulary) — a military invasion, a currency peg break, a major regime change, a first-use nuclear event — it overrides the valuation signal for approximately 90 days. During this window:

- Do not rely on pre-event multiples as a floor
- Assume the discount rate for all exposed assets has structurally increased
- Position sizing should be *minimal* — you are in the assessment phase, not the deployment phase
- Use options (defined-risk instruments) rather than direct positions where possible

**Why 90 days?** Because threshold-crossing events typically require 30–60 days for the market to price the new regime and another 30 days for the analytical consensus to form around the medium-term scenario. Investors who deploy full-size positions in the first 72 hours of a threshold-crossing event are almost always wrong about either the direction or the magnitude. The exception: if the asset is *already exposed* to the geopolitical event (e.g., you're long Gazprom on February 23) — in that case, exit immediately, because the disruption has directly targeted your earnings thesis.

### Rule 2: Extreme Valuation Overrides Incremental Geopolitical Noise

When an asset is at a **valuation extreme** (e.g., 2+ standard deviations below historical average multiple), and the geopolitical backdrop is **incremental rather than threshold-crossing** — elevated uncertainty but not structural regime change — valuation dominates. Buy the cheapness. The geopolitical noise will mean-revert before the valuation gap closes.

**The test for "incremental":** Can you name a 12-month scenario under which the geopolitical situation has returned to its prior baseline? If yes, it's incremental. If the answer is "no scenario gets us back to baseline" — it's threshold-crossing, and Rule 1 applies.

**Example:** European energy equities in 2019–2021 were trading at extreme discount to US peers (P/E of 8–10x vs. 15–18x for US counterparts). The geopolitical noise included: Brexit, ESG-driven divestment pressure, periodic trade tension. None of these was threshold-crossing. The correct answer was: buy the valuation gap, the noise will not prevent earnings from compounding. Correct.

### Rule 3: Converging Signals Require Synthesis, Not Averaging

When geopolitical signal and valuation signal point in the *same direction*, position sizing should be maximum within the Kelly constraints (Lesson 89). Aligned signals are the highest-conviction setups in the framework.

When they point in *opposite directions* — expensive asset facing geopolitical tailwind, or cheap asset facing geopolitical headwind — apply the **disruption type and earnings vector analysis** from Step 3B/3C above. Never average the signals arithmetically. Synthesis requires understanding which signal *dominates the earnings outcome*.

## 5. Position Sizing: The Uncertainty Stack

Lesson 89 introduced Modified Kelly sizing (30–50% of full Kelly for geopolitical theses). Lesson 93 adds the **Uncertainty Stack** — a calibration mechanism that determines where within the 30–50% range to land.

The Uncertainty Stack has five layers. Each layer adds or subtracts from the base sizing:

```
BASE SIZE: 50% Kelly (maximum geopolitical allocation)

LAYER 1 — Event Type Modifier:
  + Threshold-crossing event (assessed within 90-day window): -15%
  + Incremental event: 0%
  + Stable backdrop: +5% (reward for clarity)

LAYER 2 — Duration Confidence Modifier:
  + High duration confidence (structural, 3-5yr horizon): 0%
  + Medium duration confidence (12-24 months): -5%
  + Low duration confidence (unclear, could resolve fast): -10%

LAYER 3 — Earnings Vector Modifier:
  + Disruption is a direct earnings catalyst for this asset: +10%
  + Disruption is ambiguous: 0%
  + Disruption is an earnings headwind: -15% (may → 0 or short)

LAYER 4 — Portfolio Correlation Modifier:
  + This position *diversifies* existing geopolitical exposures: +5%
  + This position is uncorrelated: 0%
  + This position *concentrates* existing exposures: -10%

LAYER 5 — Instrument Precision Modifier:
  + Using options (defined-risk, correct direction): +5% (max sized up)
  + Using direct position (most liquid, most direct): 0%
  + Using proxy/indirect (adds idiosyncratic risk): -5%

FINAL SIZE: Sum of base + layer adjustments, floored at 0.5%, ceilinged at 5% of portfolio
```

**Applied to Equinor, February 25, 2022:**
- Base: 50% Kelly
- L1: Threshold-crossing event → -15% → at 35%
- L2: Duration confidence — structural (EU energy independence) → 0% → at 35%
- L3: Earnings vector — direct catalyst (higher oil/gas prices → higher revenue) → +10% → at 45%
- L4: Correlation — if no existing energy exposure, this diversifies: +5% → at 50%
- L5: Direct position (Equinor on Oslo Bors) → 0% → **50% Kelly**

At 50% Kelly on a 3% Kelly optimal allocation, this is **1.5% of portfolio** — a meaningful position, sized to capture the asymmetric upside, but not large enough to be catastrophic if the conflict escalated in ways that damaged Norwegian assets.

**The discipline:** A 1.5% position in Equinor returning 59% in 4 months adds ~0.9% to total portfolio return. Over 10 such setups per year, the compounding is substantial. This is the math of *systematic geopolitical investing at appropriate sizes* — not large concentrated bets on outcomes you can't fully predict.

## 6. The Geopolitical Investment Scorecard: A Decision Tool

To operationalize the framework in real time, use a one-page decision scorecard for each potential position. This forces the necessary discipline when markets are volatile and cognitive load is high.

```
GEOPOLITICAL INVESTMENT SCORECARD
==================================
Date: _______________
Asset / Instrument: _______________

STEP 1 — QUADRANT CLASSIFICATION
  Geopolitical backdrop: [Stable / Disrupted]
  Valuation signal: [Cheap / Expensive / Neutral]
  Quadrant: [1 / 2 / 3 / 4]
  → If Q2 or Q4: STOP. Document why. No action unless extraordinary circumstances.

STEP 2 — DISRUPTION TYPE (if Q3 or Q4)
  Supply shock / Demand destruction / Regime change / Tail event?
  Expected duration: _____ months / years (confidence: Low / Medium / High)
  Resolution conditions: _______________
  Structural residual after resolution: _______________

STEP 3 — EARNINGS VECTOR
  Does this disruption RAISE / LOWER / AMBIGUOUSLY affect earnings?
  → If LOWER: no position. Cheapness is not a floor.

STEP 4 — THESIS (five questions, Lesson 89 format)
  Claim: _______________
  Mispricing: _______________
  Direction: [Long / Short]    Instrument: _______________
  Timeframe: _____ months
  Falsifiable condition: _______________

STEP 5 — UNCERTAINTY STACK SIZING
  Base: 50% Kelly
  L1 (Event type): ___
  L2 (Duration confidence): ___
  L3 (Earnings vector): ___
  L4 (Correlation): ___
  L5 (Instrument): ___
  FINAL SIZE: ___% of portfolio

STEP 6 — LOG IN investment_log.md
```

This scorecard takes approximately 20 minutes to complete under pressure. It is *the* discipline that prevents reactive positioning. An investor who completes a scorecard before acting will never buy a Q4 asset because it "looks cheap," never sell a Q1 asset in a panic, and never size a position so large that a wrong thesis is catastrophic.

## 7. Case Study Synthesis: Four Situations, Four Different Answers

### Case 1: Japanese Equities in 2012–2013 (Q1 — Buy)
**Backdrop:** Geopolitically stable (US security umbrella intact, no hot conflict). Valuations: extreme discount — Japanese equities had been in a 20-year bear market; Nikkei at 10,000 was half its 1990 peak. Abe elected December 2012; Abenomics announced. **Scorecard: Q1, buy grade, maximum sizing within Kelly.** The Nikkei doubled by 2015. The framework called it unambiguously.

### Case 2: Emerging Market Currencies in 2013 (Q3/Q4 — Caution)
**Backdrop:** The Fed's "taper tantrum" (May 2013) disrupted EM FX globally. Brazilian real, Indian rupee, Indonesian rupiah, South African rand all sold off sharply. Valuations became "cheap" on purchasing power parity measures. **But:** was this a supply shock, demand destruction, or regime change? It was *neither* — it was a capital flow shock triggered by dollar repricing, a *tail event with mean reversion potential*. Duration confidence: medium (Fed would eventually clarify). Earnings vector: ambiguous (depends entirely on which companies, which sectors). **Scorecard: Q3, staged entry only, 1% sizes maximum, options preferred.** The correct answer was: nibble in the cheapest currencies (INR, IDR recovered 30%+ by 2014), avoid the currencies with structural BoP problems (BRL continued weakening for 3 years). Not a simple "buy EM" — a *discriminating* response.

### Case 3: Russian Equities in February 2022 (Q3 → Q4 reclassification)
**Backdrop:** Disrupted (invasion). Russian equities had traded at extreme discount (P/E of 4–5x). On February 24, they looked "even cheaper." **Scorecard:** Apply Step 3B — duration assessment: What would need to be true for resolution in 3–6 months? Full Russian withdrawal. Probability: <5%. Apply Step 3C — earnings vector: Sanctions directly targeted earnings (SWIFT exclusion, asset freeze, Western corporate exit). Vector: **LOWER**. Rule: if disruption lowers earnings power, cheapness is not a floor. **Stop analysis. No long position.** The Moscow Exchange was suspended for 3 weeks; when it reopened, foreign access was restricted; Russian equities became uninvestable for Western funds regardless of "price." The P/E of 4x became a P/E of 0 for practical purposes. The framework correctly identified this as Q3 → Q4 reclassification once the sanctions severity was clear.

### Case 4: Taiwan Semiconductor (TSMC) in 2022–2023 (Q3 — Buy with Hedged Sizing)
**Backdrop:** Cross-strait tensions elevated throughout 2022 (Pelosi visit, PLA exercises). TSMC fell 40% peak-to-trough in 2022. Valuations: attractive (15–17x earnings for the world's most critical semiconductor manufacturer). **Scorecard:** Disruption type — *tail event with incremental escalation* (not an actual invasion; PLA exercises are political signaling, not conflict). Duration: indefinite — tensions will remain elevated but full-scale invasion is not base case over 12 months. Earnings vector: **AMBIGUOUS to RAISE** — TSMC's earnings are determined by global chip demand, not cross-strait politics directly; supply chain diversification (Arizona fab) actually reduces long-term disruption risk. **Q3 — staged entry, 1.5–2% sizes, with a put-spread hedge as tail protection.** TSMC recovered from $60 to $140 by early 2024 — a 133% return. The Taiwan geopolitical risk was real but was not the earnings driver. The framework correctly identified the asymmetry.

## 8. Investment Implications

The framework itself has several direct portfolio implications:

**1. Geopolitical premium in portfolio construction:** At any given time, approximately 15–20% of your portfolio's active positions should be in assets where the geopolitical signal is the *primary* thesis driver. If it's lower, you are underusing the Prospectra framework. If it's higher, you are over-concentrated in geopolitical uncertainty.

**2. Quadrant monitoring as a rebalancing trigger:** Every 30 days, re-classify each live position in the 2×2 matrix. A position that has migrated from Q1 to Q3 (geopolitical environment has become disrupted) requires immediate reassessment — the original thesis may still be intact, but the risk profile has changed. A Q1 → Q3 reclassification is a mandatory pause-and-reassess, not an automatic sell.

**3. The "cheapness trap" as a systematic risk:** Cognitive bias research (Kahneman, Thaler) consistently shows that investors anchor on prior prices as a floor. The full constraint analysis (Lesson 92) is full of "cheap" assets that became cheaper — Turkish bonds in 2020–2022, Argentine equities in 2019–2023 peak crisis, Russian equities in February 2022. The Geopolitical Investment Scorecard is specifically designed to prevent the cheapness trap by forcing the earnings vector question before any sizing decision.

**4. Asymmetric instruments for threshold-crossing events:** In the 90-day assessment window following a threshold-crossing event, the correct instrument is almost always an option — not a direct position. Options cost premium but provide a defined-risk way to participate in the resolution of a highly uncertain situation. The premium is not waste; it is the price of the uncertainty buffer.

Applying the framework to the current environment (from the June 2, 2026 briefing):

|---|---|---|
| European defense equities | Q1: Stable-structural buildup, cheap vs. US peers | Maximum sizing within Kelly — thesis intact, momentum confirmed |

| TSMC / Taiwan semiconductors | Q3: Tail event backdrop (ongoing cross-strait tension) + cheap vs. growth | 1.5% position, put-spread tail hedge, 18-month thesis on AI chip demand |

## Reflection Questions

1. **The Q3 Reclassification Problem:** In the Russia 2022 case, the correct answer shifted from "analyze carefully" to "no position" as the scope of sanctions became clear over the first 48–72 hours. This reclassification — from Q3 to Q4 — is time-sensitive and high-stakes. Design a real-time alert system: what three *specific observable signals* would trigger an immediate Q3 → Q4 reclassification for any asset, forcing a stop-analysis-and-exit rule? Make them concrete and measurable, not descriptive.

2. **The 90-Day Rule Under Pressure:** The framework says threshold-crossing events override valuation for 90 days — use minimal size, prefer options. But markets don't wait 90 days to price new regimes. The Equinor example shows the stock moved 59% in 4 months. If you used minimal sizing in the first 90 days, you missed much of the move. Defend the 90-day rule: is the tradeoff — missing some upside to avoid catastrophic downside — the right one? Under what conditions, if any, would you compress the 90-day window to 30 days?

3. **The Scorecard as Cognitive Forcing Function:** The Geopolitical Investment Scorecard is explicitly designed as a *behavioral* tool, not just an analytical one — it forces the earnings vector question before sizing decisions. Cognitive bias research suggests that structured decision tools only work if they are used *before* an intuitive conclusion is formed (pre-decisional, not post-decisional rationalization). How do you enforce this in practice? What organizational structure or personal habit ensures the scorecard is completed *before* the investor has already decided what they want to do?
