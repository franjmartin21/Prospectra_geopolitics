## Opening Question

In 1997, the Thai baht had been pegged to the U.S. dollar for over a decade. The Bank of Thailand had foreign exchange reserves. The economy was growing. The government was not running large deficits. The peg looked solid.

Within three months, the baht had lost 40% of its value. Within six months, the contagion had destroyed currencies across Southeast Asia, triggered financial crises in South Korea and Indonesia, contributed to Russia's default in 1998, and nearly brought down a U.S. hedge fund that threatened to destabilize global markets.

**The question:** If the peg looked defensible on the conventional metrics, why did it collapse? And more importantly — *what were the signals that a small number of investors read correctly before the collapse, that the majority missed?*

This is the anatomy question. Understanding *why* currency crises happen is inseparable from knowing *how* to position ahead of them.

## 1. The Three Generations of Currency Crisis Theory

Economists built three generations of models to explain why currency crises happen. Each generation was built *after* a crisis exposed the inadequacy of the previous model. Investors who understand all three have a genuine analytical edge.

---

### Generation 1 — The Fundamentals Model (Krugman, 1979)

**Core thesis:** Currency crises happen when governments run unsustainable fiscal deficits financed by money printing, which exhausts foreign exchange reserves and makes the fixed peg untenable.

**The mechanism:**
1. Government runs a persistent fiscal deficit
2. Central bank monetizes the deficit (prints money to fund it)
3. Domestic money supply grows faster than foreign reserves
4. Inflation erodes the real value of the currency, making the peg increasingly overvalued
5. Speculators, recognizing the reserves will eventually be exhausted, attack the peg before it collapses naturally — rationally accelerating the inevitable

**What this gets right:** It explains Latin American crises of the 1970s-80s perfectly. Argentina (repeatedly), Brazil, Mexico 1994-95 — all followed this script. Fiscal profligacy + monetization + pegged exchange rate = eventual currency crisis.

**What it misses:** The Asian crisis of 1997. Thailand had strong fiscal fundamentals. No excessive money printing. Government balance sheet was fine. The first-generation model said there was no crisis coming. There was.

---

### Generation 2 — The Self-Fulfilling Attack Model (Obstfeld, 1994)

**Core thesis:** Currency crises can happen even with sound fundamentals, *if enough people believe they will happen*. The peg becomes a coordination game, and belief alone can tip it.

**The mechanism:**
1. Government has a painful choice: defend the peg (requires high interest rates → damages the economy) or abandon it (removes the constraint)
2. If investors believe the government will abandon the peg, they attack it
3. The attack forces the government to raise interest rates to defend it
4. High interest rates raise unemployment, increase borrowing costs, damage growth
5. Political pressure rises to abandon the peg
6. The government abandons — *precisely because enough investors believed it would*

The crisis becomes self-fulfilling. There was no fundamental reason the peg had to fail — but once enough market participants coordinated around the expectation of failure, failure became the equilibrium.

**The British pound, 1992.** This is the canonical case. The UK had joined the European Exchange Rate Mechanism (ERM) in 1990 with the pound pegged at 2.95 DM. Germany, needing to finance reunification, had raised rates. The UK, in recession, needed *lower* rates. The peg created an impossible conflict.

George Soros recognized the coordination problem: if enough capital could be assembled to threaten the Bank of England's reserves, the cost of defense (maintaining high rates in a recession) would become politically unbearable. He shorted £10 billion. The Bank of England spent £27 billion in a single day defending the peg. By evening, it had withdrawn from the ERM. Soros made $1 billion.

**What it misses:** The self-fulfilling model suggests crises are arbitrary — any peg is vulnerable to a large enough coordinated attack. This can't be right. The Swiss franc peg to the euro (2011–2015) held for four years despite enormous speculative pressure, then broke on the SNB's own initiative. Fundamentals still matter — they just don't *solely* determine outcomes.

---

### Generation 3 — Balance Sheet Contagion and Hidden Vulnerabilities (Kaminsky-Reinhart, 1999)

**Core thesis:** Currency crises are often driven by *balance sheet mismatches* — particularly dollar-denominated liabilities against local-currency assets — that create fragility invisible in standard fiscal metrics.

**The mechanism:**
1. Private sector (banks, corporations) borrow in dollars because dollar rates are lower and the peg makes dollar borrowing appear safe
2. Dollar liabilities accumulate against local-currency assets and revenues
3. When the peg breaks (for any reason), the domestic currency depreciates
4. Dollar liabilities soar in local-currency terms overnight
5. Balance sheets become insolvent in a single exchange rate move
6. Financial system seizes — credit dries up, growth collapses
7. The currency falls further as capital flees the collapsing financial system

**This is what happened in Thailand, 1997.** Thai banks and corporations had borrowed heavily in dollars at low U.S. interest rates, lending domestically in baht. The fiscal accounts looked fine. But the aggregate banking system had an enormous hidden dollar liability. When speculative attacks (triggered by a current account deficit and overvalued real exchange rate — first-generation signals) began, the baht fell, Thai banks' dollar liabilities exploded in baht terms, and a banking crisis compounded the currency crisis. Indonesia, South Korea, Malaysia followed the same script.

**Key insight:** Balance sheet crises create feedback loops between currency depreciation and financial sector insolvency. This is why third-generation crises are the most destructive — they aren't corrected by a one-time exchange rate adjustment. They destroy credit systems.

## 2. The Warning Indicators — The Crisis Checklist

From the three generations of theory, and from empirical studies of 200+ currency crises (Kaminsky-Reinhart-Schmukler, 2003), six indicators have strong predictive power:

| Indicator | Warning Signal | Threshold |
|---|---|---|
| **Current account deficit** | Persistent deficit requiring foreign financing | >4–5% of GDP |
| **Real exchange rate overvaluation** | Currency substantially above PPP or trend | >15–20% vs. 5-year average |
| **Foreign reserve coverage** | Reserves insufficient to cover short-term external debt + 3 months of imports | <3 months import cover OR <100% short-term debt coverage (Greenspan-Guidotti rule) |
| **Private credit growth** | Rapid expansion of private borrowing, especially in foreign currency | >20% YoY real credit growth |
| **Banking sector vulnerability** | Rising NPLs, foreign currency liabilities, maturity mismatches | NPLs >10%, FX liability >40% of bank liabilities |
| **Political constraints on defense** | High unemployment, recession, election cycle — makes interest rate defense costly | Context-specific |

**The combination matters more than any single indicator.** Thailand in 1997 had a current account deficit of 8% of GDP, a real exchange rate that had appreciated 25% over five years (making exports uncompetitive), banking sector dollar liabilities, and a central bank that had secretly used forward contracts to exhaust *true* reserves while reported reserves looked adequate.

No single indicator was fatal. All six together were.

## 3. Contagion — Why Crises Spread

The 1997-98 Asian crisis spread from Thailand to Malaysia, Indonesia, the Philippines, South Korea, and then Russia in 18 months. This is not coincidental or irrational. Three mechanisms drive contagion:

**1. Trade links.** Thailand's depreciation made its exports cheaper relative to competitors. Countries competing in similar export markets (textiles, electronics) saw their currencies become overvalued relative to a devalued baht — their fundamentals worsened *because* Thailand devalued.

**2. Financial channel contagion.** International investors with leveraged positions in Asian markets needed to raise cash when Thai positions moved against them. They sold whatever was liquid — Korean won, Indonesian rupiah, Malaysian ringgit — regardless of those countries' fundamentals. Forced selling creates the correlation.

**3. Information channel (the "wake-up call" hypothesis).** The Thai crisis revealed that dollar-denominated private sector borrowing was a *regional* pattern, not a Thai idiosyncrasy. Investors reassessed similar vulnerabilities in other markets and sold them before the crisis arrived. This is rational — it is front-running the realization of a structural vulnerability that had been mispriced.

**Investment implication:** In a regional contagion, the *correlation structure of the investment portfolio* changes radically. Positions that appeared uncorrelated (Indonesian equities and Korean bonds) become highly correlated as global investors dump the entire emerging market complex. Diversification fails exactly when it is most needed. This is why investors who hold EM during contagion periods often find that all their EM positions move together — the geographic diversification was an illusion.

## 4. Concrete Case Study — Argentina's Repeating Crisis (2018–Present)

Argentina provides the richest live laboratory for currency crisis analysis because the crisis has recurred across all three generations of models simultaneously.

**The 2018 episode — First-generation dynamics:**
- 2015–2018: President Macri ran fiscal deficits of 3–6% of GDP, partially financed by peso printing
- Inflation ran 25–30% annually while the central bank tried to defend a managed exchange rate
- Reserve coverage fell below 3 months of imports
- IMF intervention ($57 billion, the largest in Fund history at the time) — the classic first-generation response

**The 2022–2023 episode — Third-generation dynamics:**
- Private sector and government held $200+ billion in dollar-denominated obligations
- 90%+ of financial savings in Argentina held in dollars *by residents* — Argentines had already learned to distrust the peso
- Multiple parallel exchange rates emerged (official, blue/unofficial, CCL, MEP) as the government tried to maintain an overvalued official rate while capital fled
- Peso lost 50%+ in a single year while the central bank maintained negative real rates — financial repression and currency crisis running simultaneously

**The 2023–2024 Milei episode — Forced adjustment:**
- President Milei's "chainsaw" austerity: eliminated 15 ministries, cut energy subsidies, ran fiscal surplus
- Controlled devaluation then crawling-peg: peso devalued 50% overnight in December 2023
- Inflation briefly hit 290% annually — the shock absorber of a delayed exchange rate correction
- Reserve accumulation began; IMF program renewed

**The investment lesson Argentina provides:** Currency crises in countries with structural dollar liability dependencies don't end cleanly. They cycle — crisis, IMF intervention, brief stability, renewed fiscal pressure, next crisis. The tradeable question is not "will Argentina crisis again" but "where in the cycle is it?"

## 5. Investment Implications — The Positioning Playbook

Currency crises create some of the largest, fastest, and most asymmetric investment opportunities available. The framework has three phases:

---

### Pre-Crisis: Reading the Setup (12–24 months before)

**What to watch:**
- Current account deficit widening beyond 4% of GDP with no structural export improvement
- Real exchange rate reaching multi-year overvaluation vs. PPP (use the IMF's REER indices)
- Foreign reserve coverage declining — particularly if the Greenspan-Guidotti rule (reserves ≥ short-term external debt) is being breached
- Private credit growth accelerating in foreign currency
- Political calendar: elections within 18 months that preclude rate defense

**Positioning:**
- **Short the local currency** via futures, options, or NDF (non-deliverable forward) markets — carry costs are the fee for the asymmetric payoff
- **Avoid EM credit** in the vulnerable country — spreads widen violently in crises
- **Long the dollar** broadly if signals suggest a multi-country contagion is developing — dollar typically rallies strongly in EM stress events as capital repatriates
- **Long gold** as a reserve asset that appreciates in monetary disorder environments

**The risk:** Carry costs + timing risk. Currencies can remain overvalued for years against fundamentals (the market can stay irrational longer than you can stay solvent). Position sizing must account for the time component — large short with too much leverage fails even if the directional call is correct.

---

### Crisis Onset: The Acute Phase (0–6 months)

**What happens:** Reserves drain rapidly as the central bank defends the peg. Often disguised via forward contracts (true reserves are less than reported). The peg breaks, usually in a weekend announcement or forced devaluation. Volatility is extreme.

**The acute-phase trade:**
- Currency depreciation accelerates — shorts pay off
- Local equity markets crash in dollar terms even if they hold in local currency (devaluation wipes out dollar-denominated returns)
- Sovereign spreads blow out — credit shorts / CDS positions pay
- Cross-asset correlations within the country approach 1.0 — nearly every local-currency asset falls together

**The contrarian setup that emerges:**
- Export-oriented sectors *benefit* from devaluation — they earn foreign currency against local-currency costs. Brazilian agribusiness after the 2018 real devaluation. Turkish exporters after the 2021-22 lira crisis. These tend to get oversold in the panic, creating entry points.
- **Hard currency sovereign bonds** issued by the *IMF-recipient government* can be bought at deep discounts if the IMF backstop makes eventual repayment probable — but this requires careful credit analysis. Emerging market distressed debt is a specialist strategy.

---

### Post-Crisis: The Reconstruction Play (6–36 months after)

**What to watch for:**
- IMF program implementation on track (fiscal adjustment milestones being met)
- Real exchange rate undervaluation — the currency has overshot to the downside
- Reserve accumulation beginning
- Inflation starting to stabilize (lagged — typically 12–18 months after the currency shock)

**Positioning:**
- **Long local-currency bonds** once inflation has peaked and real rates turn positive — some of the best fixed income returns available are in EM bonds 12–24 months post-crisis when real rates are high and the currency is undervalued
- **Long local equities** (hedged into hard currency initially) — post-crisis reforms often unleash productivity and reform dividends
- **Long the currency itself** via carry trade once stability is established — the IMF-stabilized currency often becomes a positive carry trade

**Historical examples:**
- **Russian OFZ bonds, 2014–2015:** After the ruble collapse and sanction shock, Russian local-currency bonds briefly yielded 15%+ real returns. Investors who entered in mid-2015 earned extraordinary returns over the next 2 years.
- **Brazilian real, 2016:** After the impeachment of Dilma Rousseff and election of Temer, the real went from 4.2/USD to 3.1/USD in 6 months. Local investors who stayed long Brazilian assets through the crisis were compensated.
- **Turkey, 2023:** Post-rate-normalization (Erdogan reversed his heterodox rate policy), Turkish lira bonds briefly offered 35–40% nominal yields. The real return was punishing in the acute phase; the reconstruction trade emerging in 2024 required precise timing.

## 6. The 2026 Risk Landscape

Several economies currently display the multi-indicator vulnerability profile:

**Egypt:** Current account deficit ~5% of GDP, official exchange rate still substantially overvalued vs. parallel market, $40B+ in external dollar obligations, IMF program in progress but reform compliance mixed. Pound has been devalued multiple times since 2022. The third-generation balance sheet risk remains elevated.

**Pakistan:** External debt servicing exceeds foreign exchange earnings. IMF program active but precarious. Reserves below Greenspan-Guidotti threshold. Political instability constrains rate defense.

**Nigeria:** Naira devalued 60%+ since 2023 under Tinubu reforms. Parallel market gap has narrowed but oil revenue dependency and subsidy removal create fragility. Reconstruction trade emerging but requires monitoring.

**Geopolitical overlay:** Note that several 2026 currency vulnerabilities have a geopolitical dimension — Egypt's vulnerability is amplified by Gaza conflict disruption to Suez Canal revenues and tourism. Pakistan's is inseparable from U.S.-China-India regional dynamics that affect aid flows. Currency crises are increasingly not *just* macroeconomic events — they are events where geopolitical sponsorship (or its withdrawal) is decisive in determining whether a peg survives.

- L-71–74: Inflation, debt supercycles, fiscal dominance, financial repression — the internal dynamics that drive the fiscal pressures behind first-generation crises

**Lesson 75 synthesizes this into a crisis cycle framework.** The signal-to-investment pipeline is:
1. Identify countries with multi-indicator vulnerability (fundamentals screen)
2. Assess political constraints on defense (political economy layer)
3. Identify balance sheet dollar liabilities (third-generation risk)
4. Geopolitical overlay: does this country have a great-power sponsor? (affects IMF access, bilateral support)
5. Position across the cycle: short pre-crisis, exploit the dislocation at onset, long the reconstruction

**Portfolio-level implication:** In a world of elevated EM debt burdens and U.S. rate cycles, the probability of 2–4 significant currency crises per decade is structural, not episodic. A systematic screening framework — built in Databricks — can identify vulnerable countries 12–18 months before consensus, at the point where the position is still cheap to enter.

## Databricks Angle

**Dataset:** IMF Balance of Payments Statistics + World Bank External Debt database (quarterly) + BIS cross-border banking statistics (quarterly).

**Pipeline idea: Currency Vulnerability Score (CVS)**

Build a quarterly-updating composite score for 50+ countries:

```python
# Inputs (each normalized to z-score vs 10-year history):
z_current_account = (ca_pct_gdp - hist_mean) / hist_std  # negative = worse
z_reer = (reer_index - hist_mean) / hist_std              # positive = overvalued = worse
z_reserve_coverage = (reserves_months_imports - 3) / hist_std  # negative = worse
z_credit_growth = (private_credit_growth - 15) / hist_std     # positive = worse
z_short_term_debt = (st_ext_debt / reserves - 1) / hist_std   # positive = worse

CVS = weighted_composite([z_current_account, z_reer, z_reserve_coverage, 
                          z_credit_growth, z_short_term_debt],
                         weights=[0.25, 0.25, 0.20, 0.15, 0.15])
```

**Output:** Ranked table of country vulnerability scores, quarterly updated. Countries crossing a defined threshold enter a "watch list." Historical backtesting of which CVS levels predicted crises within 4, 8, and 12 quarters provides calibration.

**GDELT overlay:** Augment with political stability sentiment (GDELT country tone scores) to capture the political economy constraint on defense — countries with deteriorating governance sentiment AND high CVS scores represent the highest-risk profiles.
