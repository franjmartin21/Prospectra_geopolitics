## The Core Framework: Three Scenarios, Not One

The critical error in analyzing monetary transitions is to bet on a single outcome. Monetary systems are sticky — they persist far longer than economic logic would suggest because they are embedded in contracts, habits, legal frameworks, and network effects. But when they do transition, the shift is nonlinear and faster than expected. The right posture is scenario weighting, not point prediction.

### Scenario A: Dollar Resilience (Probability: 35%)
The dollar faces serious structural headwinds but retains hegemony because no alternative offers the depth, liquidity, and rule-of-law infrastructure that global commerce demands. The RMB faces capital controls and political risk. Gold cannot run a credit system. CBDCs are a payment rail, not a reserve asset. The euro lacks fiscal union. In this scenario, dollar weakness is cyclical (driven by fiscal cycles and Fed policy), not structural. The dollar remains 55-60% of global reserves in 2035.

**Portfolio implication:** Moderate USD diversification sufficient. Gold as portfolio insurance (5-8%). No need to actively position against dollar dominance.

### Scenario B: Managed Multipolar Transition (Probability: 45%)
The most likely scenario. Dollar slowly loses share — perhaps reaching 45-50% of reserves by 2035 — but no single currency fills the gap. A fragmented reserve system emerges: dollar bloc, RMB bloc (limited to BRICS+ trade), gold (as neutral asset), and potentially commodity-backed settlement instruments for specific trade flows. This is not a dollar collapse; it is a controlled erosion of dollar privilege.

**Portfolio implication:** This is the scenario we have been positioning for throughout this curriculum. Real assets outperform. Gold anchors the portfolio. Commodity-exporting EMs benefit from pricing power and bloc membership. Dollar-denominated debt becomes structurally riskier for sovereigns without strong fiscal positions.

### Scenario C: Dollar Crisis / Disorderly Transition (Probability: 20%)
A fiscal crisis in the US — triggered by a bond market revolt, a failed Treasury auction, or a sovereign credit downgrade that triggers margin calls across dollar-denominated debt — forces a disorderly monetary adjustment. Dollar loses 20-30% of its reserve share within 3-5 years. The transition is chaotic, not managed. Inflation in dollar-linked economies is severe. Hard assets and non-dollar reserve currencies outperform dramatically.

**Portfolio implication:** Maximum weight in gold, commodities, non-dollar reserve currencies (CHF, SGD, NOK), and commodity-exporting equities. Avoid long-duration dollar bonds.

## The Infrastructure Being Built: What to Watch

The monetary transition is not abstract — it is being operationalized. Four pieces of infrastructure deserve close monitoring:

### 1. mBridge (Multi-CBDC Platform)
Built by the BIS Innovation Hub with China, Hong Kong, Thailand, UAE, and Saudi Arabia. Processed $1T+ in cross-border settlements in 2025. Represents the plumbing for a parallel payment system that bypasses SWIFT and the correspondent banking network. **Key insight:** mBridge is not a reserve currency — it is a settlement rail. But settlement rails are to reserve systems what highways are to cities: they determine which routes become viable.

**Databricks angle:** Track mBridge transaction volume growth against SWIFT message data. Inflection in mBridge growth relative to SWIFT (especially for energy trade) is an early signal of Scenario B deepening.

### 2. BRICS+ Settlement Unit
Proposed commodity-basket-backed unit of account for BRICS+ trade settlements. Not yet operational as a true currency. Russia and China diverge on design (Russia wants a reserve currency; China wants a settlement unit that keeps the RMB subordinate to preserve bilateral leverage). The political economy of BRICS+ monetary cooperation is more fragile than headlines suggest. **Key insight:** Watch for whether this unit gets used to price oil — if Saudi Arabia, UAE, and Iraq begin invoicing even 5% of exports in BRICS units, the petrodollar architecture begins to crack.

### 3. Central Bank Gold Accumulation
The single clearest behavioral signal of reserve diversification. Central banks have net-bought gold for five consecutive years. China's reported reserves ($3,000/oz × 3,000+ tonnes = $9T) are almost certainly understated. India, Poland, Kazakhstan, Turkey, and Gulf states have all increased gold reserves materially. **Key insight:** This is sovereign portfolio theory in action — central banks are the "smart money" in the monetary transition, and they are voting with their balance sheets.

### 4. US Treasuries Demand Decomposition
Foreign official holdings of US Treasuries (tracked via TIC data) have been declining as a share of total outstanding since 2014. Private foreign demand has partially offset this — but private foreign buyers are price-sensitive in ways that official reserve managers are not. A Treasury market that increasingly depends on price-sensitive buyers is more volatile. **Key insight:** Watch the bid-to-cover ratio on 10Y and 30Y auctions, and watch the "indirect bidder" allocation. Deterioration is an early warning system for Scenario C.

## Historical Grounding: How the Last Transition Happened

The most instructive precedent is the British pound's loss of reserve currency status in the 20th century.

In 1900, the pound sterling was the world's reserve currency, backed by the gold standard and the British Empire's command of global trade. By 1956, it was over — the Suez Crisis exposed British dependence on US dollar financing and ended any pretense of independent monetary power.

**Key lessons from the sterling transition:**
1. The transition took 50 years but the *decisive moment* took 3 days (Suez, November 1956, when the US withheld IMF support until Britain withdrew from Egypt).
2. The US *actively accelerated* Britain's monetary decline to replace it as hegemon. No current power is positioned to do this to the US — there is no clear successor.
3. Sterling's loss of reserve status did not destroy Britain's economy. It destroyed Britain's *imperial capacity* — the ability to sustain military and political commitments globally.
4. British investors who held sterling-denominated assets lost purchasing power in real terms over 30 years. Those who diversified into dollar assets, gold, and commodity exposure preserved and built wealth.

**The 2026 parallel:** The US is not Britain. Its economy is larger, its capital markets deeper, and its military more dominant. But the structural dynamics — fiscal overreach, overextension of the reserve privilege, rising challengers — rhyme strongly with Britain in 1930-1960.

## The Investment Framework: Positioning for the Transition

Based on the scenario weights above (35% resilience / 45% managed transition / 20% crisis), the portfolio construction logic is:

### What Performs Across All Three Scenarios

**1. Commodity-exporting equities (especially energy and critical minerals)**
- In Scenario A: Normal business cycles, energy demand growth from AI/electrification.
- In Scenario B: Pricing power from commodity-backed settlement; resource nationalism premium.
- In Scenario C: Hard asset inflation protection; critical infrastructure premium.
- *Position: Overweight energy (XLE-type exposure), copper, and critical minerals producers.*

**2. Gold**
- In Scenario A: Portfolio insurance, low drag.
- In Scenario B: Structural monetary demand from central banks; performs as neutral reserve asset.
- In Scenario C: Best performer — fiat crisis hedge, historical monetary anchor.
- *Position: 10-15% portfolio allocation. Not a trade — a structural position.*

**3. Non-dollar sovereign bonds from fiscally credible states**
- Singapore, Switzerland, Norway, and to a lesser extent Australia offer credible sovereign balance sheets in non-dollar currencies.
- In Scenario A: Moderate diversification benefit.
- In Scenario B: Genuine reserve diversification play.
- In Scenario C: Flight-to-safety outside dollar system.
- *Position: 5-10% in non-dollar developed sovereign bonds as dollar hedge.*

### What to Avoid or Underweight

**1. Long-duration US Treasuries (10Y+)**
- In Scenario A: Fair value, no alpha.
- In Scenario B: Fiscal dominance means term premium rises; capital losses.
- In Scenario C: Severe capital losses and currency depreciation in real terms.
- *Position: Underweight. Duration is the wrong risk to own in a fiscal dominance regime.*

**2. EM sovereigns with dollar-denominated debt and current account deficits**
- The most exposed to a disorderly dollar repricing.
- *Position: Avoid or short. Use CDS on Turkey, Egypt, Pakistan as tail risk hedge.*

**3. Rate-sensitive sectors (utilities, REITs, long-duration growth equities)**
- Monetary transition means structurally higher rates in the dollar system (or structural repression — either way, long-duration equities face headwinds).
- *Position: Underweight.*

## The CEO Portfolio Note

As of October 2026, I assess we are in the early-to-middle phase of **Scenario B: Managed Multipolar Transition**. The pace is faster than consensus expects — the 2022 Russia sanctions were the accelerant that converted theoretical reserve diversification into urgent sovereign action. The infrastructure (mBridge, gold accumulation, bilateral swap lines, commodity invoicing) is being built in parallel.

**My updated directional views (6-18 month horizon):**
- **Gold:** Structurally long. Target $3,500-4,000/oz within 18 months. Central bank demand is the floor; monetary transition is the ceiling.
- **Commodities:** Structurally long. Energy (oil, LNG, uranium) and critical minerals (copper, lithium) benefit from both the energy transition and geopolitical bloc formation.
- **USD:** Structurally negative but not a near-term trade. The dollar weakens against real assets, not necessarily against other fiat currencies.
- **US Treasuries (10Y+):** Structurally cautious. The term premium repricing is incomplete.
- **Dollar-bloc EM equities:** Selective. Indonesia, India, Brazil — countries with commodity export capacity and strategic non-alignment.

The single biggest risk to this framework: a deflationary shock (severe recession, credit crunch, financial crisis) that triggers a dollar flight-to-safety squeeze. In that scenario, the transition pauses — but it does not reverse. The structural drivers remain. A deflationary shock is a buying opportunity in real assets, not a reason to abandon the framework.
