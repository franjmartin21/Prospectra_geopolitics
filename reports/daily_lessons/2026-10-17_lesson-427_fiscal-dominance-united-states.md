## Opening Question

Here is the number that defines US macroeconomics in 2026:

**$1,000,000,000,000.**

One trillion dollars. That is the net interest bill the United States Treasury is projected to pay this fiscal year — 3.3% of GDP, the highest share since 1991 (when interest rates were also elevated from an inflation-fighting cycle). The total federal debt held by the public stands at approximately 101% of GDP. The CBO's February 2026 baseline projects it reaching 120% of GDP by 2036 — above the previous post-WWII record of 106% in 1946. Net interest payments are projected to reach $2.1 trillion by 2036, growing at more than double the rate of total federal spending.

Now consider what the Federal Reserve is doing: holding the fed funds rate at 3.50–3.75%.

Every 25 basis points the Fed keeps rates elevated costs the Treasury approximately $62 billion per year in additional interest (on a $25 trillion debt base). Every 25bps cut saves $62 billion. Over the course of a year, that is the difference between funding the entire Department of Education or not.

The question this lesson asks is not whether politicians want lower rates. Of course they do. The question is deeper:

**At what point does the fiscal math make it structurally impossible for the Federal Reserve to raise interest rates when inflation requires it — because the economic damage of doing so falls not on the overheating economy but on the government's own balance sheet?**

That is fiscal dominance. And the United States has been drifting toward it for fifteen years.

## 1. The Core Concept: Two Regimes, One Constraint

The theoretical foundation comes from economists Thomas Sargent and Neil Wallace (1981) and was formalized by Eric Leeper (1991). The insight is elegant and uncomfortable.

**The Government Budget Constraint** states that every government, at every moment, faces a fundamental identity:

> **Government Spending = Tax Revenues + New Borrowing + Money Creation**

This is not a normative claim — it is an accounting identity. Governments can only spend what they collect in taxes, what they borrow, or what their central bank creates (monetization). The identity always holds.

The question is: **who has to adjust?**

### Regime 1: Active Monetary / Passive Fiscal (the "Normal" World)

In this regime — which has largely described the US from 1979 to 2019 — the central bank sets interest rates independently to achieve its inflation mandate. Fiscal policy (spending and taxes) adjusts to make the debt sustainable. The government borrows at whatever rate the market demands, and if rates rise, it eventually cuts spending or raises taxes to keep the debt-to-GDP ratio stable.

The central bank is the dominant player. Fiscal policy is passive — it accommodates whatever monetary policy requires.

### Regime 2: Passive Monetary / Active Fiscal (Fiscal Dominance)

In this regime, the fiscal position becomes so large or so politically locked that it cannot adjust. Spending cannot be cut (entitlements, defense, interest). Taxes cannot be raised (political deadlock). The government must borrow — and borrow at whatever volume the deficit requires.

Now the central bank faces an impossible constraint: if it raises rates to fight inflation, it increases the government's interest bill, potentially triggering a sovereign debt spiral. If it keeps rates low to protect the fiscal position, it allows inflation to run. The central bank loses its independence — not because politicians fire the governor, but because the math makes genuine inflation-fighting too costly to credibly execute.

**Fiscal dominance is not primarily a political story. It is a constraint story.** The government's budget constraint dominates monetary policy even without a phone call from the Treasury to the Fed.

## 2. Historical Grounding: The 1942–1951 Precedent

The United States has been in a fiscal dominance regime before. Understanding how it ended is the investment lesson.

### The WWII Yield Curve Control Agreement

In April 1942, the Federal Reserve entered into an agreement with the US Treasury that explicitly subordinated monetary policy to fiscal needs. The arrangement:

- The Fed **pegged the 3-month Treasury bill rate at 0.375%**
- The Fed **pegged the 10-year Treasury bond yield at 2.5%**
- The Fed committed to buy unlimited amounts of Treasury bonds at these prices to maintain the peg

The rationale was straightforward: the US was financing WWII. The 1942 deficit was 26% of GDP. The national debt would reach 106% of GDP by 1946. At market rates, the interest burden would have been crushing. The Fed subordinated itself entirely to the Treasury's funding needs.

**What happened to inflation?** During the war (1942–1945), price controls suppressed measured inflation. When price controls were lifted in 1946–1947, inflation exploded: CPI reached 19.7% in 1947. The suppressed inflation re-emerged the moment the administrative controls were removed.

**What ended fiscal dominance?** The Fed-Treasury Accord of March 1951. After years of rising inflation, the Federal Reserve, led by Chairman Thomas McCabe and his successor William McChesney Martin, negotiated its independence back from the Treasury. The peg was lifted. Rates were allowed to rise. Inflation came down. The formal act of the Fed reclaiming independence from the fiscal authority is the canonical example of an *exit from fiscal dominance* — and it required nearly a decade of suppressed monetary policy and elevated inflation to build the political will to exit.

**The investment lesson from 1942–1951:** Nominal bonds were destroyed in real terms. An investor who held 10-year Treasuries from 1942 to 1952 earned 2.5% nominal while inflation averaged 5–6% — a negative real return of roughly 3% per year. Real assets (commodities, equities, real estate, gold — before gold was remonetized) held purchasing power. This is the canonical portfolio lesson from a fiscal dominance episode.

## 3. The Post-2008 Drift: Three Stages

The US has not declared fiscal dominance. It has drifted toward it through a sequence of structurally reinforcing decisions.

### Stage 1 — QE as Proto-Fiscal Dominance (2009–2014)

When the Fed launched Quantitative Easing in November 2008, it began buying Treasury bonds and mortgage-backed securities. At peak, the Fed's balance sheet reached $4.5 trillion (2015). The effect: the Fed was, in economic terms, financing US deficits by purchasing the debt the Treasury was issuing. The Treasury could issue unlimited debt because there was a guaranteed buyer at the other end of the auction.

This was not technically fiscal dominance — the Fed could (and eventually did) begin raising rates in 2015. But it established the institutional template: the Fed as buyer of last resort for US sovereign debt.

### Stage 2 — COVID and the Debt Explosion (2020–2021)

The 2020 pandemic produced deficits of 14.9% of GDP (2020) and 12.4% of GDP (2021) — larger than the Obama-era stimulus deficits and approaching WWII levels. The Fed's response: cut rates to zero and expand its balance sheet to $8.9 trillion. Total debt held by the public went from 79% of GDP in 2019 to 98% of GDP in 2021 — a 19 percentage point increase in two years.

Critically, the Fed delayed tightening. Despite clear inflation signals in late 2021 (CPI at 6.8% in November 2021), the Fed did not begin hiking until March 2022. The delay is consistent with fiscal dominance theory: the Fed was hesitant to raise rates into a debt overhang that had ballooned during COVID accommodation. Whether or not that was the explicit reason, the effect was the same.

### Stage 3 — Political Capture Risk (2025–2026)

The 2025 budget reconciliation package added approximately $3.8 trillion to the ten-year deficit, primarily through extended tax cuts and new spending. The administration has made explicit statements pressuring the Fed to cut rates. Former President Trump's description of the Fed chair as a "political opponent" for not cutting more aggressively and the deliberate public campaign to tie Fed decisions to economic outcomes is the political overlay on top of the structural math.

**The structural case for fiscal dominance does not require political capture.** But when political pressure aligns with structural constraint, the credibility of the central bank's independence becomes a question rather than an assumption.

## 4. The Mechanism: How Fiscal Dominance Manifests in Markets

Fiscal dominance does not announce itself. It reveals itself through a sequence of observable signals in financial markets.

### Signal 1 — The Real Rate Constraint

In a normal regime, when inflation rises, the Fed raises the real rate (nominal rate minus inflation) to cool demand. In a fiscal dominance regime, every 100bps rise in the real rate adds approximately $250 billion to the annual Treasury interest bill (on $25 trillion of debt). At some point, the real rate rise causes a government budget crisis before it causes a demand slowdown.

**Observable metric:** The Fed's *willingness* to raise real rates above 2% into a debt overhang. In 2022–2023, the Fed did raise real rates to approximately 2.5% — suggesting it was not yet fully captured. But the debt load was lower then (98% of GDP vs. 101% today and rising). The question for 2026–2028 is whether the Fed will raise real rates again if inflation re-accelerates — and whether the fiscal math allows it.

### Signal 2 — Treasury Yield Volatility

Fiscal dominance typically manifests in Treasury markets before it manifests in policy. When bond markets begin to question the fiscal sustainability of a sovereign, yields rise at the long end while the central bank holds the short end — a bear steepener. This is the "fiscal premium" — the extra yield investors demand to hold long-duration government debt because they are pricing in either higher inflation (monetization risk) or higher supply (fiscal deficit risk).

**The 30-year Treasury yield in October 2026 is approximately 4.9%.** The 10-year is ~4.5%. The term premium (the extra yield on long bonds vs. short) has widened from near-zero in 2021 to approximately 80–100bps. This is the market beginning to price fiscal dominance risk.

### Signal 3 — Dollar Debasement Without Nominal Rate Increase

In a fiscal dominance regime, the release valve for fiscal pressure is currency debasement rather than rate adjustment. The government effectively inflates away the real value of the debt. The dollar weakens in real terms (inflation above nominal rate = negative real rate = slow-motion monetization).

**The dollar has weakened approximately 8–10% on a trade-weighted basis in 2025–2026** — consistent with the market beginning to price persistent negative real rates or a structural shift in reserve allocation away from the dollar.

## 5. Fiscal Dominance vs. Default: The Four Escape Paths

When debt becomes unsustainable, governments have four options. Understanding which the US is most likely to use — and which combination — is the investment thesis.

### Path 1 — Fiscal Consolidation (Spending Cuts + Tax Increases)
**Historical examples:** Canada (1990s), UK (2010–2015), Germany (2010s)
**US applicability in 2026:** Low. Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid, and defense constitute approximately 85% of federal spending and are politically immovable in the current configuration. Net interest is 15% and growing. Discretionary non-defense spending (7% of the budget) is mathematically insufficient to solve the problem.
**Investment implication:** This path would be deflationary and dollar-positive. Assign it low probability near-term.

### Path 2 — Inflation (Nominal GDP Growth Above Debt Service)
**Historical examples:** US 1943–1952, UK 1945–1975, most high-debt episodes
**US applicability in 2026:** Moderate-High. The US debt stock is predominantly fixed-rate, so if inflation runs at 4–5% while the average coupon on existing debt is ~3.5%, the real debt burden erodes. The Fed tolerates inflation slightly above target without a forceful response. This is the "soft" fiscal dominance scenario.
**Investment implication:** TIPS outperform nominal Treasuries. Gold, commodities, real assets hold value. Short-duration bonds preferred over long.

### Path 3 — Financial Repression (Forcing Domestic Institutions to Hold Government Debt Below Market Rates)
**Historical examples:** Post-WWII US (Regulation Q capping deposit rates), India and China (state-directed banking), Japan (banks required to hold JGBs)
**US applicability in 2026:** Growing. Arguments for domestic pension funds and insurance companies to hold more Treasuries are already circulating in policy circles. Any regulatory change requiring higher Treasury holdings by financial institutions is financial repression by another name.
**Investment implication:** Private credit and alternatives outperform regulated fixed income. Institutional capital flows into unregulated assets. Offshore allocation increases among those who can access it.

### Path 4 — Explicit Monetization (Central Bank Buys Debt to Cap Yields)
**Historical examples:** Japan (yield curve control 2016–2023), US 1942–1951, Weimar Germany (catastrophic), Turkey (recent, with severe inflation consequences)
**US applicability in 2026:** Low probability in the near term (0–18 months), but not zero. A scenario where long-term Treasury yields rise toward 6%+ — making the fiscal position unsustainable — could force an explicit or implicit YCC framework. Janet Yellen's January 2026 Brookings presentation described fiscal dominance as a regime "where the Fed is not fully free to set interest rates" — language that was notably non-dismissive.
**Investment implication:** This is the tail risk scenario. Gold as reserve asset, inflation hedges, dollar shorts, flight to hard assets globally.

## 6. The 2020–2023 Concrete Example: Did the Fed Already Blink?

The most powerful contemporary test case for US fiscal dominance is the 2021–2022 inflation episode. The timeline is instructive:

- **March–November 2021:** CPI rises from 2.6% to 6.8%. The Fed holds rates at zero and continues $120 billion/month in asset purchases. Justification: "transitory" inflation.
- **December 2021:** Fed taper begins, but rates remain zero with $40+ trillion in gross Treasury issuance scheduled for 2022.
- **March 2022:** First rate hike — 25bps — 15 months after inflation began rising and 4 months after CPI hit 6.8%.
- **June 2022:** CPI peaks at 9.1%. The Fed is now hiking aggressively (75bps increments) — but is 18 months behind the curve.

**The question fiscal dominance theory asks:** Was the delay due to genuine uncertainty about whether inflation was transitory? Or was the delay influenced by the fact that the US Treasury issued $7.5 trillion in net new debt in 2020–2021, and the Fed's balance sheet expansion was absorbing much of that supply?

No Fed chair will answer this question honestly. But the circumstantial evidence — that the delay was largest and most consequential precisely when fiscal need was greatest — is consistent with a proto-fiscal dominance constraint operating on the margins of Fed decision-making.

The consequence: inflation peaked at 9.1% (June 2022), the highest since 1981. The Fed then had to raise rates to 5.25–5.50% to bring it back down — the fastest hiking cycle in 40 years, which destabilized regional banks (SVB, Signature, First Republic, March 2023), crashed long-duration bond portfolios globally (UK LDI crisis, September 2022), and cost the Fed itself over $100 billion in operating losses as it paid more interest on reserves than it earned on its QE portfolio.

**The lesson:** If the Fed blinked in 2021 — even slightly, even partially due to fiscal pressure — the cost was a 9.1% inflation peak, a 5.25% hiking cycle, and three bank failures. The cost of allowing even partial fiscal dominance is not a quiet suppression of rates. It is a boom-bust amplification of the inflation cycle.

## 7. The 2026 Position: Where Are We on the Dominance Spectrum?

A structured assessment of the current US fiscal dominance risk:

| Indicator | Level | Signal |
|---|---|---|
| Debt / GDP | 101% | Elevated (prior record: 106% in 1946) |
| Net interest / GDP | 3.3% | Record since 1991; rising |
| Deficit / GDP | 5.8% | Elevated; structural (not cyclical) |
| 10-year term premium | ~80bps | Rising; fiscal premium emerging |
| Fed real rate (FFR minus CPI) | ~1.8% | Positive but modest |
| Political pressure on Fed | High | Explicit; multi-year campaign |
| Treasury market functioning | Adequate | October 2023 liquidity stress was a warning |
| Reserve currency status | Stable but challenged | HSBC central bank survey: "safe haven status being challenged" |

**Overall assessment:** The US is in a **proto-fiscal dominance regime** — not yet in explicit fiscal dominance, but structurally constrained in ways that limit the Fed's ability to tighten aggressively if inflation re-accelerates. The Fed has positive real rates today; it has demonstrated willingness to hike. But the debt load means the next tightening cycle — if it comes — will encounter larger institutional friction than the last.

The risk is not imminent monetization. The risk is that the Fed's *effective* tightening tolerance has fallen below what the economy will need if the structural fiscal deficits generate persistent inflation. The difference between a Fed that targets 2% CPI and a Fed that tolerates 3–4% CPI to protect the Treasury's funding need is the difference between a stable dollar and the slow erosion of dollar purchasing power over a decade.

## Key Concepts Summary

1. **The Government Budget Constraint** — spending must be financed by taxes, borrowing, or money creation; fiscal dominance occurs when money creation is the only remaining option
2. **Leeper's Two Regimes** — Active Monetary / Passive Fiscal (normal) vs. Passive Monetary / Active Fiscal (fiscal dominance); the US is drifting from the first toward the second
3. **The 1942–1951 Precedent** — the last full US fiscal dominance episode; the Fed-Treasury Accord of 1951 is the historical anchor for what "exiting fiscal dominance" looks like
4. **The Three-Stage Drift** — QE as proto-fiscal dominance (2009–2014); COVID debt explosion (2020–2021); political capture risk (2025–2026)
5. **The Four Escape Paths** — fiscal consolidation (low probability), inflation (moderate-high), financial repression (growing), explicit monetization (tail risk)
6. **The 2021 Blink Test** — whether the Fed's 15-month delay in tightening was influenced by fiscal need; circumstantially consistent with proto-fiscal dominance
7. **FDPI** — the Databricks metric: Fiscal Dominance Pressure Index; composite of interest/GDP, term premium, deficit trajectory, Fed real rate vs. r-star

## Questions for Next Session

1. **The r-star problem:** The neutral rate of interest (r-star) — the rate at which monetary policy is neither stimulative nor restrictive — is currently estimated by the Fed's own models at approximately 0.5% real (the Laubach-Williams model) to 1.5% real (the Holston-Laubach-Williams model). But these models were built on pre-2020 data. If fiscal dominance is suppressing the Fed's effective tightening tolerance, r-star may be *endogenously lowered* by fiscal pressure — meaning the Fed says rates are restrictive at 3.75% even as the fiscal constraint prevents it from going higher. Map the argument: how do you distinguish between a genuinely low r-star and an r-star estimate that has been contaminated by fiscal dominance? What observable data would let you tell the difference?

2. **The Treasury funding cliff:** The US Treasury refinanced approximately $7 trillion in debt in FY2024 and is projected to refinance similar volumes in FY2025–2026, with a significant share maturing in the 1–3 year bucket. If 10-year Treasury yields rise to 5.5% before the maturity cliff is resolved, the weighted average coupon on the debt rises faster than the CBO baseline assumes. Build the sensitivity table: at 4.5% average yield, 5% average yield, 5.5% average yield — what does the net interest / GDP ratio look like in 2030? At what point does it become self-reinforcing (higher interest → larger deficit → more borrowing → higher yields)?

3. **The Japan comparison:** Japan has a debt-to-GDP ratio of approximately 250% (gross), has implemented explicit yield curve control since 2016, and has had the largest carry trade in history funded by near-zero domestic rates. It has not collapsed — it has drifted in low-growth, low-inflation stagnation punctuated by inflation when global supply chains broke. Is the Japan trajectory — 30 years of suppressed rates, mild financial repression, and slow currency debasement — a plausible US path? Or does the dollar's reserve currency status mean the comparison breaks down (the US has the "exorbitant privilege" Japan never had)? Identify the three conditions under which the Japan analogy holds for the US — and the three conditions under which the US reaches an inflection point Japan never did.
