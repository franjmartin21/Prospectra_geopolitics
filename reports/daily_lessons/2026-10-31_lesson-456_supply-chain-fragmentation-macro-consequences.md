## Opening Question

> From 1990 to 2019, global goods trade as a share of GDP rose from 34% to 51%. During that same period, core goods inflation in the United States averaged just 0.1% per year — near zero for three decades. Many economists attributed this to "technology." But the real mechanism was the wholesale offshoring of manufacturing to low-cost geographies: China joined the WTO in 2001, and within a decade became the world's factory floor, suppressing the price of almost every physical good. If that mechanism is now running in reverse — if supply chains are fragmenting, duplicating, and nearshoring — what does it do to inflation, to productivity, and to the interest rate level the world needs to price correctly? And if you are still modeling the world as if "higher-for-longer" is a Fed policy choice rather than a structural macro consequence, what are you getting wrong?

Hold that question.

## 1. The Globalization Dividend — What We Had and Are Now Losing

The three-decade globalization cycle (1990–2019) produced three structural tailwinds for the global economy that are now reversing.

### Tailwind 1: Labor Cost Arbitrage → Goods Deflation

When Apple moved iPhone assembly to Foxconn in Shenzhen, it was not just choosing cheaper labor. It was accessing an entire *industrial ecosystem* — components, tooling, logistics, engineering — that had accumulated in China over two decades. The resulting cost advantage compressed consumer goods prices persistently. The BLS consumer goods deflator ran at roughly -0.5% per year for two decades after China's WTO accession.

**The reversal:** Duplicating supply chains in Vietnam, Mexico, or India does not recreate that cost structure. It creates a second-best alternative at higher cost. Early estimates from academic and corporate studies suggest that a fully diversified supply chain — not dependent on any single country — costs 10–20% more than the hyper-efficient single-source model it replaces. That premium is not a one-time adjustment. It is baked into the structural cost of producing physical goods going forward.

### Tailwind 2: Capital Efficiency → Productivity Growth

Globalization allowed companies to concentrate capital in their highest-return activities (design, branding, software, distribution) while offshoring capital-intensive manufacturing to China. Return on invested capital (ROIC) for US S&P 500 companies rose from approximately 9% in 1990 to 15% by 2019, driven in large part by asset-light business models enabled by outsourced manufacturing.

**The reversal:** Reshoring and friendshoring require large fixed capital investments — factories, equipment, infrastructure — that reduce ROIC. The US CHIPS Act alone requires $52B in subsidies precisely because semiconductor fabs are uneconomic without government support. Intel's Arizona fab is expected to carry a per-unit cost roughly 40% higher than TSMC's Taiwan facilities. This is not a failure of industrial policy — it is the price of geopolitical resilience. But it is a price: capital deployed in strategic reshoring earns lower returns than capital deployed in the 1990s model.

### Tailwind 3: Just-in-Time Inventory → Working Capital Efficiency

The lean supply chain model — pioneered by Toyota, perfected by Apple and Walmart — minimized inventory by relying on predictable, low-cost global logistics. Working capital was compressed. Companies ran with days of inventory rather than weeks.

**The reversal:** COVID and the subsequent supply chain crises (semiconductors, rare earths, pharmaceuticals) have permanently altered corporate risk management. The new strategic mantra is "just-in-case," not "just-in-time." Companies are building strategic buffer inventories, multiple supplier relationships, and geographic redundancy. All of this requires more working capital. More working capital means higher carrying costs — which is disinflationary for capital markets but inflationary for goods prices, because those inventory costs are ultimately passed through.

## 2. The Inflation Consequence — Structural, Not Cyclical

The standard policy debate treats inflation as cyclical: too much demand, central banks tighten, inflation falls. This framework has dominated macroeconomic thinking since Volcker broke the 1970s inflationary spiral.

But supply chain fragmentation introduces a **structural** inflationary component that interest rate increases cannot address:

**Structural inflation sources:**
1. Higher production costs from less efficient supply chains (10–20% premium, as above)
2. Labor market tightening in nearshoring destinations — Vietnam, Mexico, and India are all experiencing wage inflation
3. Energy cost increases from manufacturing in higher-energy-cost geographies
4. Industrial policy costs — subsidies, tariffs, and procurement mandates are all ultimately financed through taxes, borrowing, or higher prices
5. De-globalization premium on commodities: China's manufacturing scale suppressed demand for energy and materials per unit of output; less efficient supply chains use more energy and more raw materials to produce the same goods

**The quantitative estimate:** The IMF's 2023 research estimated that significant geoeconomic fragmentation could raise global consumer prices by 0.2–0.7 percentage points per year in the transition period, with more severe estimates from Oxford Economics and the World Bank ranging up to 1.5 percentage points in a deep fragmentation scenario.

A persistent 0.5–1.0% structural inflation premium changes the entire rate regime. If the neutral inflation rate is 2.5% rather than 2.0%, and if central banks maintain a real rate of +1.5%, then the neutral policy rate is 4.0–4.5% rather than 3.0–3.5%. This is not "tight" policy. It is the new normal. And every bond, equity, and real asset is priced on that difference.

## 3. The Productivity Consequence — The Solow Paradox, Reversed

The original "Solow paradox" (1987) observed that computers were everywhere except in the productivity statistics. The 1990s resolved it: IT investment eventually showed up as productivity growth. The productivity boom of 1995–2005 validated globalization and technology as mutually reinforcing forces.

Supply chain fragmentation creates an inverse Solow problem: **massive investment in manufacturing with productivity growth that lags the investment by a decade or more.**

The core mechanism:
1. Reshoring requires greenfield factories staffed with workers who have no prior experience in those industries
2. Learning curves for complex manufacturing (semiconductors, advanced electronics) take 10–15 years to flatten
3. Domestic supply chain ecosystems — the clusters of specialized suppliers, skilled workers, and knowledge that made China's manufacturing so efficient — require 20+ years to develop organically
4. Government industrial policy accelerates this, but cannot substitute for it

**The investment implication:** Countries and sectors that benefit from industrial policy in the near term will initially see revenue growth without proportional productivity gains. Margins will be compressed. This is already visible in the US semiconductor sector — CHIPS Act recipients are building capacity, but their per-wafer costs are substantially higher than Asian peers. The bet is that productivity catches up. That is a decade-long thesis, not a 12-month trade.

## 4. The Rate Regime — Why "Higher for Longer" is Structural, Not Cyclical

The core thesis:

> The three-decade globalization cycle suppressed inflation and enabled central banks to run persistently loose monetary policy. The 2010s zero-interest-rate environment was not an anomaly born purely of the GFC — it was the terminal expression of a deflationary globalization cycle. As that cycle reverses, the neutral interest rate rises.

The academic framing is the **r-star** (r*) debate — what is the "neutral" real interest rate that neither stimulates nor constrains the economy? In the 2010s, estimates of r* fell to near zero or negative. The Laubach-Williams model at the New York Fed estimated it at 0.5% in 2020.

Several structural forces are now pushing r* higher:
1. **Supply chain fragmentation** — as described: more capital required per unit of output, higher cost of goods, fiscal expansion to fund industrial policy
2. **Aging demographics reversing** in key economies (US, EU, Japan) — less global savings glut as aging populations draw down retirement savings rather than accumulating them
3. **Energy transition capex** — the global shift to renewables requires $4–5 trillion per year in investment through 2030. This competes for capital and pushes up the cost of it.
4. **Defense spending ramp** — European NATO commitments, US defense industrial base investment, Indo-Pacific security architecture: all represent large, persistent fiscal demands
5. **AI infrastructure build-out** — data center investment, grid capacity, cooling infrastructure: another multi-trillion capital cycle competing for resources

These forces compound. Each alone would modestly raise r*. Together, they represent a regime shift.

**The Warsh Fed context:** This is precisely why Fed Chair Warsh's posture at Jackson Hole (August 2026) — reluctance to signal rapid rate cuts despite moderating CPI — reflects not just hawkishness but a structural read on where neutral rates have settled. The market repeatedly prices in cuts that don't come because it is using a 2010s r* model in a post-fragmentation world.

## 5. Investment Implications — Building the Portfolio for a Higher-Rate World

The structural higher-rate thesis has direct implications for every asset class:

### Fixed Income
- Short-duration bias is structural, not tactical. Long-duration bonds (20–30 year) are priced with embedded assumptions about the terminal rate that are too low if r* has shifted up 100–150 bps
- TIPS (inflation-protected securities) outperform nominals in a persistent structural inflation environment
- EM bonds: higher US rates create headwinds for EM countries with dollar-denominated debt. The supply chain fragmentation thesis creates a two-tier EM: friendshoring beneficiaries (Mexico, Vietnam, India) face better macro; countries with no role in the new architecture face worse

### Equities
- Asset-heavy industrials and capital equipment companies benefit from the investment cycle
- Consumer staples face structurally higher COGS — companies without pricing power are structurally challenged
- Discount the 2010s "asset-light" business model premium: the model worked when capital was free and manufacturing was cheap. Both conditions are reversing
- Financial sector: higher for longer is a structural tailwind for banks (wider NIMs) but a headwind for private equity and leveraged buyouts

### Real Assets
- Commodities: manufacturing duplication is structurally more energy and materials intensive — long-duration bullish case for energy and industrial metals
- Infrastructure: industrial real estate, power generation, ports — direct beneficiaries of supply chain reconfiguration investment
- Gold: performs well when r* uncertainty is high and when fiscal credibility of major governments is questioned

### The Underowned Thesis
German and Japanese capital equipment exporters (Siemens, Fanuc, KION, Keyence) are underowned relative to their structural positioning. Every new factory in Vietnam, Mexico, or India requires tooling, automation, and manufacturing equipment — much of which is produced by a small group of specialized industrial companies in Germany and Japan. These companies have pricing power, deep technological moats, and are not well-covered by investors focused on US/China supply chain narratives.

## Key Concepts

- **Globalization dividend** — the three structural tailwinds (labor arbitrage, capital efficiency, inventory efficiency) that suppressed inflation and rates from 1990–2019
- **Structural vs. cyclical inflation** — the distinction central banks and markets often fail to make
- **r-star (r*)** — the neutral real interest rate; the parameter that explains whether "higher for longer" is policy or structure
- **Solow paradox (reversed)** — large capital investment in manufacturing with delayed productivity payoff
- **Industrial policy premium** — the cost of geopolitical resilience absorbed into fiscal deficits, subsidies, and ultimately prices
