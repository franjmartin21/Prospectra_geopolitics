## Core Concept: Fiscal Dominance

**Fiscal dominance** occurs when a government's fiscal trajectory becomes so severe that the central bank loses effective independence — it is forced to monetize debt (buy bonds) to keep borrowing costs manageable, even at the cost of inflation. The central bank's monetary mandate is subordinated to the government's fiscal survival.

This is not a theoretical construct. It is how most sovereign monetary systems operated before central bank independence was institutionalized in the 1990s. And it is where fiscal trajectories in the US, UK, Japan, and France are pointing if corrective action is not taken.

### The Mechanism: From Fiscal Stress to Monetary Dominance

The transmission from fiscal stress to monetary dominance operates through a specific sequence:

**Stage 1 — Primary Deficit Sustainability Loss**
A government runs a primary deficit (spending minus revenue before interest) that grows faster than the difference between its borrowing rate (r) and economic growth rate (g). When r > g persistently, debt-to-GDP rises without bound. The US crossed r > g on a structural basis in 2023 with the Warsh rate normalization.

**Stage 2 — Interest Expense Crowding Out**
As interest expense consumes an expanding share of the budget (currently ~18% of US federal revenue in 2026), the government faces a binary: cut discretionary spending dramatically (politically impossible), raise taxes dramatically (politically impossible), or keep borrowing and hope. It keeps borrowing.

**Stage 3 — Bond Vigilante Activation**
At some threshold of fiscal trajectory deterioration, the bond market begins to price a risk premium into long-dated sovereign debt that is not purely inflation-driven. The 10-year UST yield begins to move not because of rate expectations but because of term premium — compensation investors demand for holding long-dated paper from an issuer whose long-term fiscal trajectory is uncertain.

Term premium on US Treasuries crossed zero in 2022 (after being negative for a decade) and has been rising since. This is the early signal. It is not yet a credibility break. But the direction is established.

**Stage 4 — The Doom Loop**
Higher term premium → higher yields → higher interest expense → larger deficit → more bond issuance → higher term premium. This self-reinforcing dynamic is what the UK experienced in September 2022 (triggered by the Truss mini-budget) and what Japan is experiencing in slow motion with its JGB market.

Once the doom loop activates, the central bank faces an impossible choice:
- **Hold rates and credibility** → allow the doom loop to spiral; sovereign borrowing costs may reach an unsustainable level; potential for a genuine fiscal crisis
- **Intervene with QE** → cap yields by buying government bonds; accept the resulting inflation; sacrifice monetary credibility for fiscal survival

**Stage 5 — The Reserve Currency Paradox Inverts**
For a non-reserve currency issuer, Stage 4 produces a currency collapse and IMF bailout. For the US, the sequence is different — and more dangerous. The dollar's reserve status has two effects:
1. It allows the US to borrow at lower rates than any other country with equivalent fiscal fundamentals (the "exorbitant privilege" — coined by Valéry Giscard d'Estaing in 1965)
2. It means that when US credibility breaks, the *entire global monetary system* loses its anchor simultaneously

There is no external circuit breaker for a US fiscal credibility event. The IMF cannot bail out the US. There is no higher-order reserve currency to flee to. The only equilibrium is either: (a) fiscal adjustment restores credibility, or (b) inflation erodes the real value of the debt burden until debt-to-GDP stabilizes at a higher inflation level.

Option (b) is financial repression (Lesson 74) — the historical playbook. But financial repression works when the government has captive buyers (domestic pension funds, regulated banks forced to hold government bonds). In a globalized system with mobile capital and non-captive international bondholders, the repression strategy is much harder to execute cleanly.

## Historical Grounding

### Case 1 — The UK, September 2022: 48 Hours to Breaking Point

The Truss government announced unfunded tax cuts of £45B on September 23, 2022. The UK gilt market responded over 48 hours with a 150bps spike in 30-year yields — from 3.7% to 5.1%. Pension funds using liability-driven investment (LDI) strategies — which hold gilts as collateral for interest rate swaps — faced margin calls they could not meet. This created a forced gilt selling loop that threatened to collapse the UK pension system within days.

The Bank of England intervened on September 28 — buying gilts unconditionally, reversing its own QT program in real time. Truss resigned within weeks.

**Key investment lesson:** The break was not gradual. It was a 48-hour discontinuity. Positions designed to survive "gradual rate rises" were obliterated by a nonlinear event. The portfolio implication is not "hedge against rising yields" but "hedge against discontinuous yield events." These require options, not just duration shorts.

**The US analogy:** The LDI vulnerability exists in US defined-benefit pension funds and insurance companies that hold long-duration Treasuries as liability matching instruments. A 100–150bps spike in 30-year UST yields in a compressed timeframe would generate comparable margin call dynamics at a substantially larger scale.

### Case 2 — Japan 2022–2026: Slow-Motion Yield Curve Control Collapse

The Bank of Japan maintained Yield Curve Control (YCC) — capping 10-year JGB yields at 0.25% — for five years (2017–2022) as a tool to prevent yields rising on its 250%+ debt-to-GDP. When global yields rose in 2022, the BOJ had to buy unlimited JGBs to defend the cap, effectively financing Japan's deficit without limit.

By December 2022, the BOJ widened the cap to 0.5%. Then 1.0% (July 2023). Then abandoned YCC entirely (March 2024). The 10-year JGB yield has since risen from 0% to 1.7% (March 2026) to 2.4% (August 2026). The 30-year JGB crossed 3% in May 2026 — its highest level in modern Japanese financial history.

Japan has not experienced a fiscal crisis. But the trajectory is clarifying: the BOJ is losing the ability to suppress yields without printing money at a scale that further weakens the yen. The yen has fallen from 115 to 155 over three years as a direct consequence.

**The US parallel:** The Fed is not running YCC. But quantitative easing — buying Treasuries to keep yields below what the market would naturally set — is functionally similar. If the US fiscal trajectory deteriorates to the point where the bond market cannot absorb new issuance at current yields, the Fed faces the same impossible choice the BOJ faced: cap yields (and print money) or let rates rise (and risk a fiscal doom loop).

### Case 3 — The 1970s United States: Fiscal Dominance in a Reserve Currency

The Nixon shock (1971), the oil embargo (1973), and the stagflation of 1974–1980 represent the only prior episode where US fiscal and monetary credibility was genuinely in question. The dollar fell 25% against the DM and yen between 1971 and 1978. The US was forced to issue "Carter Bonds" — dollar bonds denominated in foreign currencies — to attract external buyers who refused dollar-denominated risk.

The resolution required Paul Volcker's deliberate recession — 20% federal funds rate in 1981 — which crushed inflation and restored monetary credibility at the cost of a deep recession. The dollar subsequently strengthened 60% between 1980 and 1985.

**The lesson:** Credibility can be restored, but restoration is economically painful and politically traumatic. The Volcker moment required a Fed Chair willing to break the economy to save monetary credibility. Kevin Warsh's hawkish posture in 2025–2026 is a soft echo of this commitment. Whether it is sufficient depends on fiscal trajectories the Fed does not control.

## Investment Implications

### The Base Case: Financial Repression, Not Sudden Break

The most likely resolution of US fiscal dominance is not a sudden gilt-crisis-style break. It is a slow-motion financial repression: the Fed allows inflation to run moderately above target (3–4%) for an extended period, suppressing real yields and allowing the debt/GDP ratio to stabilize through nominal GDP growth outpacing real debt growth.

This is exactly what the US did with the post-WWII debt burden (140% debt/GDP in 1946 → 40% by 1970) — not through rapid repayment but through financial repression, inflation, and growth over two decades.

**Portfolio implications of the financial repression base case:**
- Long duration Treasuries are wealth destruction in real terms → underweight
- TIPS (inflation-linked) outperform nominal bonds but still subject to fiscal risk
- Real assets (energy, commodities, real estate, infrastructure) preserve purchasing power
- Gold is the canonical financial repression hedge (it was precisely this dynamic that drove gold from $35 in 1971 to $800 in 1980)
- Equities outperform bonds but only in nominal terms; real return depends on whether corporate pricing power exceeds inflation

### The Tail Case: Discontinuous Credibility Break

The tail risk scenario is a nonlinear break — similar to the UK gilt crisis but at 10x the scale. This requires a triggering event: a failed Treasury auction, a credit rating action that forces institutional sellers, a geopolitical event that accelerates foreign reserve diversification away from dollars.

**Portfolio for the tail case:**
| Asset | Position | Rationale |
|-------|----------|-----------|
| Long-dated UST | Short / underweight | First-order victim of credibility break |
| Gold | Overweight | Non-dollar reserve; benefits from dollar credibility loss |
| Short USD (vs. CHF, gold, commodity baskets) | Tactical hedge | Milkshake inverts when Pillar 1 breaks |
| Short-dated T-bills | Prefer over long bonds | Duration risk is the problem; short end is less exposed |
| Energy commodities | Long | Dollar-priced; inflation hedge; real asset |
| US TIPS | Moderate long | Inflation protection but still subject to US fiscal risk |
| Bitcoin | Small satellite | Digital gold narrative strengthens in dollar credibility crises |

**Critical position sizing note:** The tail case is low probability but extremely high impact. Position sizing should reflect its tail nature — 5–10% portfolio allocation to hedges, not a portfolio reorientation around the tail. Overweighting the break scenario has destroyed returns for many "macro bears" over the past decade. The base case (financial repression) remains dominant.

### The Signal Framework: What to Watch

Five signals that the base case is transitioning toward the tail case:

1. **30-year UST term premium > 200bps** (currently ~90bps) — bond market demanding significant compensation for fiscal uncertainty
2. **Failed or tail-covered Treasury auction** — demand at auction is insufficient to absorb supply at current yields
3. **Fed reverse repo facility declining AND Treasury cash balance declining simultaneously** — systemic dollar funding stress, not just rate-driven
4. **Gold/USD correlation turns strongly positive** — gold and dollar rising together signals the milkshake is breaking (historically they move inversely)
5. **Foreign central bank UST holdings (TICS data) declining for 3+ consecutive months** — reserve managers reducing dollar exposure

None of these signals are flashing red as of August 2026. The 30-year term premium is elevated but not at crisis levels. Monitor monthly.
