## I. Why Advisory Boards Work — The Institutional Decision Architecture

To understand why advisory boards matter, understand what an institutional allocator is actually doing when they evaluate Prospectra.

The allocator is not only asking: "Is this methodology rigorous?" They are asking: "Can I justify this decision to my investment committee, my LP base, or my board if something goes wrong?"

This is the due diligence defense problem. A risk officer who recommends a $14,000/year subscription to a geopolitical intelligence service operated by a solo founder with no institutional affiliation, no third-party review, and no named advisors has nothing to point to if a Prospectra signal produces a wrong call that influences a portfolio decision. They relied on a newsletter. The political risk of that decision — within their firm — is non-trivial.

The same risk officer who recommends Prospectra with a methodology reviewed by a former head of macro research at a Tier 1 asset manager has a defense: they relied on a framework that has been independently validated by a practitioner of verifiable expertise. The signal was wrong, but the due diligence was appropriate. This is a fundamentally different decision for the allocator.

**The advisory board is not primarily an analytical asset. It is a due diligence defense mechanism for the buyer.** Every name on Prospectra's advisory board gives institutional buyers one more layer of accountability they can reference when recommending the service. Understanding this reframes everything: the advisory board is not about improving the methodology (though it might), it is about reducing the institutional buyer's decision risk.

### The Historical Case: How Research Services Have Used Advisory Boards

**MSCI Barra (1998–2010):** The factor model methodology that became the institutional standard was validated partly through an academic advisory board that included finance faculty from University of Chicago, MIT, and Stanford. The names did not change the model. They changed the model's perceived replicability — the sense that it had been stress-tested by people who had no commercial interest in its adoption.

**GDELT Project (2013):** Kalev Leetaru's credibility as GDELT's creator was substantially amplified by Georgetown University's institutional affiliation. Georgetown did not build GDELT; the affiliation provided a heuristic for institutional credibility that made GDELT a citable data source rather than a personal project.

**Geopolitical Futures (George Friedman, 2015–present):** George Friedman's founding of Geopolitical Futures leveraged his prior institutional role at Stratfor as a credibility foundation. When advisors and board members with State Department or intelligence community backgrounds joined the advisory structure, their names appeared prominently in institutional marketing — not because they wrote the analysis, but because they provided the institutional credibility transfer the buyer needed.

**The lesson for Prospectra:** Credibility in institutional intelligence markets is partly earned through demonstrated analytical track record (Lesson 325) and partly transferred through association with credentialed practitioners. The methodology document is the analytical foundation. The advisory board is the social-proof layer that institutional buyers can point to.

## II. Who Prospectra Needs — Defining the Ideal Advisory Board Profile

The advisory board should serve two distinct functions simultaneously:
1. **Analytical credibility** — someone who can genuinely evaluate the GRI methodology
2. **Commercial credibility** — someone whose name and title signals legitimacy to the institutional buyers Prospectra is targeting

These functions do not always reside in the same person. Prospectra should build a board of 3–5 advisors that covers both.

### Profile 1 — The Academic Economist or Political Scientist (Analytical credibility)

**Who:** An assistant or associate professor at a research university, specializing in political economy, international relations, or financial economics with a geopolitics focus. Not necessarily a full professor — an assistant professor who is actively publishing in the relevant literature and who takes an advisory relationship for the research access, the consulting exposure, and the network value.

**Why this profile:** Academic advisors provide citeability. A methodology document that can say "reviewed by [Dr. X], who has published peer-reviewed research on geopolitical risk transmission in commodity markets" carries a different institutional weight than an un-reviewed document. The academic does not need to be famous — they need to be verifiable, which any research university faculty member is.

**What Prospectra gets:** An analytically rigorous reviewer who may genuinely improve the GRI methodology (the second-order benefit), and whose name and affiliation can appear in the methodology document and institutional marketing.

**What they get:** Access to the GDELT + market data pipeline for their own research (this is valuable — most academics do not have data engineering infrastructure), a consulting engagement that diversifies their income, and co-authorship credit on the first Prospectra white paper if their contribution warrants it.

**Compensation:** No equity. A monthly retainer of $500–$800/month for 3–4 hours of advisory engagement per month, plus co-authorship credit on the white paper. At Scenario A MRR, this is affordable and the payback is immediate in terms of institutional credibility.

### Profile 2 — The Former Institutional Practitioner (Commercial credibility, buy-side)

**Who:** A former macro strategist, chief risk officer, or head of portfolio construction at an asset manager, hedge fund, or pension fund — someone who has personally made the decision to subscribe to geopolitical intelligence services and has sat across the table from the type of institutional buyers Prospectra is targeting.

**Why this profile:** This person's career has been the decision-maker that Prospectra is trying to persuade. Their advisory role communicates to current institutional buyers: "someone who has been in your seat found this credible enough to be associated with it." That is a uniquely powerful signal that no academic can provide.

**What they get:** Deal flow credibility (advising an emerging research platform is an interesting credential for a retired practitioner), a small income stream during what is often a career transition phase, and network value from the Prospectra relationship ecosystem.

**Compensation:** No equity. Monthly retainer of $500–$1,000/month for 3–4 hours/month. At Scenario B, this can increase to $1,500–$2,000/month as their commercial contribution is validated (introductions closed, conversions influenced).

**Where to find them:** LinkedIn search for "former [Chief Risk Officer / Head of Macro Research / Portfolio Manager]" at asset managers with $500M–$10B AUM — large enough to be credible, small enough that the person is not overcommitted to boards. Target people who are 2–4 years post their primary role and actively building a portfolio career.

### Profile 3 — The Geopolitical Intelligence Practitioner (Analytical credibility, practitioner side)

**Who:** A former Stratfor or Oxford Analytica analyst, a retired senior FSO (Foreign Service Officer), a former intelligence community analyst, or a practitioner geopolitical consultant who operates in the same market segment Prospectra is entering.

**Why this profile:** Institutional buyers who evaluate geopolitical intelligence services know the names of established players — Stratfor, Eurasia Group, Oxford Analytica, Control Risks. An advisor with a visible tenure at one of these organizations provides competitive credibility: "the methodology has been evaluated by someone who has worked at [established competitor] and found it worthy of advisory association."

**The tension with competitors:** An advisor from a direct competitor (Eurasia Group, Oxford Analytica) creates a conflict of interest and is unlikely to accept. Target practitioners from adjacent organizations or from organizations that operate in complementary rather than competing market segments.

**Compensation:** Same structure as Profile 2. No equity; monthly retainer.

### Profile 4 — The Databricks / Data Infrastructure Practitioner (Technical credibility, optional)

**Who:** A senior data engineer or architect with Databricks expertise, ideally someone who has built data pipelines at a financial services or research company. This profile is most relevant when Prospectra approaches institutional buyers who are themselves Databricks shops — the shared infrastructure stack is a credibility signal in that context.

**Compensation:** No equity; monthly retainer of $500/month for occasional technical review.

**When to add this profile:** After the Databricks platform reaches Phase 3 (packaged dashboards, shareable GRI outputs). At Scenario A, this profile is lower priority than Profiles 1–3.

## III. The Ask — How to Approach an Advisor

The advisory ask is one of the highest-leverage conversations Prospectra will have in Year 1. Done wrong, it is awkward, wastes the relationship, and produces a grudging "sure" that yields nothing. Done right, it produces genuine engagement and a long-term relationship that compounds.

### The structure of the approach

**Step 1 — The cold warm-up (not a cold pitch).** Do not approach with an advisory ask as the first contact. The first contact is a genuine, specific, non-commercial touchpoint: a note that references their work, a comment on their LinkedIn post, a response to their public writing. The goal is to be a person to them before you are a founder asking for something.

**Step 2 — The value-first offer.** Before asking for advisory commitment, offer something genuinely valuable with no strings attached. For Profile 1 (academic): "I've built a GDELT + market data pipeline in Databricks. I'd be glad to give you access for a research project — no advisory role, no strings. Here's what the pipeline produces." This is almost always taken. A researcher who receives data infrastructure access has an immediate incentive to remain in the relationship.

**Step 3 — The specific ask (after a genuine relationship exists).** The advisory ask is specific about the commitment: "I'd like to ask if you'd be open to a formal advisory relationship — 3–4 hours per month, reviewing the GRI methodology and quarterly signal outcomes, in exchange for a $600/month retainer and co-authorship credit on our first white paper. The commitment is month-to-month; either of us can end it with 30 days notice." 

The specificity is critical. An advisor who does not know what is being asked of them cannot say yes. An advisor who is asked for "general guidance" is being asked for an indefinite commitment with no defined scope — the rational response is reluctance.

**Step 4 — The documentation.** Every advisory relationship is governed by a simple letter agreement (not a full consulting contract): scope of engagement, monthly retainer, IP ownership (all work product from the advisory relationship is Prospectra's IP), and confidentiality clause. The letter is one page. It creates clarity and professionalism without bureaucratic overhead.

## IV. The Compensation Discipline — Why Not Equity

The most common mistake founders make with advisory boards is offering equity. The reasoning is intuitive: "We don't have cash; equity aligns incentives." Both parts of this are wrong in the Prospectra context.

**On cash:** At Scenario A MRR ($8,000/month), $600/month per advisor for three advisors is $1,800/month — 22.5% of MRR. This is high, but it is not prohibitive if the advisory relationships are producing commercial value (introductions, methodology credibility, institutional conversions). If the advisors are not producing commercial value, the solution is to end the advisory relationships — not to shift the cost to equity.

**On incentive alignment:** Equity aligns incentives over long timescales, but advisory boards operate on short timescales. An advisor who holds 0.5% equity has theoretical alignment but almost no practical incentive to actively promote Prospectra in the conversations that matter — because their equity has no liquidity and no near-term value. A cash retainer that is paid monthly creates a much more immediate incentive to deliver: an advisor who is not doing the work loses $600/month.

**On cap table protection:** The Prospectra cap table has a 10% option pool designed for employees who are making long-duration, full-time commitments. An advisor who contributes 3–4 hours per month and holds 0.5% equity draws from the same pool as the full-time analyst who eventually joins and makes a 4-year commitment. The dilution math does not justify advisory equity at the current stage.

**The exception:** A single advisory relationship that transitions into a part-time or full-time operating role — rare, but possible if an advisor becomes deeply embedded in the commercial or analytical work. In that case, the equity discussion happens at the point of the role transition, not at the advisory stage.

## V. Managing the Advisory Board — Without Becoming Dependent on It

The risk of an advisory board is not that it fails to deliver credibility — it is that the founder becomes psychologically dependent on the advisors' approval, outsources strategic decisions to the board, or invests disproportionate time in managing advisor relationships instead of doing the work.

### The three operating rules

**Rule 1 — Advisory relationships are inputs, not decisions.** Advisors provide perspective; the CEO makes decisions. An advisor who says "I would not price the institutional tier above $8,400/year" is providing a data point. The CEO prices the institutional tier at $12,000/year because the market testing (Lesson 333) supported it. The advisory board is a sounding board, not a governing body.

**Rule 2 — Quarterly cadence, structured agenda.** Each advisor receives a monthly check-in email (one paragraph: what is new at Prospectra, one specific question) and a quarterly 30-minute call with a structured agenda: GRI calibration update, signal outcomes review, one specific question requiring their expertise. The quarterly call is the primary engagement vehicle. Monthly check-ins keep the relationship warm but require no advisor time.

**Rule 3 — Manage toward outcomes, not inputs.** Define what each advisor is expected to produce in a 12-month period: one methodology review, two institutional introductions, one co-authorship contribution. Review this at the 6-month mark. If an advisor is not producing against these expectations, end the relationship professionally — "We've decided to restructure the advisory board as we move into the next phase" — and preserve the personal relationship without the formal engagement. An advisory relationship that is not producing value is a management cost and a credential that gradually loses credibility.

## VI. The Institutional Credibility Stack — The Full Picture

Advisory board completion represents the final layer of the institutional credibility stack. Prospectra's credibility architecture now has five layers:

| Layer | Component | Status |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Track record — auditable signal log with binary outcomes | Live (Lesson 325) |
| 2 | Methodology document — five-component GRI formula, falsification discipline, calibration history | Specified (Lesson 336) |
| 3 | Advisory board — three named practitioners who have reviewed the methodology | Building (this lesson) |
| 4 | White paper — public-facing methodology document, submitted to practitioner publications | Next 60 days (per Lesson 336) |
| 5 | Case study — one closed signal, traced from GRI spike to asset price impact, named and dated | To be developed from first fully-closed signal |

When all five layers are in place, the institutional sales conversation changes structurally. The CEO is no longer arguing for credibility; the credibility is documented and reviewable before the meeting. The conversation moves immediately to "how does this apply to our portfolio" — the highest-leverage conversation Prospectra can have with a prospective institutional subscriber.
