## Opening Question

*In Lessons 54, 55, and 56, we built a complete picture of the U.S. "economic siege architecture": CFIUS screening what enters, COINS Act restricting where U.S. capital goes, and BIS/FDPR controlling what technology leaves. We described these as tools. But here is the harder question: when you build a siege architecture comprehensive enough to cover capital, technology, and investment in every direction — what are you actually building?*

*A siege, by definition, is temporary. It ends with surrender, negotiation, or collapse. But what if neither side collapses? What if both sides, under the pressure of each other's restrictions, build parallel systems — parallel chip supply chains, parallel AI ecosystems, parallel capital networks, parallel standards bodies? Then the "siege" doesn't end with one side winning. It ends with two separate worlds.*

**Here is the question for this lesson: Are we witnessing a temporary conflict between two nations over technology supremacy — or are we witnessing the permanent fragmentation of the global technology order into distinct, partially non-interoperable blocs? And if the answer is the latter, what does that mean for every investment thesis you hold today?**

## I. What Is a Techno-Bloc? A Precise Definition

"Techno-bloc" is a term that has been used loosely in policy circles since roughly 2020. For our purposes, a techno-bloc has a precise definition — and the precision matters, because loose use of the term obscures which dimensions of fragmentation are real and which are still contested.

A **techno-bloc** is an integrated system of **five interlocking layers**:

| Layer | What It Means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Technology Supply Chain** | The hardware, components, and manufacturing processes that a country or alliance controls and produces domestically or within trusted partners | TSMC/Arizona/Japan fab cluster (U.S. bloc) vs. SMIC/Huawei Ascend/domestic EDA (China bloc) |
| **2. Capital Rules** | Investment flows — who can invest in whom, with what restrictions | COINS Act (U.S. bloc); alternative Gulf/BRI capital (China bloc) |
| **3. Standards** | Technical standards (protocols, interfaces, safety benchmarks) that define how systems interoperate — or don't | IEEE/3GPP standards (U.S. bloc); China's own AI governance framework, 5G Rel-18 counter-proposals |
| **4. Data Governance** | Where data can reside, who can access it, under what legal regime | EU GDPR + U.S. cloud act (Western bloc); China's Cybersecurity Law, Data Security Law, cross-border data prohibition |
| **5. Talent Flows** | Who can study, work, or collaborate on frontier technology | U.S. visa restrictions on Chinese nationals in sensitive tech fields; "U.S. persons" rule in export controls restricts Chinese fab employment |

A **techno-bloc** is not simply "not trading with each other." It is when all five layers become coherent and self-reinforcing within a group — when the supply chain, the capital, the standards, the data rules, and the talent flows are all oriented toward each other and away from the rival.

By this definition: a full techno-bloc has not yet formed — but the five layers are being built simultaneously, and rapidly.

## II. The U.S.-Led Bloc — "Pax Silica"

At the AI Impact Summit in New Delhi in early 2026, a new framework was formalized — unofficially called the **Pax Silica declaration**. The signatories: the United States, Australia, Japan, South Korea, the United Kingdom, India, Israel, Qatar, Greece, Singapore, and the United Arab Emirates.

The declaration articulated alignment on three dimensions:
1. Securing supply chains for AI infrastructure, semiconductors, and critical minerals
2. Harmonizing export control and outbound investment rules (aligning allied-country frameworks with U.S. COINS Act principles)
3. Building shared AI governance standards and safety benchmarks

**What the Pax Silica bloc actually controls:**

| Capability | Dominant Actors |
|---|---|
| Leading-edge chip fabrication | TSMC (Taiwan), Samsung (Korea), Intel Foundry (U.S.) |
| Chip design software (EDA) | Cadence, Synopsys (U.S.) — near-monopoly |
| Advanced semiconductor equipment | ASML (Netherlands), Applied Materials, Lam Research, KLA, Tokyo Electron |
| AI compute leadership | Nvidia, AMD (U.S.) — ~80%+ of advanced AI accelerators |
| Frontier AI models | OpenAI, Google DeepMind, Anthropic, Meta (U.S.) — all Tier 1 AI Diffusion Framework beneficiaries |
| Cloud infrastructure | AWS, Azure, Google Cloud — global hyperscaler dominance |
| Critical minerals processing | Australia (lithium, nickel), Canada (cobalt), nascent U.S./EU processing |

The critical observation: the U.S.-led bloc's chokepoint advantages are concentrated in **software and knowledge** (EDA tools, AI models, chip architectures) rather than physical materials. Software-based chokepoints are more durable in some ways (harder to replicate without the intellectual capital) and more fragile in others (can be circumvented once the knowledge is acquired).

**India's role is pivotal and relatively new.** India formally joined the Pax Silica framework in Q1 2026 — bringing semiconductor assembly/test capacity (a layer China dominates), critical mineral deposits (rare earths, thorium), and a massive AI talent base to the alliance. India's AI Governance Guidelines (2026), built on "democratization, scale, and inclusion," are being positioned as the Global South-compatible alternative to either U.S. hegemonic AI standards or Chinese surveillance-AI.

## III. The China-Centered Counter-Bloc

China's bloc-building is older and in some respects more deliberate — it has been building parallel systems since well before the U.S. began constructing its siege architecture.

**The five layers of China's counter-bloc:**

### Layer 1 — Supply Chain
China is pursuing a full-stack domestic semiconductor strategy:
- **Design**: HiSilicon (Huawei subsidiary), Cambricon, Biren — domestic chip design for AI and mobile
- **Fabrication**: SMIC (7nm demonstrated capability via DUV; road map to 5nm); CXMT for DRAM; YMTC for NAND
- **Equipment**: NAURA Technology, AMEC, Kingsemi — all receiving massive state subsidy, all still 2-3 technology generations behind U.S./Dutch equipment
- **Materials**: China's near-monopoly on rare earth processing is the single most powerful counterweight to U.S. equipment dominance

TrendForce projects **50%+ domestic AI chip market share in China by 2026** — not because Chinese chips match Nvidia's performance, but because the AI models being deployed for domestic applications don't require frontier compute.

### Layer 2 — Capital
- Belt and Road Initiative digital infrastructure: Huawei-built telecom networks across 140+ countries, creating Huawei-dependent infrastructure that creates future maintenance and integration dependencies
- **Alternative capital networks**: Gulf SWFs (ADIA, PIF, QIA) face no COINS Act restrictions and have accelerated Chinese strategic tech investment since 2023; BRICS cross-border investment frameworks (outside SWIFT infrastructure for portions of transactions)
- **State-backed VC**: Huawei's Hubble Technology Investment subsidiary has deployed >$100M into domestic semiconductor startups — a state-adjacent VC model that directly competes with U.S.-restricted private capital

### Layer 3 — Standards
This is where China has invested most aggressively in absolute terms:
- **5G/6G standards**: China leads in 3GPP standards submissions; Huawei holds more 5G essential patents than any other company (>15% of total). The 6G standards race (targeting deployment 2030+) is explicitly geopolitically contested
- **AI governance**: China published its own "AI Governance Framework" (2023) and "Global AI Governance Initiative" — an attempt to set international norms that embed Chinese preferences (state oversight of AI, content controls, data sovereignty)
- **Smart city/surveillance standards**: China exports surveillance AI (facial recognition, social credit systems, traffic monitoring) to 60+ countries through Huawei, Hikvision, Dahua — creating a "standards dependency" as those countries' infrastructure is built to Chinese interoperability specifications

### Layer 4 — Data Governance
China's data framework is the most domestically consolidated of any major economy:
- **Cybersecurity Law (2017)**, **Data Security Law (2021)**, **Personal Information Protection Law (2021)**: together constitute a comprehensive data governance regime requiring data localization, government access rights, and cross-border data transfer restrictions
- Foreign companies operating in China must store Chinese user data in China — and provide it to the government on demand
- The practical result: China's data ecosystem is largely isolated from the global web (Great Firewall), creating a self-referential AI training data pool that may be advantageous for Chinese language/context AI and disadvantageous for globally deployable models

### Layer 5 — Talent
- China graduates approximately **3x as many STEM PhDs as the U.S.** annually
- The U.S. "China Initiative" (FBI, halted 2022 but revived in modified form under NSPM-33) and visa restrictions have created uncertainty for Chinese-born researchers in U.S. universities
- China's "Thousand Talents Program" and successor programs actively recruit diaspora researchers back to China — sometimes offering 2-3x compensation packages
- The **U.S. persons rule** (export controls, Lesson 56) has expelled American and Taiwan-born engineers from Chinese semiconductor fabs — a forced talent transfer **away** from China's advanced semiconductor sector

## IV. The Middle-Power Problem: Who Refuses to Choose?

The most strategically interesting question in techno-bloc formation is not what the U.S. and China do — it's what everyone else does.

A full techno-bloc world requires the rest of the world to choose sides. But **many of the most consequential technology powers are actively refusing to choose** — and their refusal is shaping the architecture.

### The European Union

The EU occupies a fascinating position: technologically allied with the U.S. (exports control alignment, ASML/Netherlands embedded in U.S. framework, NATO security alignment), but commercially dependent on China (China is the EU's largest trading partner for goods), and **ideologically distinct from both** (EU AI Act is stricter than U.S. AI governance; EU data framework GDPR is incompatible with both U.S. cloud hyperscaler data practices AND Chinese data localization rules).

The EU is building a **third regulatory layer** that:
- Requires AI systems to be transparent and auditable (EU AI Act, 2024 enforcement)
- Restricts transfers of personal data to countries without "adequate" protections (GDPR — which currently covers neither China nor the U.S. fully)
- Funds domestic digital infrastructure through the European Chips Act (€43 billion through 2030)

The EU is not building a third techno-bloc — it lacks the semiconductor supply chain to do so. But it is building a **regulatory veto**: any company that wants EU market access must comply with EU standards, which creates a "Brussels Effect" that forces U.S. and Chinese firms to adapt to EU preferences. This is a form of techno-bloc leverage without a full techno-bloc.

**The EU-India trade deal** (concluded FTA January 2026) is geopolitically significant here: it creates a middle-power commercial axis (EU + India together are ~$3.5 trillion in GDP) that is aligned with the U.S.-led security framework but maintains commercial flexibility with China. This is **New Non-Alignment applied to technology** (connecting to Lesson 44).

### India

India's choice to join Pax Silica is notable — but India has not abandoned its historical non-alignment instincts entirely. India continues:
- Buying Russian oil (below the G7 price cap) — explicitly defying U.S. preferences
- Running parallel Huawei/non-Huawei 5G testing (though moving toward non-Huawei for core networks)
- Deploying Chinese solar equipment (Tier 1 crystalline silicon panels) in its massive renewable energy program
- Maintaining its own AI governance framework rather than adopting U.S. standards wholesale

India is **inside the Pax Silica framework for semiconductors and AI compute** while maintaining **hedged relationships with China on energy, materials, and consumer technology**. This is not contradiction — it is sophisticated multi-layer hedging. India is positioning to be the essential partner that both blocs need, maximizing its leverage by refusing to be fully captured by either.

### The Gulf States

The UAE and Saudi Arabia signed Pax Silica — but both are simultaneously:
- Allowing Huawei to build significant portions of their telecommunications infrastructure
- Deploying Chinese surveillance AI in smart city projects
- Negotiating massive AI data center investments from both U.S. hyperscalers (Microsoft $1.5B UAE, Google, Amazon) AND Chinese AI firms (ByteDance, Alibaba Cloud)
- Running sovereign wealth funds (ADIA, PIF, QIA) that invest in Chinese strategic sectors without COINS Act constraint

The Gulf position is the most explicit: **we will take the best technology from every bloc, we will host data centers for everyone, and our capital will go wherever returns are best**. Saudi Arabia's Vision 2030 explicitly requires advanced technology access regardless of geopolitical alignment. The Gulf is monetizing bloc competition rather than joining it.

## V. The Standards Battlefield — The Invisible Front

Of the five techno-bloc layers, **standards** are the least visible to investors and probably the most consequential over a 10-20 year horizon.

Technology standards define interoperability. If your country's AI system, telecommunications network, or financial infrastructure is built to Standard A, it is expensive and disruptive to switch to Standard B. Standards create **path dependency** — once adopted, they persist for decades. The country or alliance that sets the standard for an emerging technology has an enormous long-run advantage: it is essentially renting intellectual infrastructure to the rest of the world.

**Active standards battlefronts:**

### 5G/6G
- **3GPP** (the international body that sets mobile telecom standards) has become a geopolitical battleground. Chinese companies (Huawei, ZTE, OPPO) submit more proposals than any other country group; the U.S. response has been to organize allied-country coordinated submissions (through ATIS, ETSI, and Japan's ARIB)
- **6G**: both the U.S. and China have published 6G visions with incompatible architecture assumptions. If 6G forks into allied and Chinese specifications, every country will have to choose which backbone their mobile infrastructure is built on — and switching costs are enormous
- **Current reality**: Huawei has built 5G networks in ~170 countries. Even if these countries "choose" the U.S.-aligned 6G standard, replacing existing Huawei 5G infrastructure is a $200-400B estimated cost globally

### AI Governance Standards
- The **ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 42** subcommittee (international AI standards) has become a proxy for U.S.-China standards competition. China is by far the most active participant in terms of proposals submitted.
- The **EU AI Act** (2024) is the most legally binding AI governance framework in the world — it applies to any AI system sold in the EU, regardless of where it was developed. This creates a de facto global standard for any company that wants EU market access.
- **The three-way standards fragmentation**: U.S. (voluntary, industry-led, safety-focused), EU (mandatory, rights-based, risk-tiered), China (state-supervised, content-controlled, sovereignty-focused) — creates three distinct compliance regimes for AI systems

### Payment Infrastructure
- The U.S. dollar's role in international payments (Lesson 53) is under challenge from China's **CIPS** (Cross-Border Interbank Payment System) and **mBridge** (multi-CBDC platform involving China, UAE, Saudi Arabia, Hong Kong, Thailand)
- As of early 2026, mBridge has processed meaningful transaction volumes; it is not yet at a scale that displaces SWIFT, but it provides a parallel rail that doesn't pass through U.S.-controlled infrastructure — directly relevant to Lesson 29 (BRICS and De-Dollarization)

## VI. The Cambridge IO Thesis: Four Blocs by 2045?

A widely-cited 2026 paper in *International Organization* — "Digital Disintegration: Techno-Blocs and Strategic Sovereignty in the AI Era" — models the trajectory of techno-bloc formation and identifies **four distinct, partially non-interoperable technology spheres** potentially emerging by 2045:

1. **The U.S.-led allied bloc** (Pax Silica signatories + EU alignment): dominates frontier AI, chip design, semiconductor equipment, cloud infrastructure

2. **The China-centered bloc**: dominates in 5G infrastructure (installed base), surveillance AI, consumer hardware manufacturing, rare earth materials; increasingly self-sufficient in advanced chips through state investment

3. **The EU regulatory sphere** (not a supply chain bloc, but a rules-export regime): projects GDPR/AI Act compliance requirements onto any company wanting EU market access — exercising bloc-like leverage through market size without competing in hardware

4. **A Non-Aligned Technology Zone** (India, Indonesia, Brazil, Middle East, Africa): countries that deliberately maintain technology interoperability with multiple blocs, extracting maximum leverage from both sides; likely the largest zone by population

The paper's key insight: **techno-bloc membership is not binary**. A country can be in the U.S. bloc for semiconductor supply chain and in the Non-Aligned Zone for data governance and in the China-adjacent zone for telecommunications infrastructure. This multi-layer positioning is the norm, not the exception, for most of the world.

The investment implication of this multi-layer reality: **you cannot analyze techno-bloc exposure at the country level alone.** You need to analyze it at the layer level — which technology layer is this investment exposed to, and how bifurcated is that specific layer?

## VII. The Five Degrees of Bifurcation: A Practical Framework

Not all technology is bifurcating at the same speed. Here is a framework for assessing bifurcation risk by technology category:

| Technology | Bifurcation Status | Investor Implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Advanced AI chips** | HIGH — near-binary split. Nvidia/AMD (U.S. bloc); Huawei Ascend (China). Chinese domestic share rising fast. | Nvidia/AMD long-term structural buy on allied-market demand; China revenue = political option, not base case |
| **5G infrastructure** | HIGH (installed base) — de facto split. Huawei dominant in Global South; Ericsson/Nokia in Western/allied markets | Ericsson, Nokia structurally benefitted from Huawei exclusion in Western markets; no reversal |
| **Cloud infrastructure** | MODERATE-HIGH — bifurcating rapidly. AWS/Azure/Google dominant in Western/allied markets; Alibaba Cloud, Huawei Cloud dominant in China; battleground in Southeast Asia, Middle East | Hyperscalers in allied markets: structural moat. China cloud players: investable for non-COINS-restricted investors |
| **AI models (frontier)** | MODERATE — bifurcating but not binary. U.S. labs (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google) dominant globally; Chinese labs (Baidu ERNIE, ByteDance Doubao, DeepSeek) competitive for Chinese-language/domestic use; ECCN 4E091 restricts U.S. model distribution to China Tier 3 | Chinese labs' DeepSeek breakthrough shows AI capability can develop independently; U.S. model weight controls extend the lead but don't end competition |
| **Consumer electronics** | LOW — not bifurcating. Apple assembles in India/Vietnam; Samsung manufactures globally; Chinese brands (Xiaomi, Oppo, OnePlus) freely sell in most markets | Minimal direct bifurcation risk; supply chain shift to India/Vietnam is structural, but products still sold everywhere |
| **Semiconductor equipment** | HIGH — effectively bifurcated. ASML/AMAT/Lam/KLA: allied bloc only (FDPR binds globally). China developing NAURA/AMEC alternatives; 2-3 nodes behind | Equipment companies (allied) have a regulatory moat. The moat duration = how long it takes China to close the gap — 10-15 years for EUV; 3-5 years for some DUV tools |
| **Internet protocols / standards** | LOW-MODERATE — not yet bifurcated. TCP/IP, HTTP remain universal. But DNS, routing, content rules are fragmenting at the application layer | Not an immediate investment risk, but worth tracking: a DNS-level "splinternet" would force every internet-dependent company to build dual infrastructure |
| **Financial infrastructure** | MODERATE — bifurcating at the margin. SWIFT dominant; CIPS/mBridge growing. Dollar settlement still universal for commodity trade | Monitor mBridge transaction volumes as leading indicator of SWIFT bypass capacity (Lesson 29 / Lesson 53 connections) |
| **Electric vehicles / batteries** | LOW-MODERATE — China dominates components but products sell everywhere. BYD, CATL globally sold; subject to increasing Western tariffs | Tariff risk is bifurcation risk. Watch EU anti-EV tariffs (25% enacted 2024) as early indicator of product-level bifurcation |

## VIII. Investment Implications — Navigating the Techno-Bloc World

The techno-bloc framework changes how you think about **every** long-horizon technology investment. The key questions to ask for any technology company:

1. **Which layer(s) does this company operate in?** (supply chain, capital, standards, data, talent)
2. **How bifurcated is that layer today, and what is the trajectory?**
3. **Is this company inside a dominant bloc, straddling blocs, or outside both?**
4. **What is the company's China revenue exposure and how is it accounted for?**

### The "Allied Moat" Premium
Companies that are **inside the U.S.-led allied bloc and cannot be replicated outside it** have a structural competitive moat that is underpriced in most equity models. The FDPR and Entity List create a regulatory barrier to competition that is, in effect, a government-enforced oligopoly in certain equipment and software categories.

**Highest allied moat:** ASML (EUV lithography monopoly), Synopsys + Cadence (EDA duopoly), Nvidia (AI chip design), Arm Holdings (mobile chip architecture licensed globally — including China, creating a tension point)

**Important nuance on Arm**: Arm licenses its chip architecture to Chinese companies including Huawei (through an Arm China JV that has significant governance independence). This means Arm sits uncomfortably in the bifurcation: its architecture is the foundation of most of the world's mobile chips, including Chinese designs. If U.S. export controls ever extend to Arm architecture licenses, the disruption would be enormous — and it would force China to accelerate its RISC-V based alternatives.

### The RISC-V Signal
**RISC-V** is an open-source chip instruction set architecture — not owned by any company, not subject to U.S. export controls, and increasingly adopted by Chinese chip designers as insurance against Arm architecture restrictions. China's investment in RISC-V ecosystem (chip design, compilers, operating system support) is the single most important leading indicator of whether China can escape the Arm chokepoint.

**Investment signal:** Track Chinese RISC-V patent filings and product releases (Alibaba's T-Head processor line is the leading example). Rapid RISC-V adoption reduces China's Arm dependency — and reduces the Arm-based moat for Western chip designers.

### The Middle-Power Infrastructure Build
The Non-Aligned Technology Zone (India, Indonesia, Gulf, Southeast Asia) is the most underinvested region in the techno-bloc investment literature. These countries are receiving capital from both blocs as each side tries to build relationships and maintain access.

**Structural investment thesis:**
- **India AI/data center build-out**: India has full Tier 1 AI chip access (Pax Silica membership), massive talent base, English-language AI development advantage, and U.S. hyperscaler investment flowing in. Microsoft, Google, and Amazon are collectively investing $15B+ in Indian AI infrastructure in 2025-2026.
- **Gulf "Technology Switzerland" positioning**: UAE and Saudi Arabia are positioning as neutral technology hubs — Tier 1 AI chip access, Gulf capital, Chinese commercial relationships maintained. NEOM, Abu Dhabi's AI campus, and UAE's partnership with OpenAI/G42 are the infrastructure expressions of this.
- **Southeast Asia as Alternative Supply Chain**: Vietnam, Malaysia, Indonesia absorbing China+1 manufacturing. These are not bloc members but beneficiaries of bloc competition — attracting both U.S.-side FDI (Intel in Malaysia, Samsung in Vietnam) and Chinese FDI.

### The Standards Play: Long-Horizon, Hard to Trade
Standards competition is the longest-duration investment theme in the techno-bloc thesis — and the hardest to directly trade. But there are expressions:

- **Companies that SET standards** benefit when their standards win: Ericsson/Nokia (5G standards wins in Western markets = installed base moat), Qualcomm (CDMA/LTE patent royalties from the last cycle), ASML (de facto EUV standard)
- **Companies that COMPLY with standards** face compliance cost risk as standards fragment: any global software company (Microsoft, Google, Salesforce) now must manage EU AI Act, U.S. export controls, China data localization, and Indian data governance simultaneously — rising compliance costs that are a structural headwind
- **Standards infrastructure companies**: Intertek, Bureau Veritas, and other testing/certification companies benefit from standards fragmentation — more standards = more testing = more revenue

### The Bifurcation Losers: Straddling Companies
The riskiest positions in a techno-bloc world are companies that currently derive significant revenue from **both blocs** and whose products are in **highly bifurcated layers**:

- **TSMC**: produces chips for both Nvidia (U.S. bloc) and until recently for Huawei (China bloc). TSMC's Arizona buildout is explicitly bloc-alignment. TSMC's Taiwan operations must navigate increasingly strict rules on what they can produce for Chinese customers. TSMC's bifurcation risk is existential geopolitical risk (Taiwan) plus regulatory risk (FDPR pressure). Long-term, TSMC is the most important company in the global tech economy and has no clean substitute — but its Taiwan-based operations carry scenario risk that must be explicitly modeled. (Connects to Lesson 45: Taiwan: The $10 Trillion Chokepoint.)
- **ASML**: Dutch, inside the allied bloc for export control purposes, but dependent on Chinese rare-earth inputs for EUV systems. Rare-earth countermeasures (China's October 2025 rules) are a direct supply-chain attack on ASML. Long-term constructive on ASML; near-term, monitor rare-earth supply chain developments carefully.
- **Qualcomm**: still licenses its chipset architecture to Chinese smartphones (via a China-specific licensing arrangement that has survived multiple rounds of restrictions). Qualcomm's China revenue (~$8B annually) is its biggest risk — and the thing that makes it most vulnerable to policy shifts.

## IX. The CEO Portfolio Note — Techno-Bloc Positioning

The techno-bloc framework crystallizes into five portfolio positions for long-horizon investors:

1. **Long the "allied moat" layer** — ASML, KLA, Synopsys/Cadence (EDA), Nvidia. These companies have government-enforced competitive barriers that are not going away. Hold with patience; model China revenue as zero.

2. **Long the "Non-Aligned Infrastructure Build"** — India AI/data center infrastructure, Gulf technology real estate and hyperscaler hosting, Southeast Asian advanced manufacturing. These regions are receiving investment from both blocs simultaneously. The return profile compounds as techno-bloc competition intensifies.

3. **Short the "straddling" exposure** — Qualcomm China licensing revenue, any PE/VC fund with commingled U.S.-China exposure, any company with >20% China revenue in a highly bifurcated technology layer. These are positions whose optionality is being systematically priced out by policy.

4. **Watch RISC-V as a disruption signal** — If China's RISC-V ecosystem matures faster than expected (3-5 years vs. 7-10 years), it accelerates China's escape from the Arm chokepoint. This is bearish for Arm's long-run moat in China-adjacent markets and bullish for companies positioned around RISC-V tooling.

5. **Monitor mBridge transaction volumes as a structural signal** — The speed at which mBridge scales is the single best proxy for whether the financial infrastructure layer (Lesson 53) bifurcates before or after the technology supply chain layer. If financial infrastructure bifurcates, the capital layer follows — and the COINS Act framework faces enforcement problems.

## X. Databricks Angle

**Techno-bloc formation is trackable via public data signals across all five layers.**

| Signal | Data Source | What It Predicts |
|---|---|---|
| **Standards body participation counts** | ITU, 3GPP, IEEE submission databases (public) | Rate of U.S. vs. China standards proposals by technology area — predicts which standards future products will need to comply with |
| **5G vendor market share by country** | GSMA Intelligence (partial public access); press releases | Tracks Huawei vs. Ericsson/Nokia installed base expansion — proxy for which "standards layer" each country is locked into |
| **RISC-V ecosystem patent filings** | USPTO + CNIPA patent databases | Chinese RISC-V patent growth rate predicts timeline to Arm chokepoint escape — bears on allied moat duration |
| **Gulf/ASEAN AI data center investment announcements** | GDELT GKG theme `INVEST_TECH` + press release scraping | Tracks non-aligned zone technology infrastructure build-out — leading indicator of middle-power tech capacity |
| **China AI benchmark publications** | arXiv + Chinese Academy of Sciences preprints | Rate of Chinese AI capability improvement relative to U.S. frontier models — tracks whether ECCN 4E091 controls are slowing Chinese AI development |
| **mBridge transaction volume reporting** | BIS (Bank for International Settlements) quarterly reports | Direct measure of parallel payment infrastructure adoption — predicts financial infrastructure bifurcation timeline |
| **Entity List + COINS Act expansion announcements** | Federal Register RSS | Near-term market signal: Entity List additions negative for affected companies' Western partners; COINS Act expansion negative for commingled PE/VC exposure |

**Pipeline idea: Techno-Bloc Alignment Score (TBAS)**
For each country in the watchlist (20-30 target countries), compute a composite score measuring their position across the five techno-bloc layers:
- Telecom infrastructure vendor (Huawei vs. Ericsson/Nokia — 0 to 1)
- AI chip procurement (U.S.-bloc sourcing vs. Huawei Ascend — 0 to 1)
- Data governance framework alignment (GDPR-compatible vs. China-compatible vs. independent — categorical)
- Capital structure (% investment from U.S./EU vs. China-affiliated sources)
- Standards body alignment (participation in aligned vs. China-led initiatives)

The TBAS output predicts which countries are drifting toward which bloc — and creates a forward indicator for where investment restrictions, export control alignment, or geopolitical tensions are heading.
