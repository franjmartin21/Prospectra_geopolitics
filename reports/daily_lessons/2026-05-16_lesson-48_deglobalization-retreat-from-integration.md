## Opening Question

> *If globalization was the defining economic force of 1990–2020 — collapsing distances, suppressing inflation, and lifting billions out of poverty — what exactly breaks when it runs in reverse? And more precisely: is what we're experiencing now an actual reversal, or something more complex that most investors are misreading?*

Hold that question. The answer determines whether you're buying or selling the next decade.

---

## 1. The Globalization Peak and What It Actually Was

To understand deglobalization, you need to first understand what globalization *was* — structurally, not rhetorically.

**The globalization trade ratio:** From 1990 to 2008, global trade as a share of world GDP rose from ~39% to ~61%. This wasn't just "more trade." It was a fundamental restructuring of production: the emergence of vertically integrated global value chains (GVCs) where a single product (a car, a smartphone, a pharmaceutical) crossed borders 5–8 times before reaching a consumer. Nike designs in Oregon. Cuts fabric in Vietnam. Assembles in Bangladesh. Ships through Rotterdam. Sells in Munich.

**The three pillars that made it work:**
1. **Geopolitical peace dividend** — post-Cold War US hegemony made the seas safe, sanctions rare, and borders reliably open.
2. **Cheap energy** — low oil prices made long-distance shipping economically rational.
3. **China's WTO entry (2001)** — the single most deflationary event in modern economic history. ~800 million low-wage workers entered the global labor market simultaneously.

All three pillars are now structurally weakened. That is the thesis behind deglobalization — and it's more durable than a tariff cycle.

---

## 2. What Peaked When

The first break came in 2008. The Global Financial Crisis caused trade to collapse faster than GDP — it fell 12% in a single year. Trade recovered, but the *growth rate* of globalization never did. The ratio of trade to GDP has been essentially flat since 2010.

Key inflection points:
- **2008:** GFC — trade credit collapse exposed fragility of long chains
- **2012:** China labor cost inflation begins eroding the wage arbitrage
- **2018:** US-China trade war — first explicit tariff weaponization
- **2020:** COVID — supply chain "pandemic stress test" exposed single points of failure (semiconductors, PPE, pharmaceuticals)
- **2022:** Russia-Ukraine war — energy and food weaponization; sanctions regime escalation
- **2025:** Trump 2.0 tariff escalation — reciprocal tariffs, 145% China tariffs, global trade policy uncertainty spikes
- **2026:** WTO now projects goods trade growth of just 0.5% for 2026 (vs. ~4% historical average)

**The DHL Global Connectedness Tracker paradox (2025):** Despite narratives about deglobalization, goods trade and greenfield FDI crossed their *longest average distances on record* in 2025 — while the share occurring within regions fell to *new lows*. This is the key nuance: we're not seeing a clean reversal. We're seeing *restructuring*. Trade is reconfiguring around geopolitical blocs, not simply retreating.

---

## 3. The Three Deglobalization Dynamics (They Are Not the Same)

Investors routinely conflate three distinct processes. This is where mispricings emerge.

### 3a. Reshoring
Production physically returns to the home country. Driven by security mandates (chips, pharma, munitions), subsidies (US CHIPS Act, EU Net-Zero Industry Act, India PLI), and political pressure.

**Reality check:** True reshoring is expensive, slow, and structurally incomplete. The US semiconductor industry cannot replicate TSMC's workforce in 5 years regardless of subsidy levels. Reshoring announcements often lead years before actual production. The investment signal is *years-long capital expenditure* in defense-critical sectors — not immediate output.

### 3b. Nearshoring / Friendshoring
Production moves to geopolitically aligned, geographically proximate countries. Mexico absorbs US-China decoupling. Vietnam absorbs labor-intensive manufacturing. Morocco absorbs European supply chains.

**2026 data:** Deloitte projected 40% of US companies would relocate at least part of their supply chains to North America by 2026. This is real — Mexican exports to the US hit records in 2024. But note: Chinese companies have been the primary investors building factories in Mexico and Vietnam. The geopolitical logic of friendshoring is frequently being arbitraged by the very party it's designed to exclude.

### 3c. Trade Bloc Formation
The largest structural shift. Trade is not declining — it's *reorienting around blocs*. The US-led bloc (North America, Japan, Korea, Australia, Europe) and the China-led bloc (BRICS+, SCO members, Global South) are developing parallel supply chains, payment systems, and standards.

**2026 signal:** Canada — a G7 member — signed a deal with China in 2026 restoring Chinese EV imports in exchange for lower tariffs on Canadian canola. A G7 country breaking ranks with US trade policy is not a footnote. It's a preview of bloc fracturing under economic pressure.

## 4. The Inflationary Architecture

This is the investment-critical takeaway: **globalization was a 30-year deflationary shock.** Its reversal is a structural inflationary force.

The deglobalization inflation channel:
1. **Tariff pass-through** — higher input costs → higher consumer prices (US tariff revenue is effectively a consumption tax)
2. **Redundancy costs** — building dual supply chains means paying for capacity you don't use; this raises the cost base of global industry permanently
3. **Labor arbitrage erosion** — as cheap-labor advantages shrink (China costs up, Vietnam filling up), the deflationary pressure from labor cost differentials fades
4. **Energy re-routing** — Russian gas to Europe was geographically efficient; the replacement (US LNG to Europe) is more expensive and more carbon-intensive

IMF estimate: Full fragmentation into two trading blocs could reduce global GDP by 2–7% — permanently. For long-term investors, this is not a cyclical recession. It's a structural recalibration of global productive capacity.

## 5. Concrete Example: The Semiconductor Supply Chain (2020–2026)

Semiconductors are the clearest case study in deglobalization dynamics.

**The pre-2020 structure:** The chip industry was arguably the most globalized supply chain ever built. US chip design (Qualcomm, Nvidia, AMD) → TSMC fabrication (Taiwan) → ASML lithography equipment (Netherlands) → Samsung/SK Hynix memory (Korea) → Chinese assembly and packaging. Every node depended on every other across 4 continents.

**The fracture sequence:**
- 2020: Trump entity list hits Huawei; TSMC cuts off supply
- 2022: CHIPS Act ($52B) + Commerce Dept export controls on advanced chips to China
- 2023: Japan and Netherlands join US in restricting advanced chip equipment exports
- 2024: China counter-escalates with gallium and germanium export controls (critical chip inputs)
- 2025: China expands rare earth export restrictions in response to 145% tariffs
- 2026: TSMC Arizona fab operational but yields below Taiwan levels; costs ~50% higher per wafer

**Investment outcome:** US chip equipment companies (Applied Materials, Lam Research, KLA) face bifurcated markets — selling to two parallel ecosystems. Defense-adjacent chip companies (CACI, Booz Allen, L3Harris) benefit from domestic sourcing mandates. Nvidia faces a China revenue ceiling. TSMC faces the cost of operating in multiple political jurisdictions simultaneously.

The semiconductor case demonstrates the core dynamic: deglobalization doesn't destroy the industry, it *restructures its cost curve upward* and *creates winners and losers based on which bloc they serve.*

## 6. Investment Implications

### Winners in a Deglobalizing World

| Asset / Sector | Thesis | Timeframe |
|---|---|---|
| Defense & industrial manufacturers | Reshoring + military capex driving domestic production mandates | 5–10 years |
| Commodity producers in "neutral" geographies | Non-aligned exporters (Brazil, GCC, Indonesia) become swing suppliers serving both blocs | 3–7 years |
| Logistics & supply chain infrastructure | New trade routes (Mexico-US, Vietnam-EU, Morocco-EU) require new port, rail, and warehouse capacity | 3–8 years |
| Domestic-facing equities in large home markets | If trade shrinks, companies serving domestic demand have relative insulation | Ongoing |
| Inflation-linked bonds & real assets | Structural inflation from deglobalization supports TIPS, gold, real estate in supply-constrained markets | 5+ years |

### Losers in a Deglobalizing World

| Asset / Sector | Risk |
|---|---|
| Highly globalized consumer goods (luxury, fashion) | Cross-border tariff accumulation compresses margins |
| Emerging markets dependent on export-led growth | Vietnam, Bangladesh, Cambodia face trade route uncertainty |
| Global logistics incumbents (container shipping) | Volume slowdown + route restructuring hits utilization |
| EM debt in politically misaligned countries | Bloc alignment affects access to IMF, World Bank, dollar credit |
| Long-duration assets generally | Structural inflation lifts the discount rate on all long-duration valuations |

### The Neutral Zone — Where Geopolitics Creates Opportunity
Middle powers that can credibly serve both blocs — India, UAE, Saudi Arabia, Brazil, Turkey — become structurally important as trade intermediaries. They absorb Russian oil, refine it, and sell it to Europe. They take Chinese manufacturing, repackage it, and export to the US. This is not corruption of the system — it is a rational arbitrage of bloc friction. Long-term investment in middle-power infrastructure, financial services, and logistics reflects this structural opportunity.

## 7. Signal vs. Noise — How to Distinguish Real Deglobalization from Cyclical Noise

| Signal (Structural) | Noise (Cyclical) |
|---|---|
| Permanent capital expenditure in domestic production | Tariff-driven inventory front-running |
| Regulatory mandates for domestic sourcing (CHIPS Act, EU CBAM) | Political rhetoric without funding |
| Bloc-based payment systems (mBridge, CIPS expansion) | Bilateral FX swap arrangements |
| Companies restructuring legal entity footprint by bloc | Companies adjusting shipping routes temporarily |
| GVC participation ratios declining over 5+ years | Single-year trade volume dips |

The investor's discipline: only act on structural signals. Cyclical noise will trigger reactive trades that are almost always wrong from our long-horizon perspective.

## 8. Databricks Angle

**Deglobalization is highly measurable — and most of the data is free.**

### Datasets to build:
1. **UNCTAD Trade Data + World Bank GVC Participation Index** — quantify GVC participation by country over time; identify which economies are decoupling fastest
2. **GDELT Event Data** — filter for trade-related event types (sanctions, tariffs, bilateral agreements) by country-pair; build a trade friction index
3. **US Census Bureau / Eurostat trade statistics** — track import source diversification at the HS code level; measure "friendshoring shift" as share of imports from allied vs. non-allied countries
4. **FRED + BLS** — track US domestic manufacturing investment (CHIPS Act dollars) vs. import volumes by sector

### Pipeline idea:
**Geopolitical Trade Bloc Classifier** — assign every country-pair relationship a "bloc alignment score" (0 = neutral, +1 = US-aligned, -1 = China-aligned) updated quarterly based on UN voting patterns, trade agreements, and sanctions participation. Use this score to weight trade flow data, then detect when a country is "switching blocs" — a leading indicator for bilateral trade route changes. This would be a genuinely novel dataset.

**Feature engineering:**
- Trade route diversification index (Herfindahl of import sources by sector)
- Friendshoring momentum (rate of change in allied vs. non-allied import share)
- Reshoring capex signal (US manufacturing construction spending relative to GDP)

## 9. Key Concepts Summary

- **Deglobalization ≠ anti-trade** — it is a restructuring from efficiency-optimized GVCs to resilience-optimized regional/bloc-based supply chains
- **Three distinct dynamics** (reshoring, nearshoring, bloc formation) have different investment implications and timescales
- **Globalization was deflationary; its reversal is structurally inflationary** — this is the single most important macro implication
- **Middle powers win** from bloc friction as indispensable intermediaries
- **The DHL paradox** — trade distances are rising while regional shares fall; we are not deglobalizing uniformly, we are *reglobalizing around new axes*
- **The semiconductor sector** is the cleanest case study: a 6-year arc from integrated GVC to weaponized, bifurcated supply chain

## 10. Reflection Questions

1. **The Mexico arbitrage:** Chinese companies are heavily investing in Mexican manufacturing to access the US market under USMCA, effectively nullifying the geopolitical intent of US-China decoupling. 2. **The inflation trade:** Deglobalization is structurally inflationary, but central banks target inflation at ~2%. 3. **The neutral bloc:** India has masterfully positioned itself to benefit from both US and China — receiving US investment in semiconductors while maintaining Russian oil imports.
