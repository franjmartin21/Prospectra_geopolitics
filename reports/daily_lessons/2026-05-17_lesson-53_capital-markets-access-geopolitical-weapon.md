## Opening Question

*In February 2022, within 72 hours of Russia invading Ukraine, MSCI removed every Russian stock from its Emerging Markets Index. $150 billion in assets tracked to that index were forced sellers of securities that could no longer be sold. The Moscow Exchange was closed. Russian equities went to zero in Western portfolios — not because the companies were worthless, but because the plumbing that connects investors to markets was severed.*

Here is the question: **Who controls the plumbing?**

Not the companies. Not the governments. Not even the central banks. The entities that control access to global capital — the index providers, the correspondent banks, the exchanges, the clearinghouses — hold a kind of power that is rarely discussed, almost never elected, and entirely capable of making a country disappear from the financial map overnight.

This is what we call **capital markets access risk** — and in a world of intensifying great power competition, it is one of the most important and least-priced risks in every cross-border portfolio.

## I. The Infrastructure of Global Finance Is Not Neutral

Every previous lesson in this curriculum has circled the same insight from a different angle:

- **International institutions** (Lesson 51): The IMF, World Bank, and WTO are instruments of Western-led order, not neutral arbiters.
- **Credit rating agencies** (Lesson 52): Moody's, S&P, and Fitch operate with embedded Western assumptions and can trigger capital flight with a single letter change.

Now we go deeper. Below the level of institutions and agencies lies the **technical infrastructure of capital markets** — the pipes, switches, and directories that determine whether investors can physically access an asset. This infrastructure is:

1. **Heavily concentrated** in Western hands
2. **Increasingly weaponized** as traditional sanctions reach their limits
3. **Being duplicated** by non-Western actors who have watched Russia's exclusion and drawn their own conclusions

The key nodes are:
- **SWIFT** — the messaging system for cross-border bank transfers
- **CHIPS / Fedwire** — the US dollar clearing system that settles ~$1.8 trillion/day
- **CLS Bank** — the foreign exchange settlement system
- **MSCI / FTSE Russell / S&P Dow Jones** — the index providers whose decisions force $10+ trillion in passive capital to move
- **NYSE / Nasdaq** — the prestige listing venues that confer legitimacy and liquidity on foreign companies
- **DTCC / Euroclear / Clearstream** — the clearinghouses where securities actually settle
- **OFAC's SDN List** — the US sanctions authority that can render any entity untouchable in dollar markets

Control one node; inconvenience a counterparty. Control several nodes simultaneously; end their access to global capital. This is the power that the US and its allies demonstrated in 2022. It is the power that every major non-Western economy is now racing to work around.

## II. The Russian Case: Capital Markets Weaponization at Scale

The 2022 Russia sanctions were the most significant deployment of financial infrastructure as a weapon in history. The sequence matters:

**February 24, 2022:** Russia invades Ukraine.

**February 25–27:** UK, EU, US, Japan, Canada, Australia coordinate. The speed is unprecedented.

**February 26:** Seven Russian banks disconnected from SWIFT, including Sberbank and VTB — Russia's two largest. Not all banks (energy payments exempted initially to avoid a European gas crisis), but enough to cripple international transactions.

**February 28:** Central Bank of Russia's $640 billion in foreign exchange reserves frozen. A sovereign's reserves — held at the Fed, the ECB, the Bank of England, the BIS — suddenly inaccessible. This had never been done to a G20 economy. Ever.

**March 2:** MSCI announces Russia will be deleted from its Emerging Markets and Frontier Markets indices, effective March 9. Result: every EM fund tracking MSCI is forced to liquidate Russian exposure. Buyers effectively disappear. Russian equities trade at 99% discounts to NAV in London before being suspended entirely.

**March 3:** FTSE Russell follows. S&P DJI follows. Russia is erased from the indices that direct most of the world's passive investment capital.

The financial consequences were severe. But the geopolitical consequence was arguably more important: **the world saw that the West could use financial infrastructure to effectively excommunicate a major economy from the global capital system in 72 hours.**

China, India, Saudi Arabia, Brazil, Turkey — every major non-Western power processed this lesson immediately. The race to build alternative financial plumbing accelerated.

## III. The Chinese ADR Crisis: A Different Version of the Same Risk

The Russia case was adversarial — deliberate exclusion as punishment. But capital markets access risk can also be **involuntary** — when the two systems (US and Chinese) become incompatible, the companies caught between them suffer.

The Chinese ADR (American Depositary Receipt) crisis of 2021–2022 demonstrated this.

**Background:** For two decades, Chinese technology companies listed in New York. Alibaba, Tencent (Hong Kong-listed but also accessible via HK), Baidu, JD.com, Didi, Pinduoduo — collectively worth trillions of dollars at peak. American investors owned them via ADRs. The arrangement benefited both sides: Chinese companies got access to deep US capital markets; US investors got exposure to China's growth.

**The crackdown begins:** In 2021, the Chinese government launched a sweeping regulatory crackdown on its own technology sector — targeting Didi (ride-sharing), TAL Education (tutoring), Meituan (food delivery), and dozens of others. Didi went public in New York on June 30, 2021. Two days later, Chinese regulators banned it from app stores. The stock lost 80% of its value and was eventually delisted from NYSE.

**The US response:** The Holding Foreign Companies Accountable Act (HFCAA), signed in late 2020, required Chinese companies listed in the US to allow American regulators (PCAOB) to inspect their audit working papers. China's national security laws prohibited this. The standoff created a countdown: either audit access is granted or the companies are delisted from US exchanges.

By 2022, the SEC had published a list of 160+ Chinese companies facing potential forced delisting. The market capitalization at risk was over $1 trillion.

**The resolution (partial):** In August 2022, US and Chinese audit regulators reached a deal — PCAOB inspectors gained access to audit files in Hong Kong. The immediate delisting threat was suspended. But the underlying tension remains. The deal can be revoked. Chinese companies have accelerated secondary listings in Hong Kong as insurance.

**The investment signal:** Chinese tech stocks lost 60–80% of their value from peak to trough during this period — not primarily because of earnings deterioration, but because of **regulatory jurisdiction incompatibility**. The market was pricing in the risk that these companies might simply become uninvestable for US holders.

This is capital markets access risk in its peacetime form: not sanctions, but regulatory fragmentation that produces the same effective outcome.

## IV. Index Inclusion as Geopolitical Signal

Index inclusion — the decision by MSCI, FTSE Russell, or S&P to add a country's equity market to a benchmark index — is nominally a technical decision based on market accessibility, liquidity, and institutional quality. In practice, it functions as a geopolitical signal and a capital flows trigger.

**The MSCI China inclusion saga** illustrates this:

MSCI first proposed including Chinese A-shares (mainland China stocks, traded in Shanghai and Shenzhen) in its EM Index in 2013. It was rejected three consecutive times (2014, 2015, 2016) due to concerns about market accessibility, capital controls, and trading suspensions.

When MSCI finally approved limited inclusion in 2017 (implemented 2018), it triggered an estimated $20–35 billion in passive capital inflows into Chinese equities. The inclusion factor was initially just 5% of the stocks' actual weight — meaning MSCI was deliberately limiting exposure while testing the system. By 2019, it had increased to 20%. MSCI signaled it would increase further — and then paused, citing ongoing market accessibility concerns.

The Chinese government understood the game: index inclusion drives capital flows, capital flows provide political leverage, and the decision to include or exclude is made in New York, not Beijing. This is one reason China has invested heavily in building the **FTSE-Russell RAFI indices** (a rival system) and why it has been pushing for more Chinese assets to be included in international bond indices (Bloomberg Barclays Global Aggregate, JPMorgan GBI-EM).

**Saudi Arabia's Vision 2030 and MSCI EM inclusion (2019):** When Saudi Arabia was upgraded from Frontier to Emerging Market by MSCI in 2019, it was simultaneously a financial event (billions in passive inflows) and a geopolitical one — the Kingdom was signaling openness, the US was signaling Saudi Arabia's strategic legitimacy. The timing, 18 months after the murder of Jamal Khashoggi, was noticed.

**The emerging market graduation problem:** Index inclusion/exclusion can also punish success. When a country's economy grows large enough, it graduates from Emerging to Developed Market status. South Korea and Taiwan have been reclassified debates for years. The reclassification would trigger forced sales by EM-mandate funds and forced buys by DM-mandate funds — a multi-hundred-billion dollar capital rotation driven by a classification decision, not by any change in underlying value.

## V. SWIFT, CHIPS, and the Dollar as Chokepoint

Lesson 3 covered the Dollar System in full. But it's worth revisiting one specific mechanism through this lens: the US dollar's role in global settlements makes CHIPS and the Fedwire the most powerful financial chokepoints on earth.

**CHIPS (Clearing House Interbank Payments System)** settles ~95% of all dollar-denominated international transactions — approximately $1.8 trillion per day. Every correspondent banking relationship ultimately runs through CHIPS. To be cut off from CHIPS is to be cut off from the dollar system.

**Correspondent banking** is the mechanism by which smaller banks gain access to the global dollar system — by holding accounts at larger banks with direct Fed access. The threat of **derisking** — large US banks closing correspondent accounts with banks in high-risk jurisdictions — has become a major policy tool. It doesn't require Treasury action; private banks make these decisions based on compliance risk.

**The result:** Countries with significant numbers of correspondent banking relationships terminated (typically smaller economies, high-risk EM) face effective financial isolation even without formal sanctions. Afghanistan, Yemen, and several Caribbean nations have experienced near-complete correspondent banking collapses. This is not listed as a "sanction" anywhere. It happens quietly, through private-sector risk management, and it is nearly impossible to reverse.

The implications for portfolio construction: **dollar-denominated debt in countries with fragile correspondent banking networks carries more political risk than the yield spread implies.**

## VI. The Counter-Infrastructure: How the World Is Building Around Western Plumbing

Every major non-Western power has drawn the correct lesson from Russia's 2022 exclusion. The race to build alternative financial infrastructure is real, accelerating, and underpriced by most Western investors.

**CIPS (Cross-Border Interbank Payment System):** China's SWIFT alternative, launched in 2015. As of 2026, it has 1,000+ direct participants and 3,000+ indirect participants across 100+ countries. Transaction volumes are growing rapidly. It primarily handles RMB-denominated transactions — but that universe is expanding.

**mBridge:** A multi-central-bank digital currency (mCBDC) platform connecting China, Hong Kong, UAE, Thailand, and Saudi Arabia. Enables direct cross-border settlement between central banks without going through SWIFT or CHIPS. In pilot operation. The BIS participated in design (then exited amid Western political pressure). The prototype works.

**BRICS Cross-Border Payments:** The 2024 BRICS summit committed to building interoperable payment infrastructure among BRICS+ members. This is not yet operational at scale — but the political will is there.

**Russia's SPFS:** Russia's own SWIFT alternative, now handling ~80% of domestic interbank messaging. Limited international reach, but Russia has successfully survived SWIFT exclusion at a functioning level.

**Key insight:** None of these alternatives match SWIFT/CHIPS in scale, liquidity, or network effects — yet. But the trajectory is clear: the financial world is bifurcating. There will be a Western-dollar system and an emerging alternative system, with a set of countries and companies trying to operate in both simultaneously. **The switching costs and fragmentation premiums are not yet priced.**

## VII. Investment Implications

This lesson's framework generates a specific set of investable signals:

**1. Capital markets access risk premium — underpriced in most EM debt**

Sovereign bonds from countries with fragile correspondent banking relationships, high sanctions exposure, or poor index inclusion status trade with insufficient risk premium for the capital market access risk they carry. Nigeria, Pakistan, Egypt, and several frontier markets have all experienced capital flow disruptions that were not predicted by their credit ratings.

*Directional view:* Be more selective in EM hard-currency debt. Screen for correspondent banking fragility and sanction exposure, not just credit fundamentals.

**2. Chinese equity discount — structural, not temporary**

The ADR crisis and ongoing regulatory jurisdiction uncertainty have created a structural discount on Chinese equities for US-domiciled investors. This discount may be permanent — not because Chinese companies are less profitable, but because the legal and regulatory risk of owning them has changed. The optimal structure for US investors seeking China exposure may now be via Hong Kong-listed shares under local-law securities, not US-listed ADRs.

*Directional view:* If holding Chinese equity exposure, prefer H-shares (Hong Kong-listed) over US-listed ADRs. The delisting risk is non-zero and non-recoverable on short timeframes.

**3. Index inclusion as a leading indicator of capital flows**

When MSCI, FTSE, or Bloomberg index providers signal a pending inclusion decision, passive capital flows are highly predictable. The signal leaks before the formal announcement (index providers consult with market participants). Countries moving toward inclusion (Saudi Arabia in 2018, India scale-up over 2023–2025, potentially Indonesia or Vietnam) receive capital inflows independent of earnings fundamentals.

*Databricks application:* Track MSCI consultation documents, FTSE watchlist announcements, and Bloomberg index rebalancing calendars as leading indicators for EM equity and bond flows.

**4. Alternative financial infrastructure as a geopolitical signal**

Adoption of CIPS, mBridge, or BRICS payment systems by a country is a signal of strategic alignment away from the Western financial system. This has long-duration implications for that country's access to Western capital markets. The set of countries increasing CIPS usage (Russia, Iran, increasingly parts of the Gulf and Africa) is a leading indicator of future sanctions risk and capital market fragmentation.

*Directional view:* Countries deepening integration with alternative financial systems are pricing in a future where they may not need — or may not have — access to Western capital markets. Position in those countries accordingly.

**5. The golden share of financial plumbing operators**

MSCI, FTSE Russell (owned by London Stock Exchange Group), S&P Global, Moody's, ICE (NYSE parent), and CME Group are the private-sector operators of the Western financial system's chokepoints. In a fragmenting world, their pricing power over the Western segment of global capital markets does not diminish — it increases. Every financial institution that needs to comply with Western capital markets rules is a captive customer.

*Directional view:* Financial market infrastructure operators (MSCI, SPGI, ICE, CME, LSEG) are structurally advantaged in a deglobalization scenario. Add on weakness.

## VIII. Databricks Angle

**Dataset:** SWIFT monthly reporting on RMB internationalization, CIPS transaction volume, and cross-border payment infrastructure adoption. These are public reports published monthly/quarterly.

**Pipeline idea:** Build a **Capital Markets Access Index** for each country in your coverage universe:
- Input variables: MSCI/FTSE inclusion status, number of US-listed ADRs, SWIFT connectivity score, correspondent banking relationship count (BIS data), OFAC sanctions designations
- Output: A composite score representing how easily capital can enter/exit each country
- Use it as a risk overlay on EM equity and debt positions — countries with declining access scores should require higher yield premiums

**Feature engineering:** Track delta changes in CIPS transaction volumes and SWIFT usage by country. Rapid shifts (sudden CIPS adoption, sudden SWIFT volume decline) are leading indicators of financial system realignment — and should trigger review of any long positions in those markets.

## IX. Signal vs. Noise

**Signal:** The fragmentation of global financial infrastructure is a structural, multi-decade trend — not a cyclical blip. Every incident (Russia 2022, Chinese ADR crisis 2021-22, Iranian SWIFT exclusion 2012 and 2018) accelerates investment in alternatives. The endpoint is a world with two partially overlapping financial systems, higher transaction costs at the seams, and persistent capital market access risk for companies and sovereigns caught between them.

**Noise:** Individual announcements about CIPS membership or mBridge pilot transactions. The alternative systems are growing but not yet at scale. News about specific new joiners is directionally meaningful but not tactically tradeable.

## X. CEO Portfolio Note

The series of lessons on financial architecture — international institutions (L51), credit ratings (L52), and capital markets access (L53) — completes a conceptual framework that I want to make explicit:

**The Western-led financial order is not one thing. It is a stack.**

From top to bottom:
1. Dollar reserve currency status (Lesson 3)
2. International institutions as rule-setters (Lesson 51)
3. Credit rating agencies as gatekeepers (Lesson 52)
4. Index providers as capital flow directors
5. SWIFT/CHIPS as transaction infrastructure
6. Correspondent banking as last-mile access

China, Russia, and the BRICS coalition are attacking this stack from the bottom up — building alternatives at layers 4–6, which are more technical and less visible. This is a smarter strategy than attacking the dollar (layer 1) directly.

**Portfolio implication:** The biggest structural trade of the next decade may not be "sell US assets, buy EM" — it may be "short the seamlessness of global capital markets" — positioning for higher fragmentation premiums, wider sovereign spreads, and greater access risk in cross-border portfolios.

We are early on this trade. But we are not wrong about the direction.
