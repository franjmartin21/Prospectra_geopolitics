## Section 2: The Framework — Ceasefire Equilibria

L138 established that an attack on a sovereign-military target needs an escalation-ladder model, not an insurance-pricing model. This session extends that: once the shooting stops, the *type* of ceasefire determines which pricing model applies to the post-conflict period. There are three types, and they have different market implications.

---

### Type 1: Enforced Ceasefire

A third-party guarantor — UN peacekeeping force, a monitoring mission with real teeth, an Article 5 trigger from an alliance — imposes material costs on the party that breaks the agreement. The ceasefire's stability is a function of the guarantor's credibility and resources, not either combatant's preferences.

**Market implication:** Price out conflict premium relatively quickly once guarantor credibility is established. The residual premium should reflect only the probability that the guarantor fails, which is typically low and slow-moving.

**Historical example:** The Sinai MFO (Multinational Force and Observers) established after the 1979 Camp David Accords. US-led peacekeeping presence; Egypt and Israel have violated their arrangement essentially zero times militarily in 45 years. Markets priced Egyptian and Israeli sovereign risk without a sustained war premium against each other.

---

### Type 2: Mutually Hurting Stalemate (MHS) Ceasefire

No external enforcer. Both sides stop because continued fighting costs more than stopping — what William Zartman (1985) called the "ripe for resolution" moment. Stability is high as long as the cost structure holds, because neither side wants to restart. Breaks when one side's calculation changes — new government, new external patron, new weapon system.

**Market implication:** Price out conflict premium gradually, with a residual tail for the scenario where the cost structure shifts. The Iran-Iraq War ceasefire (1988) is canonical: eight years, 500,000–1M dead, UN Resolution 598 provided face-saving cover, but both sides stopped because they were genuinely exhausted. Markets priced Iranian and Iraqi sovereign risk with a peace premium — cautious, but not war-discounted — until the Kuwait invasion in 1990 reset everything.

---

### Type 3: Strategic Pause / Signaling Ceasefire

Neither side has been defeated, neither side has agreed to abandon the military option, and no external party enforces the agreement. Both sides stop because the pause is *useful to them* — to rest, rearm, extract a diplomatic concession, or send a message about their tolerance limits. The June MOU fits here: the US stopped striking Iranian military infrastructure, Iran stopped attacking Gulf shipping and US bases, but both retain full capacity and, demonstrably, willingness to resume. The ceasefire is a diplomatic envelope around an unresolved conflict, not a resolution of the conflict.

**Market implication:** Do not price out conflict premium fully. Price in a persistent "managed-conflict premium" — lower than acute-crisis pricing, but not zero, and not trending smoothly to zero. The residual premium is proportional to the frequency and severity of in-ceasefire violations. The June 28–29 exchange — two tanker strikes, an IRGC drone attack on the US Fifth Fleet's headquarters in Bahrain, and two rounds of US retaliatory strikes, all inside the MOU framework — is direct evidence of what Type 3 residual risk looks like in practice.

**The key investor error in Type 3 ceasefires:** treating the absence of active fighting as equivalent to conflict resolution. Markets made this error after the June 17 MOU signature — Brent fell from the $75–80 range to $72 in the week that followed. That repricing was not entirely wrong; it correctly updated on reduced conflict probability. But it was fast, pricing a Type 1 or Type 2 resolution when the evidence supported only Type 3. The subsequent June 28–29 exchange confirmed the Type 3 classification. The question is not whether conflict risk remains — it demonstrably does — but what the correct residual premium size is and how it should decay over time.

---

### The 20-Year Precedent: India-Pakistan Line of Control, 2003

In November 2003, India and Pakistan agreed to a ceasefire along the Line of Control in Kashmir. No enforcement mechanism. No third-party guarantor. Pure Type 3 — both sides chose to stop for strategic reasons (internal political constraints on both sides, pressure from Washington, and a calculation that the post-Kargil escalation cycle was costing both sides more than it was delivering).

The ceasefire technically persisted for years — but the UN Military Observer Group in India and Pakistan documented approximately 3,500 violations through 2019 before a major breakdown in cross-border fire in 2020. For markets, the lesson was not "the ceasefire held" or "the ceasefire broke" — it was that the equilibrium produced a *persistent floor of residual conflict premium* that showed up in:
- Indian and Pakistani sovereign CDS spreads, which never fully converged to pure fundamental credit risk
- Rupee volatility, which carried a permanent Kashmir-tail component 
- Insurance pricing for businesses operating in border industries and tourism
- Agricultural land values in Punjab

Not a war discount. Not a peace premium. A **managed-conflict premium** — persistent, slow-moving, and priced across multiple asset classes simultaneously rather than concentrated in any single one. This is the equilibrium state the Hormuz situation is currently entering.

---

### Fortna's Base Rates (2003): What the Literature Says

Virginia Fortna's "Scraps of Paper?" (International Organization, 2003) is the most rigorous empirical study of ceasefire durability. Key findings:
- **Type 3 ceasefires (no monitoring, no enforcement) break within 5 years at approximately 60% frequency.**
- Adding a monitoring mechanism — even without enforcement teeth — drops the break rate to approximately **30%.** Monitoring alone changes behavior because it raises the visibility of violations and creates reputational costs for the side that breaks first.
- The June MOU has **no monitoring mechanism.** The 60-day technical talks are negotiating *content* (enrichment, Hormuz tolls, Lebanon), not monitoring architecture. Until a monitoring mechanism exists, the base rate for the Hormuz ceasefire is 60% break within 5 years — not because Iran or the US is uniquely unreliable, but because that is the structural property of this class of agreement.

**Investment implication of the base rates:** the managed-conflict premium should decay toward zero only as monitoring architecture is established. It should not decay on the basis of time elapsed alone.

## Key Concepts Covered

- **Ceasefire equilibria taxonomy:** Type 1 (enforced / third-party guarantor), Type 2 (mutually hurting stalemate, no enforcer but genuine exhaustion), Type 3 (strategic pause / signaling ceasefire, no enforcer, unresolved underlying conflict) — and the different market pricing regime each implies
- **Fortna (2003) base rates:** Type 3 ceasefires break at ~60% within 5 years; monitoring alone (not enforcement) drops the rate to ~30%; the June MOU has no monitoring mechanism
- **India-Pakistan LoC 2003 as the 20-year precedent:** managed-conflict premium as the equilibrium state — persistent, multi-asset, not trending to zero without structural change
- **P01 kill switch definitional clarification:** "IRGC stand-down" = Type 2 or Type 1 resolution; a Type 3 strategic pause with in-ceasefire sovereignty-domain strikes does not qualify
