## 1. What Makes a Reserve Currency — The Requirements No One Talks About

The de-dollarization debate typically focuses on the wrong metrics: which country has the largest GDP (China), which currency is gaining SWIFT share (the yuan), which bloc is settling more trade in local currencies (BRICS). These are real trends. They are not, however, the determinants of reserve currency status.

A reserve currency is a currency in which other countries' **central banks, sovereign wealth funds, and commercial banks** choose to hold their **savings** — not their transactions. The distinction is critical. Transactions follow commerce. Reserves follow *trust*. These are fundamentally different decisions.

The requirements for reserve currency status have four components, derived from every reserve currency transition in history (sterling to dollar being the clearest template):

**Requirement 1 — Deep, liquid capital markets.** A reserve holder needs to be able to park $10 billion in a single trade without moving the market — and exit the next day if conditions change. The US Treasury market ($27 trillion, $700+ billion in daily turnover) is the only instrument on earth that can absorb this. Chinese government bonds, at $3–4 trillion in foreign holdings out of a roughly $24 trillion total market, are growing but remain structurally illiquid for foreign central banks that need instantaneous exit.

**Requirement 2 — Full capital account convertibility.** A reserve currency must be unconditionally exchangeable. There can be no circumstance in which a central bank holds yuan reserves and discovers it cannot convert them at the time of its choosing. This is not a procedural issue — it is the fundamental assurance that makes reserves "reserves" rather than term deposits. The US provides this guarantee absolutely: there are no circumstances under which the Federal Reserve or Treasury blocks a foreign central bank's conversion of dollar assets to other currencies. China does not provide this guarantee. The 2026 SAFE tightening is not a neutral data point — it is a direct signal about Beijing's willingness to maintain this requirement.

**Requirement 3 — Rule of law that protects asset holders independent of the political relationship.** Reserve holders must trust that their assets will not be confiscated if the political relationship sours. This is the lesson of the Russian $300B seizure — but note the direction: it was *Western* financial infrastructure that proved capable of confiscation, which is precisely why China accelerated CIPS. The structural question for the yuan is different: Chinese law allows the State Council to restrict capital movements under the Foreign Exchange Administration Regulations. No foreign central bank can fully trust that yuan-denominated assets are immune from PRC administrative action in a crisis.

**Requirement 4 — Network effects.** Currency use exhibits extreme network externalities. Because everyone prices oil in dollars, denominating oil contracts in dollars reduces transaction costs. Because trade finance is in dollars, corporate treasuries hold dollars for working capital. Because corporate treasuries hold dollars, banks maintain dollar liquidity. The network is self-reinforcing. Disrupting it requires a simultaneous, coordinated shift in *all* nodes of the network — not just a bilateral agreement between two countries.

**The verdict:** On all four requirements, the dollar leads by structural orders of magnitude — not by political preference. The yuan is advancing on requirement 1 (capital markets are deepening) and making marginal progress on requirement 4 (bilateral trade settlement networks). It is actively retreating on requirement 2 (capital controls are tightening) and making no credible progress on requirement 3.

## 2. Where the RMB Actually Stands — The Data Without the Narrative

Strip away the narrative and the data tells a more precise story.

**SWIFT payment share:** The RMB was the 4th most active currency for global payments by value in December 2024, with a 3.75% share — up from 2.1% in 2022. This sounds like progress. Context: the dollar's SWIFT payment share is approximately 47%. The euro: ~23%. The pound: ~7%. The yuan at 3.75% is not a reserve currency alternative — it is a trade settlement currency for China-adjacent commerce.

**CIPS vs. SWIFT:** China's CIPS processed RMB 175 trillion ($24 trillion equivalent) in cross-border transactions in 2024, up 43% year-on-year. It now reaches 1,791 institutions across 117 countries. This is genuine growth. The crucial caveat: **CIPS is a payment messaging system, not an alternative financial system**. The majority of CIPS transactions still clear through SWIFT for the non-yuan legs of transactions. CIPS is the yuan payment rail; it does not replace dollar clearing. A world that uses CIPS heavily is a world where yuan payments are efficient — it is not a world that has escaped dollar dependency.

**IMF SDR reserve basket:** The yuan was added to the SDR basket in 2016, initially at 10.92% weight, rising to 12.28% in 2022. Central banks now hold approximately 2.12% of global FX reserves in yuan — a real increase from essentially zero in 2015, but nowhere near the SDR weight, suggesting limited appetite beyond the SDR-driven minimum.

**The gold data — the real signal:** Global central bank gold reserves have risen from 13% of global reserves in 2017 to approximately 30% in 2025. This is the most important reserve trend of the decade, and it has nothing to do with yuan adoption. Central banks are not moving from dollars to yuan — they are moving from dollars toward **gold**. This is a vote of no confidence in *any* sovereign issuer's reserve management intentions, not an endorsement of China's alternative. Poland, Hungary, India, Turkey, China itself — all are buying gold at historic rates. The driver: the Russian asset seizure proved that any sovereign-issued reserve asset held in another country's financial system is subject to political risk. Gold is not.

**The 56.9% dollar share:** The dollar's reserve share has fallen below 57% for the first time since 1995 — genuinely significant. But interpret it correctly: the dollar lost share to a basket of smaller currencies and gold, not primarily to the yuan. The Fed's own research finds that most dollar reserve diversification since 2000 has occurred into the euro, the pound, the Australian dollar, the Canadian dollar, and the yen — not into BRICS currencies. The structural shift is toward *diversification across Western currencies* and gold, not toward the yuan.

## 3. The Capital Account Paradox — Beijing's Impossible Trinity

Here is the fundamental intellectual puzzle at the center of Chinese monetary strategy, which the research literature calls the "RMB paradox" or, more formally, the constraint of the Mundell-Fleming trilemma.

A country can have at most two of three things simultaneously:
1. **Free capital movement** (capital account openness)
2. **Fixed or managed exchange rate** (exchange rate stability)
3. **Independent monetary policy** (interest rates set by domestic conditions)

The dollar is reserve currency because the US chose (1) and (3) — completely free capital movement and completely independent monetary policy, at the cost of a floating exchange rate that markets determine. This works for a reserve currency because reserve holders need capital account openness (requirement 2 above) and don't care about day-to-day exchange rate stability if the long-run real value is maintained.

China has chosen (2) and (3) — managed exchange rate and independent monetary policy, at the cost of capital controls. This is a coherent policy for a developing economy building domestic capital markets while preventing destabilizing outflows. It is *incompatible* with reserve currency status.

**Why China will not liberalize its capital account — the honest analysis:**

Beijing's core political stability model rests on preventing capital flight. China's high-net-worth population, its corporations, and its local governments all face persistent incentives to move capital offshore: better returns, better legal protections, less political risk. The $50,000 individual annual quota is not an economic policy — it is a capital flight containment mechanism. Without it, estimates of the potential outflow scale range from $500 billion to multiple trillions over a 3-5 year period. The banking system, carrying roughly $5 trillion in problematic loans (IMF estimates), cannot absorb that outflow without systemic crisis.

The SAFE tightening on January 1, 2026 — requiring banks to maintain 10-year records of international transfers, tightening enforcement of the $50,000 individual quota, and adding additional approval requirements for international M&A — is not a policy reversal. It is a policy intensification. It signals that Beijing continues to prioritize domestic financial stability over RMB internationalization when the two conflict.

**The "partial internationalization" strategy:** What Beijing is actually pursuing is *selective* internationalization without capital account openness — building yuan usage in bilateral trade settlement while maintaining the ability to enforce capital controls domestically. This is a coherent strategy for reducing China's vulnerability to dollar weaponization (sanctions, asset seizures). It is not a strategy for achieving reserve currency status. Chinese scholars and PBC economists have written extensively about this distinction — acknowledging that China is pursuing "RMB internationalization as a defensive financial weapon, not as a bid for reserve currency hegemony."

## 4. The Petroyuan — What the Data Actually Shows

The petroyuan narrative gets disproportionate attention because energy currency invoicing feels decisive — the petrodollar was how the US secured dollar dominance post-Bretton Woods, so a "petroyuan" feels like a symmetric counter-move. The data is more complex.

**What is real:**
- Saudi Arabia effectively ended its exclusive dollar commitment in June 2024, building yuan settlement infrastructure through a $7 billion currency swap with China and participation in the mBridge platform.
- The mBridge platform — backed by the central banks of China, UAE, Saudi Arabia, Thailand, and Hong Kong — has processed over $55.5 billion in transactions, with the digital yuan accounting for 95% of volume.
- Iran's March 2026 Hormuz gambit — conditioning transit access on yuan settlement — is a real geopolitical move, though its practical effect on global currency invoicing is marginal given that Hormuz-disruption risk affects *all* buyers, not just dollar-settlement advocates.
- The UAE's May 1, 2026 departure from OPEC — the same week it negotiated US Treasury swap lines — is perhaps the most diagnostic data point of the year: the UAE is hedging *between* dollar and yuan structures, not choosing one over the other.

**What is noise:**
- "OPEC is moving to yuan pricing" — false. Among OPEC producers, analyst consensus is clear: appetite for yuan-denominated oil pricing is "almost nil." Saudi Arabia wants to maintain its strategic relationship with the US, which is predicated on petrodollar recycling into US Treasuries and US security guarantees. The yuan-for-crude architecture exists for *marginal* volumes and specific politically sensitive flows (Iran, Russia, China's strategic reserve purchases) — not for the global oil benchmark.
- "mBridge replaces SWIFT" — false. mBridge processes $55.5 billion; SWIFT processes approximately $5 trillion per day. The platforms are not comparable in scale or function.
- "The petrodollar is ending" — misleading. The petrodollar system is evolving, not ending. The evolution is toward a *multi-currency* energy settlement system where the dollar remains the dominant pricing benchmark even if settlement in other currencies becomes more common. Price-setting and settlement are different functions, and the dollar's grip on price-setting is far more durable than its grip on every bilateral payment.

**The CEO's read on petroyuan:** It is a real, structural, growing phenomenon — from essentially zero in 2015 to perhaps 8–10% of Chinese crude import settlement today. It is not a dollar replacement. It is a Chinese energy security tool, specifically designed to ensure China can import energy even if the dollar financial system imposes sanctions. Over a 10–20 year horizon, as Chinese crude import volumes grow and Middle East producers diversify their political relationships, yuan settlement share will grow. But the dollar's role as the global energy pricing benchmark — WTI, Brent — is not under serious pressure within any investable horizon.

## 5. The Dollar's Actual Trajectory — The Honest Assessment

The dollar is not losing reserve status. It is losing *reserve share*. These are different.

**The structural supports for dollar dominance — what actually matters:**

1. **US capital markets depth:** No alternative. The US equity market ($45+ trillion) and Treasury market ($27 trillion) are the world's deepest liquid stores of value. China's bond market, despite growing, remains partially closed and has demonstrated capital control risk.

2. **Rule of law:** US courts enforce contracts against the US government. There is no comparable assurance in any other major financial center. Singapore comes closest for commercial law; no one comes close for sovereign asset protection.

3. **Military backstop:** Reserve currencies in history have always been backed by the dominant military power. The dollar is no exception. The US remains the world's only genuinely global military power — the only country capable of projecting force in every ocean simultaneously.

4. **The invoice-to-settle-to-reserve chain:** Commodities priced in dollars → companies need dollars to buy commodities → companies hold dollar working capital → banks hold dollar reserves to serve clients → central banks hold dollar reserves to manage currency risk. Breaking this chain requires breaking *all* links simultaneously. No credible challenger has the economic and institutional infrastructure to offer an alternative at each link.

**The structural headwinds for dollar dominance — what is real:**

1. **US fiscal trajectory.** Federal debt-to-GDP above 120% and rising. Structural deficits embedded in mandatory spending and interest costs. The risk is not dollar replacement — it is gradual *duration risk* repricing of US Treasuries. Foreign reserve holders are slowly shortening duration: holding more T-bills, fewer 30-year bonds. This is not a vote to exit the dollar; it is a vote against taking duration risk in the dollar's fiscal trajectory.

2. **Weaponization risk.** The Russian asset seizure established that dollar reserve assets are conditionally confiscable. This is now a first-order risk that every foreign reserve manager prices. The response — gold accumulation, CIPS development, bilateral swap lines — represents *hedging*, not replacement. But hedging reduces demand at the margin, and marginal demand matters when it compounds over years.

3. **The debt ceiling as a policy risk.** Each debt ceiling crisis — particularly the 2025 episode that briefly raised technical default questions — undermines the "risk-free" framing of US Treasuries. Foreign central banks are watching US institutional dysfunction with increasing discomfort. The US political system's willingness to routinely threaten default on the world's reserve asset is a structural negative with no analog in Swiss franc, euro, or gold reserve management.

**The 10-year scenario:** The dollar's reserve share continues declining toward 50–52% by 2030–2032. Gold's share continues rising. The yuan's share reaches perhaps 5–6% — significant growth in absolute terms but still a distant second. The system evolves toward *dollar multipolarity* rather than dollar replacement — a world where the dollar remains dominant but no longer unchallengeable. This is the investment-relevant scenario, and it is not a crisis. It is a slow structural shift that produces identifiable investment signals.

**Specific positions the CEO maintains conviction on:**

**Long gold (structural, 5–10 yr):** Central bank gold buying is structural, not cyclical. The Russian seizure created an irreversible repricing of sovereign-held dollar assets. Central banks will continue accumulating gold at 800–1,000 tonnes/year. Gold is the *only* reserve asset that is: (a) no one's liability, (b) outside any financial system, (c) internationally liquid. At $3,200–3,400/oz, gold is pricing significant reserve demand. This does not make it overvalued — it makes it fairly valued for a structural 10-year accumulation trend.

**Short-duration US Treasuries over long-duration (tactical, 1–3 yr):** Foreign reserve managers shortening duration is a documented trend. Adding to a 30-year Treasury when foreign demand is structurally declining is a crowded long-duration risk. The Fed's eventual QT completion + continued fiscal deficits = structurally higher term premium on long-duration.

**India local currency bonds (structural, 3–5 yr):** Already the CEO's flagship EM call (see Lesson 60). The de-dollarization environment *enhances* this thesis: as reserve managers diversify, they need geopolitically accessible, institutionally credible EM fixed income. INR-denominated bonds are exactly this. India's inclusion in JP Morgan's EM bond index (since June 2024) is the structural catalyst for this flow — it is mechanical, not discretionary.

**Against the yuan as reserve asset:** Do not build portfolios around yuan reserve share growing to a global stabilizer level within an investable horizon. The capital account paradox makes this structurally impossible without a Chinese political economy transformation that has no observable momentum. The yuan is a trade settlement currency for China-adjacent commerce; size positions accordingly.

## 7. The Signal vs. Noise Framework for Dollar Dominance

Use this to evaluate each incoming data point on de-dollarization:

**Signal (warrants repositioning):**
- Dollar's SWIFT share falls below 40% (currently ~47%) — sustained, not monthly
- IMF formally revises SDR basket to give yuan >15% weight
- US Treasury market: foreign central bank holdings fall below 20% of total outstanding (currently ~30%)
- A second major allied-country asset seizure — i.e., a precedent beyond Russia
- China liberalizes capital account: removes individual FX quota or allows full convertibility (the opposite of the 2026 direction)

**Noise (do not react):**
- Any bilateral oil settlement in yuan — marginal, not structural
- BRICS announce new payment system — wait to see volumes
- Any individual country reduces dollar reserve allocation — look at the aggregate
- Headlines about "dollar collapse" or "petroyuan revolution" — always ahead of data

**CEO Portfolio Note:** The de-dollarization thesis is real but dramatically over-hyped in its near-term investment implications. The dollar is losing share slowly, gold is the primary beneficiary, and the yuan is gaining transaction use without gaining reserve status. Build portfolios for *gradual multipolarity*, not for dollar replacement. The asymmetric risk to monitor is US fiscal dysfunction — not Chinese monetary ambition. The former is self-inflicted and harder to reverse; the latter is structurally constrained by China's own capital control priorities.

## 9. Reflection Questions

1. **The Capital Account Decision.** China's leadership clearly understands the Mundell-Fleming trilemma — the PBC's economists are sophisticated and have written extensively about it. If they know that capital account openness is required for reserve currency status, and they are *tightening* controls in 2026, they have made an explicit choice. What does that choice reveal about Beijing's actual strategic objective? Is the goal reserve currency status — or is the goal something more limited and more achievable: reducing China's vulnerability to dollar weaponization while maintaining domestic financial control? And what is the investment implication of each answer?

2. **The Gold Signal.** Central bank gold purchases have risen from 100–200 tonnes/year (2000–2010) to 800–1,000 tonnes/year (2022–2025). This is the most dramatic structural shift in reserve management in 50 years. But gold produces no yield, has no central bank that defends its price, and cannot be used to settle trade at scale. Why are central banks willing to pay these opportunity costs — and what would make the gold accumulation rate reverse? Build the falsification condition: at what central bank gold share (currently ~30% of global reserves) does the gold accumulation trend become self-limiting, and why?

3. **The mBridge Scenario.** The mBridge platform — digital yuan interoperability with Gulf central banks — has processed $55.5 billion and is growing. It is architecturally distinct from SWIFT: it uses central bank digital currencies (CBDCs) rather than correspondent banking, which means it *does not require SWIFT connectivity* to clear. If mBridge scales to $5 trillion/day — the scale of SWIFT — it would represent a genuinely alternative global payment infrastructure. What would need to happen for that scaling to occur? Map the bottlenecks: (a) technical scalability of the CBDC architecture, (b) geopolitical participation (which countries would join?), (c) liquidity in yuan-denominated markets to make settlement practical. Which bottleneck is most binding — and what is your probabilistic timeline for mBridge reaching $1 trillion/day?
