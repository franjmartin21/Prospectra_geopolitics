## Part I: The Grammar of Currency as Power

### 1.1 Why Exchange Rates Are Political

Most financial analysis treats exchange rates as the output of macroeconomic variables: interest rate differentials, inflation, current account balances, purchasing power parity. This is correct in the long run. But in the medium run — the 6-to-36-month horizon where geopolitical investment opportunities live — exchange rates are frequently determined by **political decisions, not economic equilibrium**.

Three channels connect currency to power:

**Channel 1: Competitive Devaluation (the "Beggar-Thy-Neighbor" Channel)**
A country weakens its currency to make its exports cheaper and imports more expensive. This transfers demand from trading partners to itself. The recipient countries lose jobs and industrial capacity. If every country retaliates by devaluing, you get a currency war where all countries lose — the classic 1930s dynamic that contributed to the Great Depression.

The modern version is subtler. China does not announce devaluations; it manages the daily fix for the yuan within a trading band and maintains extensive capital controls. The effect is similar — a structurally undervalued yuan that provides a persistent manufacturing subsidy — but it is deniable. The US Treasury's "currency manipulator" designation is the diplomatic instrument for calling this out, but enforcement is weak.

**Channel 2: Dollar Weaponization (the "Sanctions Plus" Channel)**
Because the dollar is the dominant global reserve and trade settlement currency, the US controls access to the global financial plumbing. Any transaction that clears through a US correspondent bank — even a trade between two non-US entities — falls under US jurisdiction. SWIFT (the interbank messaging system) connects 11,000+ banks across 200 countries. When the US pressures SWIFT to disconnect a country — as it did with Iran in 2012 and Russia in 2022 — that country loses the ability to conduct international commerce in dollars.

This is not just an economic sanction. It is the weaponization of the currency itself. The dollar becomes a weapon precisely because of its ubiquity.

**Channel 3: Reserve Currency Architecture (the "Structural Power" Channel)**
The country whose currency is the global reserve currency enjoys what Valéry Giscard d'Estaing called an *exorbitant privilege*: it can borrow in its own currency, run persistent current account deficits without facing a balance-of-payments crisis, and finance its military and foreign policy at lower rates than any other nation. The US has exploited this since Bretton Woods.

But structural power also creates structural vulnerability: if the reserve currency loses credibility — through excessive money printing, weaponization of the financial system, or geopolitical overreach — other countries have an incentive to build alternatives. This is the core dynamic of dedollarization.

## Part II: Five Historical Cases That Built the Framework

### Case 1: The Plaza Accord (1985) — Coercing an Ally

In the early 1980s, the strong dollar (driven by Reaganomics: tax cuts + tight Fed policy) devastated US manufacturers and created a massive current account deficit, primarily with Japan. The US political response was protectionist — Congress was threatening tariffs. The Reagan administration chose a different instrument.

In September 1985, the finance ministers of the G5 (US, UK, France, West Germany, Japan) met at the Plaza Hotel in New York and issued a coordinated statement: the dollar was overvalued and needed to decline. Central banks would intervene to achieve this. Between 1985 and 1987, the dollar fell roughly 50% against the yen.

**What this was:** The US using its G5 diplomatic leverage to force currency appreciation on Japan — its largest trading partner and security dependent. Japan had no real choice. It relied on the US security umbrella for defense against the USSR and China. Refusing would have risked trade war or security abandonment. The currency accord was economic coercion dressed as multilateral cooperation.

**The outcome:** The strong yen contributed to Japan's asset bubble of the late 1980s (cheap credit as the Bank of Japan loosened to offset export losses) and its eventual crash in 1990. The "Lost Decade" has direct roots in the Plaza Accord. This is what happens when a country loses control of its monetary policy to a geopolitical patron.

**Investment lesson:** Exchange rate coercion on allies can create asset bubbles in the target country. When political pressure forces abnormal monetary accommodation, asset prices decouple from fundamentals.

### Case 2: The Asian Financial Crisis (1997–98) — Vulnerability of Dollar Pegs

Several Asian economies — Thailand, Indonesia, South Korea, Malaysia — had pegged their currencies to the dollar throughout the 1990s. This attracted capital inflows (investors got dollar returns with EM growth exposure) and kept inflation low. But it created a structural vulnerability: their **external debt was in dollars, their revenues were in local currency**. When the dollar strengthened in the mid-1990s, their export competitiveness eroded. When speculative pressure hit, they couldn't defend the peg.

In July 1997, the Thai baht collapsed after the government burned through its reserves defending the peg. Contagion spread to Indonesia, Malaysia, South Korea, and the Philippines within months. The IMF arrived with bailout packages — but attached stringent conditionality: interest rate hikes, fiscal austerity, structural reforms. In Indonesia, IMF conditionality contributed to political instability and ultimately Suharto's fall.

**What this was:** Currency pegs to the dollar created a hidden geopolitical dependency. Countries that surrendered monetary sovereignty to the dollar system were exposed to dollar-driven external shocks — and then to IMF conditionality (effectively US-directed financial governance) when those shocks hit.

**Investment lesson:** EM currency pegs are geopolitical risk concentrators. When they break, the sequence is: currency collapse → banking crisis → sovereign debt crisis → political instability. The asset class order of impact is FX first, equities second, bonds third, political risk fourth. Recognizing peg vulnerability before the break is the alpha opportunity.

### Case 3: China's Yuan Strategy (2005–present) — Managed Undervaluation as Industrial Policy

China maintained a strict dollar peg from 1994 to 2005 at approximately 8.28 yuan per dollar. During this period, China accumulated the world's largest foreign exchange reserves — over $4 trillion at peak — and built the world's largest manufacturing export sector. The undervalued yuan was a continuous subsidy to Chinese exporters and a continuous tax on Chinese consumers.

In 2005, under US pressure, China moved to a "managed float" — allowing gradual, controlled appreciation. But the management was asymmetric: the People's Bank of China (PBOC) prevented appreciation that would hurt exporters while occasionally allowing modest moves to deflect political pressure. The yuan appreciated from 8.28 to approximately 6.0 by 2014, then the PBOC managed it in a narrow band.

The 2015 surprise: In August 2015, the PBOC devalued the yuan by ~2% in a single day — its largest one-day move in two decades. Global markets panicked (commodity prices fell, EM currencies sold off). The message: China retains the right to use currency policy offensively.

China's long-term play: Yuan internationalization. The Belt and Road Initiative (covered in a future lesson) is partly a yuan circulation strategy — Chinese loans and trade denominated in yuan. The Petro-yuan (China paying for Saudi oil in yuan) is a direct challenge to the Petrodollar system. China's gold accumulation (buying in central banks rather than announcing) builds yuan credibility without triggering dollar-system retaliation.

**Investment lesson:** China's currency strategy is 20-year, not 20-month. Short-term yuan volatility is tactical. The structural thesis — yuan gradually displaces dollar in Asian trade settlement, reducing dollar demand — is a multi-decade shift in reserve currency architecture. Gold and commodities priced in yuan are the early-stage expression.

### Case 4: Turkey Under Erdogan (2018–2023) — Political Control of Monetary Policy

Turkey's lira depreciation is a case study in what happens when a political leader overrides central bank independence for ideological reasons. Erdogan holds the heterodox view that high interest rates cause inflation (the inverse of conventional economics). He repeatedly fired central bank governors who raised rates, replacing them with politically compliant officials.

Result: Between 2018 and 2023, the lira lost approximately 80% of its value against the dollar. Inflation peaked above 85% in 2022. Turkey's external debt — largely in dollars — became dramatically more expensive in lira terms. Turkish households dollarized their savings in self-defense.

Eventually, after the 2023 elections, Erdogan appointed a credible finance team (Şimşek) and central bank leadership. The lira stabilized (at a massively depreciated level) as orthodox policy was restored.

**Investment lesson:** Politically compromised central banks destroy currency value systematically. The pattern is detectable: political interference in central bank appointments → rate cuts despite inflation → currency sell-off → dollarization → capital flight → eventual credibility crisis or policy reversal. Monitoring central bank independence is a precondition for EM currency positions. This is why Lesson 12 (Reading Central Banks Through a Geopolitical Lens) matters for FX investing.

### Case 5: Russia 2022 — Weaponizing the Dollar System and the Countermove

The freezing of Russian reserves in February 2022 was the most significant event in the geopolitics of currency since the Nixon shock of 1971. In one decision, the G7 demonstrated that reserve assets held in Western financial institutions were not sovereign property if the holder was sufficiently sanctioned.

Russia's response was the first live test of a post-dollar financial architecture:
- **Ruble-for-gas decree:** Russia demanded payment for gas in rubles, forcing European buyers to open accounts at Gazprombank.
- **SPFS (Russian equivalent of SWIFT):** Expanded connections to non-Western counterparties.
- **Yuan settlement:** Russia pivoted dramatically to yuan-denominated trade with China. By 2023, the yuan had become the most traded currency on the Moscow Exchange, surpassing the dollar.
- **Gold repatriation:** Central banks globally accelerated gold purchases to non-seizable, non-digital reserve assets.

The ruble itself: After initially collapsing (hitting 139 per dollar in March 2022), the ruble recovered dramatically — supported by capital controls, the ruble-for-gas decree, and high commodity revenues. This confused Western commentators who expected a currency collapse to translate into regime collapse. It did not. Currency can be stabilized by administrative means even as the underlying economy suffers.

**Investment lesson:** The reserve asset system is bifurcating. Dollar-bloc assets (US Treasuries, dollar deposits) remain dominant but now carry political risk that did not previously exist. Gold, yuan-denominated assets, and commodity-backed instruments are attracting allocation from sovereigns hedging dollar-system exclusion risk. This is not dedollarization at speed — but it is structurally directional.

## Part III: The Current Landscape — 2025/2026

### Dedollarization: Real Signal or Noise?

The dollar's share of global reserves has declined from ~72% in 2000 to approximately 57-58% in 2025. The decline is real but slow. The dollar remains dominant because:
1. No single alternative exists at scale — the euro, yuan, and yen each have structural limitations.
2. US Treasury markets remain the deepest, most liquid sovereign bond market on earth.
3. Dollar invoicing in trade is sticky — oil, commodities, and much of global trade are still dollar-denominated.

But the *marginal* direction is away from dollar concentration. Central banks are diversifying into gold (record purchases in 2022, 2023, 2024), into yuan for trade settlement, and into bilateral swap lines (China has swap lines with 40+ central banks, creating yuan liquidity outside the dollar system).

**Signal vs. Noise:** Dedollarization is a 10-20 year structural trend, not a 12-month trade. The investment implication is not "short dollar now" but rather "structurally overweight real assets and alternative reserve stores over a multi-year horizon."

### BRICS+ and the Alternative Architecture

The BRICS expansion (Saudi Arabia, UAE, Iran, Egypt, Ethiopia, and Argentina invited in 2023/2024) represents an attempt to build a non-dollar trade and settlement architecture at scale. A BRICS currency has been discussed but faces fundamental obstacles (no member wants to give the PBOC control of a shared currency; divergent economic interests). More likely than a BRICS currency: bilateral trade in national currencies + gold settlement + expanded yuan invoicing in energy trade.

### The Dollar Smile and EM FX

The "dollar smile" theory (Stephen Jen) holds that the dollar strengthens in two scenarios: when the US economy is growing strongly (attracting capital) and when global risk aversion spikes (flight to safety). It weakens in the middle — when the global economy is growing but the US is not outperforming. This creates a structural challenge for EM currencies: they get hit hardest precisely when global conditions deteriorate, which is also when geopolitical risk is often highest.

For EM currency investing, the key variables are:
1. **Dollar direction:** Strong dollar = EM FX headwind.
2. **Commodity prices:** Commodity-exporting EMs (BRL, ZAR, CLP, PEN) benefit from commodity bull cycles.
3. **Current account position:** Deficit-running EMs are structurally vulnerable to capital flow reversals.
4. **Central bank credibility:** Independent, inflation-fighting central banks protect currency value.
5. **Geopolitical alignment:** Countries at risk of sanctions or in conflict with dollar-system enforcers face reserve freeze risk.

## Part IV: Investment Implications

### Directional Views (6–18 Month Horizon)

**1. Gold — Structural Overweight**
The most direct beneficiary of reserve system bifurcation. Central bank demand from non-Western sovereigns is structural, not cyclical. Gold is the only reserve asset that cannot be frozen, has no counterparty risk, and is not controlled by any single state. Target allocation: 5-10% of a geopolitically-aware long-horizon portfolio. Not a trading position — a structural hedge against dollar-system fragility.

**2. EM FX — Selective Long, Country-Specific**
Blanket EM FX exposure is not a thesis — it's an index bet. The framework requires country-specific analysis:
- **Constructive:** Brazilian Real (BRL) — commodity revenues, reasonably independent central bank, strong current account; Indian Rupee (INR) — structural non-alignment, massive domestic demand, managed appreciation; Indonesian Rupiah (IDR) — commodity exporter, stable governance.
- **Cautious:** Turkish Lira (TRY) — central bank credibility still rebuilding, election cycle risk; Egyptian Pound (EGP) — dollar-dependent external financing, IMF dependency; Argentine Peso (ARS) — structural dollarization dynamic.
- **Avoid or short:** Currencies in active sanctions regimes (RUB, IRR) — not investable for Western long-only portfolios.

**3. Yuan-Adjacent Assets — Positioning for Incremental Internationalization**
Not a bet on yuan displacement of the dollar. A bet on yuan's growing role in Asian trade settlement. Expression: Chinese sovereign bonds (for portfolios with access), commodities settled in yuan, Hong Kong-listed commodity producers. Risk: capital controls limit exit liquidity.

**4. Dollar-Denominated EM Debt — Duration Caution**
EM sovereign debt in dollars is the most direct exposure to the "dollar weaponization" risk premium. Countries that are geopolitically exposed to US-led sanctions (or that are building alternative architectures) face a hidden risk premium that standard credit models don't capture. Screened selection: favor EM issuers with strong commodity revenues, low external dollar debt, and geopolitical non-alignment.

**5. Defense and Financial Infrastructure — Structural Beneficiaries**
Countries building payments alternatives (India's UPI, China's CIPS, Russia's SPFS) are investing in financial infrastructure that reduces dollar dependency. This is a 5-10 year infrastructure build. Financial technology companies servicing this build are structural beneficiaries.

## Databricks Angle

**Dataset: BIS (Bank for International Settlements) — Triennial FX Survey**
The BIS publishes the most comprehensive data on global FX trading volumes, currency pairs, and geographic breakdown every three years. This is the ground truth for dollar dependency in global trade settlement.

**Dataset: IMF COFER (Currency Composition of Official Foreign Exchange Reserves)**
Quarterly data on global reserve currency composition by currency. This is the most direct measure of dedollarization. Available via IMF API. A pipeline ingesting COFER quarterly + FRED for dollar index + central bank gold purchase data (World Gold Council) creates a "dollar system stress index" that is highly investable.

**Pipeline idea: EM FX Stress Monitor**
Build a pipeline that scores EM currencies on:
- Current account deficit as % of GDP (IMF World Economic Outlook API)
- Central bank independence index (Freedom House / own scoring)
- External dollar debt as % of reserves (BIS)
- GDELT geopolitical risk score for the country
- Commodity terms-of-trade index (IMF)

Output: A weekly EM FX vulnerability score for 20-30 currencies. Flag currencies whose composite score is deteriorating before they hit the headlines. This is the kind of systematic framework that produces early signal — not noise.

## Market Connection

The most direct market expression of this lesson is **gold**. Central bank gold buying — running at record levels for three consecutive years (2022, 2023, 2024) — is the clearest market signal that sovereigns are hedging against dollar system risk. The buyers are predominantly non-Western: China, India, Turkey, Poland, Czech Republic, Singapore. This is not a cyclical trade. It is a structural reallocation of reserve portfolios away from dollar-denominated assets toward non-seizable real assets. An investor who understood the geopolitics of currency weaponization in early 2022 had the framework to hold gold through the post-2022 bull cycle rather than trading around it.

**Second-order expression:** EM currency selectivity. The countries building their own financial infrastructure (India with UPI/NPCI, Saudi Arabia with MBC, UAE with mBridge) are also the countries with the most structurally supportive EM FX outlooks. Geopolitical alignment toward non-alignment is an EM FX tailwind.
