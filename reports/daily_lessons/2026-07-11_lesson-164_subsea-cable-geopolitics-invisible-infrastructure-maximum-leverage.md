## 1. The Scale of the System

There are approximately **600 submarine cable systems** in operation today, stretching over 1.4 million kilometers across the ocean floor. They carry **more than 99% of all transoceanic data** — including:

- SWIFT interbank messaging
- NYSE, CME, and all major equity and derivatives trades
- Satellite positioning corrections
- Cloud infrastructure traffic (AWS, Azure, GCP)
- Government and military communications (even those that think they use satellites)

Satellites handle less than 1% of this traffic. They are too slow (latency), too narrow (bandwidth), and too expensive per bit. The internet, global finance, and modern military command-and-control all ride on glass fiber laid in the dark at the bottom of the sea.

This is not a niche infrastructure fact. **It is the physical foundation of the entire rules-based economic order.**

---

## 2. Historical Grounding — The First Cable Wars

The first transatlantic telegraph cable was laid in 1858. Within weeks, the British and Americans recognized its strategic importance. Britain — then the dominant maritime power — quickly established control over the global cable network. By World War I, one of Britain's first acts was **cutting Germany's transatlantic cables**, isolating Berlin from Washington before the war had barely begun.

The pattern is consistent across history: **whoever controls the cables controls the information. Whoever controls the information wins the war.**

The Cold War added a new chapter: the USS *Halibut*, a modified U.S. submarine, in 1971 successfully tapped a Soviet communications cable in the Sea of Okhotsk in **Operation Ivy Bells** — eavesdropping on Soviet naval communications for a decade before being betrayed by NSA analyst Ronald Pelton. The cables have always been both a target and an intelligence platform.

## 3. The Modern Threat Landscape — Two Theaters

### Theater 1: The Taiwan / Indo-Pacific Campaign

China has developed a systematic playbook for **below-threshold cable disruption** around Taiwan:

- In 2023, cables connecting Taiwan's **Matsu Islands** were severed **12 times**, costing $2.9M in repairs and isolating 14,000 residents.
- In 2024–2025, Insikt Group documented **five incidents involving five distinct cable damages** around Taiwan, consistently linked to Chinese-flagged or China-affiliated vessels using the **anchor drag technique** — a deniable, low-tech method of severing cables without direct contact.
- In 2025, China unveiled a **deep-sea cable-cutting vessel** capable of operating at 4,000 meters depth — a purpose-built grey-zone warfare asset.

The strategic logic is clear: if China moves on Taiwan, severing its cables is **not an act of war under international law** — it is a maritime "accident." But it would be one of the first kinetic moves in any serious conflict scenario, isolating the island from global communications and financial systems before a single shot is fired.

**Taiwan has two primary cable landing stations, both in metropolitan areas** — a concentration that makes them simultaneously critical and catastrophically vulnerable.

### Theater 2: The Baltic Sabotage Campaign

Russia (with suspected Chinese coordination on at least one vessel) has been systematically probing European cable infrastructure:

- **November 2024**: The *Yi Peng 3*, a Chinese-linked vessel, reportedly severed cables between Finland, Germany, Lithuania, and Sweden.
- **December 2025**: A Russian-linked vessel was suspected of deliberately cutting the Helsinki-Tallinn cable.
- **January 2026**: Baltic littoral countries entered **heightened alert status**. NATO launched **Baltic Sentry**, deploying additional patrol vessels, aircraft, and rapid-response staff specifically for undersea infrastructure protection.
- Insikt Group documented **four incidents involving eight distinct cable damages** in the Baltic Sea between 2024 and 2025.

The strategic message from Moscow and Beijing is identical: *We can turn off your infrastructure. And we can do it in a way that international law cannot cleanly address.*

## 4. The Structural Vulnerabilities

Three features make submarine cables uniquely exploitable:

**1. Geographic chokepoints.** Cables, like oil pipelines, cannot escape geography. The Strait of Malacca, the Bab-el-Mandeb, the Taiwan Strait, the Luzon Strait, and the English Channel are all **cable chokepoints** where dozens of cables converge through narrow corridors. A handful of coordinated cuts in the right locations could isolate entire continents from the global financial system.

**2. The repair bottleneck.** There are **roughly 60 cable repair ships in the world**, and they are commercial vessels, not military assets. Repair of a deep-sea cable takes weeks to months. During that window, traffic must reroute — or fail. AI traffic growth is dramatically increasing the utilization rates of all existing cables, making any outage increasingly costly.

**3. Ownership fragmentation.** Most cables are now owned by **tech hyperscalers** (Google, Meta, Microsoft, Amazon), which have built private networks parallel to the shared internet infrastructure. This is efficient but creates a governance gap: no single state controls the private cable networks, NATO has no authority over Google's cables, and international law has minimal enforcement mechanisms for undersea cable protection.

---

## 5. The Policy Response — Too Slow, Too Fragmented

The EU's **Subsea Cable Security Action Plan** (2025–2026) focuses on: prevention, detection, response, recovery, and deterrence. NATO's Baltic Sentry provides naval presence. Australia, the Philippines, and the U.S. have all announced enhanced monitoring.

But structural reform lags the threat:

- There is no international equivalent of ICAO (aviation) or IMO (shipping) specifically governing undersea cable security.
- Attribution for cable cuts is legally complex and politically contested.
- The repair fleet is privately owned and not deployable on military timelines.
- The AI boom is rerouting cable infrastructure across the Indian and Pacific Oceans to avoid the South China Sea — a significant physical restructuring of the internet's geography that will take a decade to complete.
