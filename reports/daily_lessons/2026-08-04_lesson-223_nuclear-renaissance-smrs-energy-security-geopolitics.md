## Opening Question (Socratic Frame)

*If a country can produce electricity from a reactor that fits inside a shipping container, that never needs refueling for 20 years, and whose fuel supplier also happens to be its geopolitical patron — who holds the power in that relationship?*

Nuclear energy used to be a Cold War story: superpowers building massive reactors, deterrence through megawatts, atoms for peace as soft power theater. That story ended, more or less, at Chernobyl and Fukushima. What's happening now is different. A new generation of reactor technology, a decade of European energy shock, and the arithmetic of climate targets are producing a global nuclear revival — but the strategic logic driving it has almost nothing to do with the atom and everything to do with who controls the supply chain.

## Core Concept: Why Nuclear Is Back — and Why It's Different This Time

### 1. The Energy Security Imperative Post-2022

The Ukraine war was the forcing function. Europe discovered in 2022 what energy security analysts had been warning for years: dependence on a single supplier for a critical energy input is a strategic liability, not just a commercial risk. The policy response — across Europe, Asia, and North America — was to accelerate every non-fossil, non-Russian energy source available. Nuclear, previously being wound down in Germany and declining across much of the West, suddenly looked very different through an energy security lens.

The Iran War of 2026 reinforced this. With Hormuz disruptions spiking oil prices and LNG spot markets going parabolic, countries with domestic nuclear capacity looked stable. Countries without it scrambled.

### 2. The Technology Break: Small Modular Reactors (SMRs)

Traditional large-scale nuclear plants have a structural problem: they take 10-20 years and $10-20 billion to build, cost overruns are endemic (Vogtle in the US ran to $35bn from a $14bn budget), and they require enormous grid infrastructure to connect. This made nuclear financeable only with government guarantees and near-certain to disappoint.

Small Modular Reactors (SMRs) are the proposed solution. Key characteristics:
- **Scale:** 10–300 MWe per unit (vs. 1,000–1,600 MWe for conventional plants)
- **Modularity:** Factory-built, standardized modules shipped to site — the same logic that made container shipping cheap
- **Speed:** Target construction times of 3-5 years once design is certified
- **Siting flexibility:** Can be placed near industrial heat users, remote locations, or decommissioned fossil fuel plants (using existing grid connections)
- **Cost target:** $5,000-8,000/kW installed vs. $10,000-15,000/kW for large plant (still contested — no SMR has yet been built at commercial scale in the West)

Lead players: NuScale (US, first SMR design approved by NRC), Rolls-Royce (UK), X-energy (US), Terrestrial Energy (Canada), and — critically — Rosatom (Russia) and CNNC (China), both of which already operate or are building commercial SMR-class reactors.

### 3. Advanced Reactor Designs Beyond SMRs

The nuclear technology frontier is broader than SMRs:
- **High-Temperature Gas Reactors (HTGRs):** Produce process heat at 700-950°C, enabling industrial decarbonization (steel, cement, hydrogen production) — China's HTR-PM at Shidaowan is operational
- **Molten Salt Reactors (MSRs):** Operate at atmospheric pressure, cannot melt down in the conventional sense, can use thorium fuel — multiple startups (Terrestrial, Moltex, Kairos)
- **Fast Reactors / Breeder Reactors:** Burn nuclear waste as fuel, extending uranium resources dramatically — Russia's BN-800 and BN-1200 series
- **Fusion (distant):** Commonwealth Fusion Systems targeting 2030s; ITER operational 2035+

### 4. The Fuel Supply Chokepoint: Enriched Uranium

Here is where nuclear becomes acutely geopolitical. The nuclear fuel cycle has multiple chokepoints:

| Stage | Who Controls It |
|---|---|
| Uranium mining | Kazakhstan (43% of world supply), Australia, Canada, Namibia, Uzbekistan |
| Conversion (UF6) | US (ConverDyn), Russia (Tenex), France (Orano), China |
| Enrichment | Russia (Rosatom — 46% of global enrichment capacity), US, France, China, UK |
| Fuel fabrication | Fragmented, but Rosatom has dominant share for Soviet-design reactors |

**The critical constraint:** Russia's Rosatom controls roughly 46% of global uranium enrichment capacity and supplies fuel to 17% of the world's nuclear reactors — including many in Eastern Europe. The West has been scrambling to build alternative enrichment capacity since 2022, but enrichment facilities take 5-10 years and billions to build. The US passed the Prohibiting Russian Uranium Imports Act in 2024 but granted waivers through 2027 because there's simply not enough Western enrichment capacity to replace Rosatom immediately.

China is building enrichment capacity aggressively — not just for its own reactor build-out (targeting 150+ GWe by 2035, up from ~57 GWe today) but to offer fuel services as part of its reactor export strategy.

## Historical Example: Rosatom and the Geopolitics of Reactor Export

The clearest case study in nuclear geopolitics is Rosatom's export strategy over the past 20 years.

Russia figured out before anyone else that selling a nuclear reactor is not a one-time transaction — it's a 60-year relationship. When a country buys a Russian VVER reactor, they get:
- Russian-designed and built reactor (revenue)
- Russian fuel (recurring revenue, ~$50-100m/year per reactor)
- Russian operating support and maintenance
- Russian-trained operators
- Russian waste management services
- Russian financing (state-backed loans at below-market rates through Sberbank/VEB)

This is the complete lock-in. A country that buys a Rosatom reactor cannot easily switch suppliers — the fuel is reactor-specific, the trained workforce is versed in Russian systems, and switching would require essentially rebuilding from scratch.

By 2022, Rosatom had contracts to build 35 reactors across 12 countries: Bangladesh, Belarus, China, Egypt, Finland, Hungary, India, Iran, Turkey, and others. Hungary (an EU member) is building two new VVER-1200 units with Russian financing even as the EU implements sanctions on Russia over Ukraine — because Hungary calculated it cannot afford to abandon the sunk cost and has no credible alternative.

Turkey's Akkuyu plant is even more extreme: Rosatom owns and operates the plant on Turkish soil under a BOO (Build-Own-Operate) model, selling electricity to Turkey at a guaranteed price. It's a Russian power plant inside a NATO country.

This is why the US, UK, France, South Korea, Japan, and Canada have all explicitly designated nuclear reactor exports as a national strategic priority: the economic case is important, but the strategic case — locking in 60-year energy relationships that compete with Rosatom — is the primary driver.

## Investment Implications

### Uranium: The Floor Has Changed

The uranium bull case is structural, not cyclical. Global reactor capacity is growing (China, India, Southeast Asia, Middle East, Eastern Europe), reactors are running longer (US extending licenses to 80 years), and new builds are accelerating. Meanwhile, Kazatomprom (the world's largest uranium producer) cut production guidance in 2023-2024 due to supply chain constraints, and Western utilities have been rebuilding inventory after years of spot-market underinvestment post-Fukushima.

**Directional view:** Uranium spot prices (currently $65-90/lb depending on when you're reading this) have structural upward pressure over a 3-5 year horizon. Spot price is volatile; long-term contract price is what matters for producers.

*Assets:* Physical uranium (Sprott Physical Uranium Trust — SPUT), Cameco (CCO), Kazatomprom (KAP), NexGen Energy (NXE), Paladin Energy, Uranium ETFs (URA, URNM).

**Risk:** Political disruption in Kazakhstan (which accounts for 43% of global supply and sits between Russia and China), enrichment bottleneck persisting longer than expected, SMR delays (perpetually 5 years away).

### Nuclear Utilities: The Quietly Bullish Trade

Existing nuclear utilities have something new: their sunk cost assets are suddenly politically protected and economically repriced. The US government has provided production tax credits for existing nuclear ($15/MWh through IRA), extended the Price-Anderson liability protection, and is considering further subsidies. European governments that once planned to close plants are extending licenses.

*Assets:* Constellation Energy (CEG — largest US nuclear operator), Duke Energy (DUK), Dominion Energy (D), Électricité de France (EDF — state-owned), Korea Hydro & Nuclear Power (KHNP, unlisted but parent Korea Electric Power — KEP).

### SMR and Advanced Reactor Plays

This is venture-stage risk embedded in public market vehicles. Most SMR companies are either private or have come public through SPACs with minimal revenue. The exception is Rolls-Royce, which has a credible SMR program alongside its aerospace business.

*Assets:* NuScale Power (SMR — US), BWX Technologies (BWXT — nuclear component manufacturer for both civilian and defense), Curtiss-Wright (CW), Aecon Group (nuclear construction, Canada). Rolls-Royce PLC (RR.L) for the patient investor who wants SMR exposure with an aviation hedge.

**Key risk:** SMR cost curves are entirely theoretical. NuScale cancelled its Carbon Free Power Project in 2023 when projected costs rose to $89/MWh — making it uncompetitive against solar + storage in many markets. The SMR bull case requires that factory manufacturing actually delivers the cost reductions promised.

### Geopolitical Trade: The Rosatom Displacement Theme

If the West is serious about displacing Rosatom from Eastern European and Global South markets, someone has to finance and build the replacement reactors. The contenders:
- **US:** Via US Export-Import Bank, US International Development Finance Corporation — financing NuScale, X-energy exports
- **South Korea:** KHNP won the UAE's Barakah contract (4 units, operating) and is bidding for Czech, Polish, Romanian, and Philippine projects
- **France:** EDF bidding for UK Sizewell C, India's large-scale program

South Korea's nuclear export strategy is arguably the most commercially credible right now — they built Barakah on time and on budget, which is more than any Western nation can say for recent large-scale projects. The KHNP parent (KEPCO) is listed in Seoul (Korea Exchange: 015760).

## Market Connection

Nuclear is one of the few sectors where geopolitical risk, energy security, and climate policy all point in the same direction: toward more capacity, higher uranium prices, and a Western-vs.-Russia-vs.-China competition in reactor technology that will persist for decades. The investment case is structural, not tactical — and it runs through uranium miners, nuclear utilities, and the small but growing ecosystem of reactor component manufacturers.

The caveat: nuclear has been "about to take off" for twenty years. SMR timelines consistently disappoint. The bull thesis requires patience, and patience requires conviction in the structural demand story. That conviction is stronger today than it has been since the 1970s — but this remains a 5-10 year trade, not a 12-month one.
