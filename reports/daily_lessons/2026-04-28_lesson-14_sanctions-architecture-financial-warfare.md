## I. What Sanctions Actually Are — The Architecture

Most investors think of sanctions as a binary on/off switch. They are not. Sanctions are a layered, modular system with multiple legal authorities, target categories, and enforcement mechanisms. Understanding the architecture is prerequisite to pricing sanctions risk correctly.

### The Legal Foundation (US-centric, because dollar dominance makes it universal)

**OFAC — Office of Foreign Assets Control**
The primary US sanctions authority, sitting inside the Treasury Department. OFAC administers:

- **Comprehensive sanctions programs** — blanket prohibition on nearly all transactions (Iran, North Korea, Cuba, Syria)
- **Sectoral/targeted sanctions** — restrictions on specific sectors (Russian energy, finance, defense) or specific entities
- **SDN List (Specially Designated Nationals)** — any entity or individual on this list is effectively cut off from the dollar system globally. Not just Americans — anyone transacting in dollars anywhere in the world is exposed to US jurisdiction

**The key legal mechanism:** If a transaction touches a US correspondent bank — which almost all dollar transactions do, because clearing happens through New York — it falls under US jurisdiction. This is why OFAC has global reach even for transactions between two non-US entities.

### Three Types of Sanctions Risk

| Type | Description | Investment Implication |
|------|-------------|----------------------|
| **Primary sanctions** | Direct prohibition on US persons/entities | Affects US-domiciled investors directly |
| **Secondary sanctions** | Penalize non-US entities for transactions with sanctioned parties | The nuclear option — makes dollar access conditional on compliance globally |
| **Blocking sanctions** | Freeze assets, prohibit any dealing | Instant asset impairment for holders |

Secondary sanctions are the most powerful and most feared. When the US imposed secondary sanctions targeting Iran's oil sector, European companies that had re-entered Iran after the 2015 JCPOA nuclear deal were forced to exit — not because they faced US primary sanctions, but because they valued their access to the US financial system more than their Iran business.

## II. The SWIFT Weapon — Mechanics and Limits

**SWIFT** (Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunication) is a messaging network — not a payments system itself — used by ~11,000 financial institutions in 200+ countries to communicate transaction instructions. Being disconnected from SWIFT doesn't make transactions impossible. It makes them vastly more expensive, slower, and less reliable.

### What SWIFT Disconnection Actually Does

When Iran was disconnected from SWIFT in 2012 (and again in 2018), its oil exports didn't go to zero. They fell ~50–60% over several years. The damage came from:

1. **Transaction friction** — correspondent banking relationships collapse; even willing counterparties can't easily execute
2. **Payment uncertainty** — buyers can't pay, sellers can't receive, trust evaporates
3. **Insurance pullback** — marine insurers won't cover cargoes; shipping companies won't load; the logistics chain seizes
4. **Reserve depletion** — can't repatriate export earnings; foreign exchange position degrades

### The 2022 Russia Case — The Most Important Modern Test

Russia's February 2022 disconnection from SWIFT was the most significant sanctions deployment in the postwar era. The West expected a swift economic collapse. Here's what actually happened:

**What worked:**
- Ruble fell from ~75/USD to ~150/USD in March 2022 — a 50% devaluation
- Foreign reserves frozen: ~$300 billion immobilized in Western custodians
- Corporate debt refinancing essentially impossible
- Russian access to Western technology, semiconductors, and capital markets severed
- GDP contracted ~2.1% in 2022

**What didn't work as expected:**
- Ruble recovered to pre-war levels by April 2022 — capital controls, mandatory export proceeds conversion, and commodity export revenues stabilized it
- Energy revenues continued flowing: Europe kept buying Russian gas for over a year because it had no alternative
- China and India absorbed redirected Russian oil at discounted prices — the oil still moved, just at lower margins
- Russia pivoted to non-SWIFT channels: bilateral clearing, barter arrangements, yuan-settled trade
- The economy stabilized; by 2023 Russia was running a current account surplus

**The lesson:** Sanctions work better against smaller economies with fewer alternatives and fewer powerful patrons. Russia is large enough, resource-rich enough, and backed by China closely enough that the financial isolation was painful but not catastrophic.

## III. The Dollar as a Weapon — and the Backlash

The Russia sanctions accelerated a shift that was already underway: **major non-Western powers treating the dollar's centrality not as neutral infrastructure, but as a geopolitical risk to manage**.

### The Dollar's Structural Power

Why does dollar dominance translate to financial coercive power?

1. **Invoicing dominance** — ~50% of global trade is invoiced in dollars, including commodities that have nothing to do with the US
2. **Reserve currency** — ~59% of global foreign exchange reserves held in dollars (down from 73% in 2000, but still dominant)
3. **Correspondent banking** — even non-dollar transactions often route through US correspondent banks
4. **Petrodollar recycling** — oil exporters price in dollars, recycle into US Treasuries, reinforcing both dollar demand and US financing capacity

This creates the "exorbitant privilege" — but also "exorbitant responsibility" and, increasingly, "exorbitant liability." Every time the US weaponizes the dollar system, it creates an incentive for every other government to reduce exposure.

### The Countermeasures

The past 4 years have seen the fastest de-dollarization in decades — not from a single actor but from a distributed response to perceived risk:

| System | Operator | Status (April 2026) |
|--------|----------|---------------------|
| **CIPS** | China (PBoC) | 160+ countries, accelerating — now dominant in Asia |
| **SPFS** | Russia | Bilateral with select partners |
| **mBridge** | BIS/China/UAE/Thailand/HK | CBDC multi-currency corridor — live pilot |
| **Yuan bilaterals** | China + Saudi, Brazil, Russia | Russia-China now 90% non-dollar |

**What this means for the dollar's coercive power:**
The weapon is being blunted slowly, not quickly. The dollar system won't collapse, but the marginal cost of sanctions on large, connected economies is rising. Against Iran or North Korea — still highly effective. Against Russia — effective but not decisive. Against China — almost certainly counterproductive; the financial disruption to the global system would be symmetric.

## IV. The China Sanctions Question — The Unspeakable Scenario

The most important unspeakable question in sanctions analysis: **Can the US credibly sanction China?**

The Russia case tells us: probably not in any comprehensive way.

- China holds ~$800 billion in US Treasuries — a financial freeze would be mutually catastrophic
- China is embedded in global supply chains in a way Russia never was; sanctions would hit every consumer product in every Western country
- China has CIPS as a partial alternative to SWIFT
- China's domestic market is large enough that many multinationals cannot afford to exit

The US sanctions toolkit against China is therefore limited to **targeted, sectoral, technology-focused tools** — OFAC SDN designations of specific firms (Huawei, SMIC), export controls on semiconductors, investment restrictions in sensitive sectors. These are meaningful but not existential.

**The practical ceiling:** Comprehensive sanctions against China would cause a global recession. That's not a credible threat because the sender suffers as much as the target. The threat has to be credible to deter — and this one isn't. China knows it. The US knows it. This asymmetry shapes every confrontation.

## V. Investment Implications — How Sanctions Move Asset Prices

### Imposition of Sanctions (the shock)

When sanctions are announced, the sequence typically plays out as follows:

**Immediate (hours to days):**
- Target country currency collapses (20–50% devaluation is typical; Iran lost ~60% vs USD over 2018 sanctions reimposition)
- Target country equity markets, if accessible, fall sharply
- Commodity markets reprice if the target is a major producer (oil, gas, grain, metals)
- European banks with exposure sell off — compliance departments suspend all dealings immediately
- Safe haven flows into USD, gold, CHF

**Short-term (weeks to months):**
- Commodity price depends on supply substitutability. If the sanctioned country is hard to replace (Russia gas in 2022), prices spike. If supply is diversifiable (Iran oil in 2012), prices rise modestly then settle as alternatives emerge.
- Third-country equities exposed through trade or investment (Indian banks, Turkish commodity traders, UAE conglomerates) sell off on secondary sanctions risk
- Defense equities in sanctioning countries often rally — sanctions signal elevated geopolitical tension, which raises defense budgets

**Long-term (months to years):**
- Depends entirely on effectiveness. If sanctions fracture the target economy (Zimbabwe, Venezuela), EM contagion risk rises. If they don't (Russia post-2022), markets habituate and the initial asset price moves partially reverse.

### Sanctions Relief (the lift)

Sanctions relief events — Iran JCPOA 2015, partial Iran waivers 2019 — are often **better priced in advance** than imposition events. The signal sequence:

1. Diplomatic back-channel reports → oil price drops on anticipated supply increase (Iran scenario)
2. Formal announcement → sharp one-day commodity move, target currency rally
3. Gradual re-entry → European bank stocks with EM exposure often outperform as business relationships re-open

**Key asymmetry:** Relief is slower to execute than imposition. A sanctions package goes on in 72 hours. Relief requires Congressional waiver, international coordination, and counterparty recalibration — often 12–24 months before trade flows normalize.

### Secondary Sanctions — The Contagion Risk

For investors, secondary sanctions risk is the most underpriced exposure:

- A European bank processing Russian energy trade finance gets penalized
- A Chinese bank facilitating Iranian oil payments faces correspondent banking cutoff
- An Indian refinery buying discounted Russian crude gets flagged

The equity impact on institutions caught in secondary sanctions can be severe and fast — and the market typically hasn't priced it because the institution hasn't been disclosed as a target until it is.

**How to monitor:** OFAC SDN list additions, Congressional sanctions threat letters (often a leading indicator), State Department "name and shame" reports, secondary sanctions designations under Iran Freedom and Counter-Proliferation Act.

## VI. Live Application — Iran Sanctions in the Hormuz Crisis

Connecting back to the active Hormuz scenario from Lesson 13:

**The current escalation ladder has a sanctions rung that hasn't been activated:**

Iran's oil exports are currently ~2.2 mb/d (much of it to China via shadow tanker fleet, at steep discounts). If the US decides to escalate beyond diplomatic pressure and naval posturing, the escalation toolkit includes:

1. **Snap-back sanctions** — reimposing full JCPOA-era restrictions on Iran's financial system
2. **Secondary sanctions on China** — penalizing Chinese banks that finance Iranian crude purchases (the nuclear option — would cause a US-China financial crisis)
3. **Designating IRGC-controlled entities** managing the strait closure as SDNs — targeted, lower escalation cost
4. **SWIFT re-disconnection of Iranian banks** — already partially in place; full disconnection would be escalatory signal

**For your energy position:** If secondary sanctions on China's Iran oil purchases are announced, expect:
- Brent spike of $15–25 (removes ~2 mb/d from accessible supply; remaining Chinese demand must source elsewhere)
- But — China retaliates through CIPS restrictions, trade measures, or rare earth export curbs
- The net effect on your energy position is positive (higher prices), but exposure to anything with Chinese supply chain risk needs reassessment simultaneously

## VII. Databricks Angle

**Dataset:** OFAC SDN list — updated publicly, downloadable as XML/CSV. OFAC also publishes Consolidated Sanctions List.

**Pipeline idea: Sanctions Signal Tracker**
```
Source: OFAC SDN list daily snapshot (XML feed)
Transform: diff against prior day → extract new additions by country/sector/entity type
Enrich: cross-reference with GDELT actor database → identify geopolitical event co-occurrence
Output: "Sanctions velocity" metric — rate of new SDN additions by target country
Signal: Spike in SDN additions for a country → leading indicator of escalation
        → join to FX and commodity price data → lag analysis for alpha potential
```

**Why this works:** SDN additions are often announced with less market fanfare than headline sanctions packages, but they represent escalation signals. A spike in IRGC-linked SDN additions before a major announcement could be a 2–4 day leading indicator.

**Secondary pipeline:** EU sanctions register (EUR-Lex, updated daily), UK OFSI sanctions list — combine with OFAC for a multi-jurisdiction sanctions coverage score by country.

**Relevant FRED series:** Bilateral exchange rates for target currencies (Iran IRR, Russia RUB, Turkey TRY — all visible in periods of sanctions pressure). Commodity price series for cross-correlation.
