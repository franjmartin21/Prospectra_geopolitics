## The Engine Under the Hood: How Equities Are Actually Valued

Every equity price is, at its mathematical root, a discounted cash flow problem. The present value of a stock equals the sum of all future earnings, discounted back to today at some rate that reflects the risk and time value of money:

**Price = E₁ / (r - g)**

Where:
- **E₁** = next year's expected earnings
- **r** = the discount rate (risk-free rate + equity risk premium)
- **g** = the long-run earnings growth rate

This deceptively simple formula contains the entire story of why fiscal dominance destroys equity valuations in a specific, predictable way.

**The discount rate is the denominator.** When it rises, the present value falls — even if earnings grow. And it rises most destructively for **long-duration assets** — companies whose earnings are weighted heavily toward the distant future, not the near term. High-growth tech companies, early-stage biotech, speculative real estate: these are long-duration equities in the same mathematical sense that a 30-year Treasury is a long-duration bond. A 1% rise in the discount rate hits them harder than it hits a commodity producer with near-term, tangible cash flows.

This is the core insight that most equity investors missed in 2022 and are still processing in 2026: **the bear market in bonds IS a bear market in long-duration equities.** They are the same mathematical phenomenon.

## The Equity Risk Premium Problem

The equity risk premium (ERP) is the additional return investors demand for owning equities over the risk-free rate. In the U.S., the historical ERP has averaged roughly 4–5% per year. This is the compensation for equity volatility, illiquidity, and the risk of permanent capital loss.

The ERP can be approximated as:

**ERP ≈ Earnings Yield (E/P) − Risk-Free Rate**

As of mid-2026:
- U.S. equity earnings yield: approximately 4.5% (S&P 500 P/E around 22x)
- 10-year Treasury yield: approximately 5.2%
- **Implied ERP: negative 0.7%**

Read that again: U.S. equities are currently offering a **negative** risk premium over Treasuries. Investors are being paid *less* to take equity risk than to hold government bonds.

This is not normal. It has happened before — briefly in 1999-2000, and again at various points in the 2017-2021 ZIRP era. But there is an important difference now: **in the ZIRP era, the risk-free rate was artificially suppressed to near zero, so a negative ERP reflected the absence of alternatives.** In 2026, the risk-free rate is 5.2% and rising. There are real alternatives. A negative ERP now means equities are being priced as if fiscal dominance is permanent and that it will always be resolved in equity holders' favor — a deeply optimistic assumption.

**The implication is stark:** if the 10-year yield rises another 100 basis points (which the term premium repricing trajectory of 2025-2026 makes plausible), U.S. equities as a whole would need to fall roughly 20% just to restore a historically normal ERP of 4%. This is not a prediction — it is a structural observation about where the math sits.

## Sector Rotation: Who Wins and Who Loses

Not all equities are equally vulnerable. The fiscal dominance regime creates clear winners and clear losers at the sector level. Understanding this map is the practical output of the valuation framework above.

### Winners in the Fiscal Dominance Regime

**1. Energy & Commodities Producers**
These are near-duration companies: their cash flows come from extracting and selling physical resources now, not from profits projected 10–15 years in the future. Rising commodity prices (driven by the same fiscal expansion that causes inflation) boost revenues directly. They are the equity expression of the real assets thesis.

**2. Financials — Selectively**
Banks earn net interest margin — the spread between what they borrow and what they lend. Higher-for-longer rates mechanically widen this spread, boosting bank profitability. The caveat: credit quality. If fiscal dominance tips into a recession, loan defaults rise and the benefit reverses. Regional banks and insurance companies with long-duration liabilities are more vulnerable. Large-cap universal banks with diversified revenue are the safer play.

**3. Defense & Industrial**
Government spending is the defining feature of fiscal dominance. Defense budgets globally have expanded sharply in 2025-2026 as NATO commitments increase, Asian powers rearm, and the energy transition drives government-directed industrial policy. Defense and aerospace companies are essentially government revenue streams — insulated from the discount rate problem because their earnings are near-term, contracted, and backed by sovereign balance sheets.

**4. Companies with Genuine Pricing Power**
The ability to pass inflation through to customers is a moat in a high-inflation regime. Consumer staples with genuine brand loyalty (distinct from commoditized staples), luxury goods, and healthcare companies with inelastic demand can protect real margins in ways that price-competitive industries cannot.

### Losers in the Fiscal Dominance Regime

**1. Long-Duration Technology Growth**
The most directly exposed sector. High multiples (P/E ratios of 30–50x are common in tech) imply that investors are pricing in earnings growth far into the future. A higher discount rate compresses those forward earnings in present value terms. The math is brutal: a stock with 70% of its value in earnings expected more than 5 years out is hit harder by a 1% rate rise than an oil company with 80% of its value in the next 3 years of production.

**2. Utilities**
Utilities are "bond proxies" — investors hold them for their stable, dividend-like yields. When the risk-free rate rises above utility dividend yields, rational investors rotate out of utilities and into Treasuries. This is happening now and has more room to run if yields rise further.

**3. REITs (Real Estate Investment Trusts)**
REITs carry structural leverage (they borrow to buy property) and are priced on yield spreads versus government bonds. Higher yields compress REIT valuations directly. The one exception: industrial REITs that serve logistics and defense infrastructure, which benefit from government spending tailwinds.

**4. Consumer Discretionary in Levered Households**
If fiscal dominance results in negative real wage growth (inflation outpacing nominal wages), consumers with variable-rate debt (mortgages, auto loans, credit cards) face a real income squeeze. Discretionary spending — travel, retail, restaurants — is the first to contract.

## The Geographic Lens: Why International Equities Are Undervalued

U.S. equity valuations are elevated by any historical or cross-sectional standard. As of mid-2026:

| Market | P/E (trailing) | Earnings Yield | ERP vs. Local 10yr |
|---|---|---|---|
| S&P 500 (U.S.) | ~22x | ~4.5% | ~-0.7% (negative) |
| Euro Stoxx 50 | ~14x | ~7.1% | ~+2.6% positive |
| MSCI EM | ~12x | ~8.3% | ~+2.8% positive (vs. avg EM 10yr) |
| Nikkei (Japan) | ~15x | ~6.7% | ~+5.7% positive (vs. near-zero JGB) |

The gap is striking. European and emerging market equities are offering 2.5–3% more equity risk premium over their local risk-free rates than U.S. equities are. This is partly explained by higher perceived risk (geopolitical exposure, EM currency risk, governance quality). But it is also partly explained by the narrative premium investors have attached to U.S. tech companies — a premium that is vulnerable if the earnings growth story disappoints or if the discount rate continues to rise.

**The structural case for international diversification in 2026 is the strongest it has been in over a decade** — not because European or EM companies are better businesses, but because they are substantially cheaper for any given unit of earnings power.

## The Earnings Growth Escape Valve

There is one legitimate bull case for U.S. equities in this environment: **earnings growth can substitute for multiple expansion.** If the economy grows and earnings compound faster than the discount rate damages valuations, stocks can still generate positive real returns — even with a flat or contracting multiple.

This escape valve requires:
1. **Nominal GDP growth that exceeds the risk-free rate** — which is possible in an inflationary environment but not guaranteed
2. **Corporate profit margins holding at current elevated levels** — which requires continued pricing power and labor cost control
3. **Earnings being concentrated in companies with real moats** — not levered to the economic cycle

In practice, this is an argument for **quality over growth**: companies with high return on invested capital, low debt, genuine pricing power, and near-term earnings realization. This is a narrower set than the S&P 500 as a whole, and it explains why active stock selection is making a comeback after the index-everything era of 2010-2021.

## Investment Implications

| Structural View | Tactical Expression |
|---|---|
| U.S. multiples are compressed by ERP math | Underweight U.S. equity index; overweight earnings quality within U.S. |
| Sector rotation away from long-duration tech | Shift from growth to value; commodity producers, financials, defense |
| International valuation advantage | Overweight European value, selected EM with current account surpluses |
| Earnings quality > growth | Factor tilt: high ROIC, low leverage, strong free cash flow yield |
| Fiscal dominance = defense spending surge | Defense sector as a multi-year structural overweight |

**Portfolio Construction Note:** A fiscal dominance-aware equity portfolio looks substantially different from a passive market-cap-weighted index. The market cap index in the U.S. is heavily overweight the most rate-sensitive sectors (big tech). An active rotation into value, commodities, defense, and international markets is not a contrarian call — it is the mean-reversion call that pure valuation math implies.
