## Opening Framing Question

A weekly briefing identifies a calendar date as a "binary event" — pass/fail, deal/no-deal. Before building any decision protocol around that date, there is a prior question that has to be asked and answered first: **is the thing expiring on that date actually the thing that determines the outcome you care about, or is it one input among several that happen to share a deadline?** Conflating "a deadline exists" with "the deadline is the lever" is a specific, nameable error. Hold that question — Section 4 below shows exactly where the June 16 briefing made it.

---

## Section 1: Mechanism A — The Section 122 Global Surcharge

Here is what is actually expiring on July 24, and why it exists at all.

**The legal vehicle.** Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 authorizes a president to impose a temporary import surcharge — capped at 15%, capped at 150 days — to address a "fundamental international payments problem." It is a narrow, deliberately bounded tool. On February 20, 2026, the same day the Supreme Court invalidated the prior administration tariff program under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA), Trump issued a new proclamation imposing a Section 122 surcharge — 10% initially, raised to the statutory maximum 15% within 48 hours — effective February 24, 2026, for exactly 150 days. Run the calendar: February 24 plus 150 days lands on **July 24, 2026**, precisely. That date is not a negotiating deadline anyone chose. It is a statutory clock that started ticking the moment the proclamation took effect.

**Critically, this surcharge applies uniformly to all countries** and does not preserve the country-specific caps that had been individually negotiated under the prior IEEPA regime — including the EU's own negotiated 15%-cap arrangement from August 2025 (more on that in Section 3). Trade partners that had bilateral deals lost the protection of those deals the moment Section 122 landed on top of them, because Section 122 was built as an undifferentiated global instrument, not a country-by-country one.

**The legal status, as of this writing, is contested and unresolved.** On May 7, 2026, the Court of International Trade ruled 2-1 that the Section 122 surcharge exceeds the president's statutory authority — the same conclusion, on different statutory grounds, that doomed the IEEPA tariffs three months earlier. The Department of Justice appealed immediately. The Federal Circuit issued an administrative stay on May 12 and, after an expedited briefing schedule, granted a full stay pending appeal on June 11 — meaning the tariffs remain in effect right now, and will keep collecting, while the underlying legal question is litigated. No oral argument date has been publicly scheduled as of June 19. There is a real possibility the appeal is still pending, unresolved, on July 24 — at which point the statutory 150-day sunset would lapse on its own terms regardless of what the Federal Circuit eventually decides about its legality.

That is two independent off-ramps converging on roughly the same window: the court could strike it down, or the clock could just run out, or Congress could extend it, or — and this is the option the next section says is the most probable, because it already happened once — the administration could simply replace it with something else before either of the first two off-ramps matters.

## Section 2: The Concrete Example — This Already Happened Four Months Ago

The curriculum's "give a real example" discipline usually reaches back years. This time the most relevant precedent is from this same administration, this same year: **the IEEPA-to-122 swap of February 20, 2026.**

When the Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariff program, the textbook expectation — the one most trade desks were pricing — was a meaningful gap: tariffs invalidated, importers owed refunds, effective rates falling toward pre-2025 baselines while the administration regrouped. That gap lasted hours, not weeks. The same proclamation day produced a replacement instrument (Section 122) calibrated to land at almost the same effective rate the market had just priced out. The adverse ruling did not produce a tariff reduction. It produced an instrument swap.

**Why this matters for July 24 specifically:** an administration that has already demonstrated, within the last four months, that it treats "this specific legal authority was struck down" and "the tariff wall comes down" as two completely separate questions is not a administration for which "Section 122 expires" should be read as "tariffs go away." The base rate for a clean snap-back, given this administration's revealed behavior pattern, is lower than the June 16 briefing implicitly assumed by calling July 24 a "cliff" at all. A cliff implies falling. This administration's track record over the last four months is building a new ledge at the bottom before the old one gives way.

**The deeper historical grounding — why Section 122 exists in the first place.** This is not the first time a president has reached for a unilateral import surcharge to manage an international payments or trade problem. In August 1971, as part of what became known as the Nixon Shock, Nixon imposed a 10% import surcharge alongside suspending dollar-gold convertibility — the event Lesson 3 (The Dollar System & Bretton Woods Legacy) covered as the end of Bretton Woods. Nixon's surcharge relied on emergency authority under the Trading with the Enemy Act of 1917, a wartime statute being stretched well past its original purpose. It held for about four months before being lifted as part of the December 1971 Smithsonian Agreement, and a federal court later found — after the fact, once the surcharge was already history — that the legal basis had been shaky. **Congress's response, three years later, was Section 122 itself**: a purpose-built, explicitly bounded statutory tool (15% max, 150 days max) designed specifically so a future president would not need to stretch a wartime statute past its intent to do the same thing Nixon did. The tool that exists today because Congress was trying to prevent presidential overreach on tariffs is now itself the subject of a court ruling alleging presidential overreach. That is not a coincidence of phrasing — it is the same structural tension (executive trade authority vs. its statutory limits) recurring on a 55-year cycle, with the dollar system as the backdrop both times.

## Section 3: Mechanism B — The EU-US Bilateral Framework

This is the mechanism the June 16 briefing's "framework agreement levels" language was actually pointing at — and it is not the same instrument as Section 122 at all.

In August 2025, the US and EU issued a Joint Statement capping most US tariffs on EU goods at 15%, in exchange for the EU removing its own tariffs on US industrial goods and easing market access for US agricultural products. That bilateral arrangement is the "framework" the briefing meant. Here is what happened to it, on a timeline the June 16 briefing's own seven-day lookback window narrowly missed: the EU Council and European Parliament reached a provisional political agreement to implement the tariff elements of that Joint Statement on **May 20, 2026**, and the European Parliament gave its **final ratification vote on or around June 16–17, 2026** — the same 48 hours the G7 Évian summit was producing its own rare-earths declaration. The implementing regulation carries a sunset clause running to the **end of 2029**, plus an EU-side enforcement mechanism: if the US does not hold steel and aluminum derivative tariffs at or below 15% by December 31, 2026, the EU can suspend its own concessions in response.

**This is a separate legal track from Section 122, on a separate clock, ratified by a separate set of institutions, that just resolved — durably, through 2029 — in the same week the June 16 briefing was written.** The briefing's framing implicitly treated "no EU-US arrangement reached" as the live risk scenario for European industrials. That scenario had already stopped being live. The arrangement existed since August 2025; what completed on June 16–17 was its formal legislative implementation on the EU side.

## Section 4: Where the Conflation Happened — Answering the Opening Question

Return to the opening question: is the expiring thing the lever, or one input sharing a deadline with something else? Here is the precise answer for July 24.

**What actually expires on July 24:** the Section 122 global flat surcharge — an instrument that, by design, ignored the EU's bilateral cap and applied uniformly worldwide. **What does not expire on July 24, because it runs on its own 2029 sunset clause and just got ratified on a completely different legislative track:** the EU-specific 15% framework. The June 16 briefing's sentence — "tariffs snap back from framework agreement levels" if no arrangement is reached — describes a scenario (no EU arrangement) that was never actually contingent on July 24 at all. The EU arrangement's fate was being decided in Brussels and Strasbourg on its own calendar, independent of the Trade Act of 1974's 150-day clock. The briefing borrowed urgency from a date that belongs to a different mechanism.

This is the same category of error Lesson 127's Section 3 flagged in the Warsh protocol code — a label (`mentions_defense_spending_inflation`) that was narrower than the concept it claimed to represent, almost causing a misclassification. Here the error runs the other direction: a date label (`july_24_tariff_cliff`) was implicitly treated as broader than what it actually governs. It governs the global flat surcharge. It does not govern, and never did, whether the EU specifically gets a deal. **Practical consequence for the portfolio:** the European industrials underweight position's actual stated review triggers, on record in the investment log since April 23, are Brent price and European growth data — not a tariff date. That was already correct. The mistake would have been adding a new trigger (July 24) to a position that never needed one, on the strength of a conflated briefing sentence. We are not making that mistake. The position's existing rules stand untouched.

## Section 5: The Protocol — What July 24 Actually Determines

Having separated the two mechanisms, here is the decision protocol for the one that's genuinely still open: Section 122's fate.

```python
# PRE-COMMITTED INTERPRETATION PROTOCOL
# Section 122 Global Surcharge — Statutory Sunset, July 24, 2026
# Built: June 19, 2026, five weeks ahead of the event.
# Rule: revisable only before July 24. Not revisable based on the headlines that day.

def classify_section122_outcome(real_gap_occurs, replacement_mechanism_invoked,
                                  congress_formally_extends, fed_circuit_ruled_before_july24,
                                  fed_circuit_upheld_tariffs):
    """
    Returns a signal classification and the resulting action for the
    USD broad/dollar-index view and the Long TIPS (P-12) fiscal-dominance thesis.
    Does NOT touch the European industrials underweight — see Section 4.
    """

    if real_gap_occurs and not replacement_mechanism_invoked and not congress_formally_extends:
        signal = "CLEAN_LAPSE"       # genuine tariff reduction, no replacement
    elif replacement_mechanism_invoked and not congress_formally_extends:
        signal = "AUTHORITY_PIVOT"   # Feb 2026 playbook repeats — new vehicle, same rate
    elif congress_formally_extends:
        signal = "LEGISLATED_EXTENSION"  # most durable outcome, requires GOP-fracture-resistant majority
    else:
        signal = "JUDICIAL_RESOLUTION"   # Fed Circuit ruling itself becomes the controlling event

    # --- USD / broad dollar view ---
    if signal == "CLEAN_LAPSE":
        usd_action = "Tactical USD-negative data point — modest disinflationary signal, log only, no position"
    elif signal in ("AUTHORITY_PIVOT", "LEGISLATED_EXTENSION"):
        usd_action = "No change — confirms tariff wall is structural, not a 150-day experiment"
    else:
        usd_action = "Await Fed Circuit ruling text before any read — judicial outcome carries its own signal"

    # --- P-12 Long TIPS (fiscal dominance / structural inflation thesis) ---
    if signal == "CLEAN_LAPSE":
        p12_action = "Log as ONE negative data point — thesis rests on defense spending + energy floor + deglobalization broadly, not on this single tariff. No size change on one data point."
    elif signal in ("AUTHORITY_PIVOT", "LEGISLATED_EXTENSION"):
        p12_action = "NO ACTION — thesis reinforced. Tariff persistence via any mechanism confirms structural, not cyclical, inflation source."
    else:
        p12_action = "NO ACTION pending ruling — do not pre-position on appellate speculation"

    return {"signal": signal, "usd_action": usd_action, "p12_action": p12_action}
```

**What to literally watch between now and July 24, in priority order:**

1. **Does the administration pre-announce a replacement mechanism before July 24** — a new proclamation, an expanded Section 301 action, or additional Section 232 derivative-product designations — the way it did within hours of the IEEPA ruling in February? This is the single highest-probability outcome given the four-month-old precedent, and the easiest one to miss if you are only watching for "did Congress vote."
2. **Does the Federal Circuit rule on the merits before July 24?** If it upholds the CIT, the surcharge's legal foundation collapses independent of the statutory sunset — a stronger, more durable signal than a simple expiration. If it reverses, Section 122 survives both the calendar and the courtroom.
3. **Any Congressional vote, floor motion, or committee action specifically on Section 122 extension** — distinct from the general tariff debate, and worth tracking against the same GOP-fracture pattern that produced the Ukraine aid override in early June (Lesson 126/127 territory): a controversial blanket tariff is exactly the kind of measure where cross-party defection is plausible.
4. **EU effective tariff data specifically** — confirm whether EU imports are, in practice, already being assessed at the bilateral 15% framework rate or still carrying the Section 122 overlay. This is the one observable that would falsify Section 4's claim that the EU track is decoupled; if EU goods are still being hit by the global flat surcharge today, the "two mechanisms" read needs revision before July 24, not after.

## Databricks Angle

Today's research surfaced a data-modeling trap worth flagging before any tariff feature gets built into the pipeline: **"US tariff rate" is not a single scalar time series.** As of today there are at minimum two live, overlapping mechanisms (Section 122 global flat surcharge; EU-US bilateral 15% framework) with different statutory bases, different sunset dates, different governing institutions, and different country scope — plus Section 232 and Section 301 actions layered on specific product categories. A pipeline that ingests "US tariff rate" as one number per country per day will silently average over exactly the kind of distinction this lesson just spent five sections drawing out.

**Concrete build recommendation:** extend the `fomc_signal_log` pattern from Lesson 126 with a new table, `prospectra.portfolio.trade_mechanism_log`, with one row per active legal tariff mechanism rather than per country:

| mechanism | statutory_basis | scope | effective_rate | sunset_date | legal_status | last_reviewed |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Section 122 global surcharge | Trade Act of 1974 §122 | All countries (minus §232-covered goods, USMCA, Annex II) | 15% | 2026-07-24 (statutory; pending appeal) | Federal Circuit stay in effect; CIT ruled against, on appeal | 2026-06-19 |
| EU-US bilateral framework | Aug 2025 Joint Statement, implemented via EU regulation | EU goods only | 15% (cap) | 2029-12-31 (sunset clause) | Ratified, EU Parliament, June 2026 | 2026-06-19 |

This table structure forces the question this lesson asked in Section 4 — "which mechanism actually governs this country/product?" — to be answered explicitly per row, rather than collapsed into one rate per country that would have hidden exactly the conflation this lesson just corrected.
