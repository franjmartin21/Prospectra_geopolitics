## Opening Framing Question

*If fiscal dominance is ultimately a story about the slow erosion of sovereign balance sheet credibility — governments that must inflate rather than default, central banks that cannot raise rates without triggering debt crises — then what is the right asset to own when the unit of account itself is being debased? And why has gold risen 65% since 2025 even as "measured" inflation stabilized? What is the market actually pricing?*

---

## 1. The Central Claim: Real Assets Are a Credibility Hedge, Not Just an Inflation Hedge

The conventional framing is wrong: **commodities and real assets are not primarily an inflation hedge in a fiscal dominance regime. They are a policy-credibility hedge.**

This distinction matters enormously for portfolio construction.

An inflation hedge responds to *realized* consumer price increases. A credibility hedge responds to *deteriorating confidence* that the monetary-fiscal framework will be able to maintain the real value of paper claims. These can come apart dramatically:

- In 2021–2022, inflation was high but policy credibility was intact (markets believed central banks would eventually act). Gold underperformed real inflation.
- In 2025–2026, measured inflation stabilized but gold rose 65%. Why? Markets began pricing the **term premium expansion** — the growing risk that governments cannot afford the real rates needed to fully restore price stability. The credibility of the framework, not just the CPI print, cracked.

This is what Sargent and Wallace's 1981 paper ("Some Unpleasant Monetarist Arithmetic") described theoretically: when fiscal deficits are large enough, monetary tightening today only guarantees monetary expansion tomorrow. Inflation becomes deferred, not suppressed.

**In a fiscal dominance regime, real assets are the market's way of owning something that cannot be issued by a government.**

---

## 2. The Taxonomy of Real Assets in a Debasement Environment

Not all real assets respond identically. Understanding the mechanism behind each class is essential:

### Gold: The Monetary Metal

Gold's role is the oldest and most documented. It is not primarily an industrial commodity (though industrial demand exists); it is a **monetary reserve without sovereign counterparty risk**.

Key characteristics in a fiscal dominance regime:
- **Central bank demand becomes strategic.** Since 2022, central banks — particularly from EM countries seeking to reduce dollar dependence — have been net buyers at record rates. This is not cyclical. It is structural: countries are building reserve assets that cannot be sanctioned, frozen, or inflated away by a foreign power. (Lesson 437 covered the dollar's vulnerability; gold is the direct alternative.)
- **The real yield relationship weakens.** Gold historically traded inversely with US real yields (higher real yields = higher opportunity cost of holding gold). This relationship has broken down in 2025–2026. Gold rises even as real yields stay positive because the **fiscal risk premium** now dominates. Markets are pricing the risk that today's positive real yield cannot be sustained without a debt crisis.
- **Gold is now a geopolitical asset.** Russia's reserves were frozen in 2022. Every sovereign that observed that event now knows that dollar reserves are conditionally safe — safe until they are not. Gold in your own vault is unconditionally yours. This is a one-way ratchet on demand.

### Energy Commodities (Oil, Natural Gas, Uranium)

Energy is the foundational input of industrial economies. In a fiscal dominance environment:
- **Nominal energy prices tend to rise** as currencies debase, preserving the real value of energy-exporting nations' output.
- **Underinvestment risk compounds the fiscal risk.** Years of ESG-driven underinvestment in fossil fuel capex, combined with chronic underinvestment in nuclear, create supply constraints that are **independent of demand**. This is a supply-side price floor.
- **Energy assets are geopolitically scarce.** You cannot print more Permian Basin. Supply shocks in a fiscal dominance world hit harder because central banks are constrained from the aggressive tightening that would normally crush demand.
- **Uranium is the outlier:** it trades on nuclear buildout expectations (structural decades-long demand growth) more than on debasement directly, but the thesis converges: energy security in a fragmented world drives sovereign-backed procurement programs.

### Industrial Metals (Copper, Lithium, Rare Earths)

The energy transition is a commodity supercycle mechanism. Every solar panel, EV battery, and wind turbine requires materials that must be physically mined. Key dynamics:
- **Copper is the oil of electrification.** Demand forecasts show structural deficits beginning in this decade; no amount of monetary policy changes the mining cycle.
- **Critical minerals are geopolitically concentrated.** China controls 60–80% of refining capacity for most battery metals. The US-China decoupling creates friend-shoring investment mandates that are price-insensitive — governments will pay a premium to secure domestic or allied supply.
- **In a fiscal dominance world**, these capex-intensive projects become harder to finance with cheap credit. Supply tightens further. Real prices rise.

### Real Estate and Infrastructure

Physical productive assets — toll roads, ports, power grids, data centers — have two attractive properties in fiscal dominance:
1. **Pricing power:** revenues are typically indexed to inflation or repriced frequently
2. **Sovereign-backed cash flows:** many infrastructure assets have government-backed offtake agreements, creating the paradox that they benefit from the same fiscal expansion that creates fiscal dominance

The risk: rising rates increase the discount rate applied to long-duration cash flows. In a fiscal dominance regime where rates cannot rise too far, this risk is capped — the very dynamic that creates fiscal dominance (rate suppression) also makes infrastructure more attractive on a relative basis.

## 3. Historical Grounding: The 1970s as the Reference Template

The most comparable historical episode is the 1970s in the United States:
- **Fiscal context:** LBJ's "guns and butter" spending created structural deficits. Nixon's 1971 dollar/gold decoupling removed the last nominal anchor.
- **Result:** Gold rose from $35/oz in 1971 to $850 in 1980 (a 24x move). Oil prices rose 10x between 1973 and 1980. Real estate held value.
- **What failed:** Bonds were catastrophic in real terms ("certificates of confiscation"). Cash was destroyed. Equities were flat to negative in real terms for most of the decade.
- **What worked:** Physical commodities, energy companies, inflation-linked assets.

**The crucial difference today:** The 1970s ended with Volcker's 20% rates because the US had the fiscal space to sustain them. In 2026, the US debt-to-GDP ratio is dramatically higher. A Volcker moment is arithmetically harder. The fiscal dominance regime may prove more durable than the 1970s episode.

---

## 4. The Credibility Crack: Why 2025 Marked a Phase Transition

Gold's 65% rise in 2025 — its strongest year since 1979 — is not noise. It is the market's signal that a phase transition occurred. Specifically:

1. **Term premium expansion:** Long-term US Treasury yields rose not because inflation rose but because bond buyers demanded more compensation for holding long-duration sovereign paper. This is the classic early signal of fiscal credibility erosion.
2. **Central bank gold buying at record levels:** Official sector demand (central banks) exceeded 1,000 tonnes per year for the third consecutive year. This is not tactical — it is strategic reserve diversification away from dollars.
3. **Real yields rose but gold rose faster:** The old model (gold rises when real yields fall) broke down. This is the tell that the market moved from pricing *inflation* risk to pricing *solvency/credibility* risk.
4. **EM currency pressures accelerated:** As Lesson 437 covered, the currencies of high-deficit EM nations came under severe pressure. Gold outperformed every major EM currency in local terms.

The market is now pricing a world where the real value of sovereign promises — bonds, currencies — is less certain than the real value of physical assets that require labor, capital, and time to produce.

## 5. Investment Implications

### Directional Views (6–18 Month Horizon)

**Gold:**
- **Long bias maintained.** Not a trade — a structural position. The central bank demand floor is set. The credibility premium is unlikely to fully deflate unless a credible fiscal consolidation path emerges (low probability across major economies near-term).
- **Sizing:** Gold should be a strategic allocation, not a tactical trade. 5–15% of a diversified portfolio in a fiscal dominance regime is within historical norms for preservation-oriented allocations.
- **Watch:** Any credible shift in fiscal trajectory (spending cuts, revenue increases) in major economies is the key thesis risk. US elections, Congressional budget deals, EU fiscal compact enforcement — these are the falsifying conditions.

**Energy Commodities:**
- **Oil:** Geopolitical supply risk (Iran, Russia, OPEC+ cohesion) supports prices. Demand destruction from the energy transition is a slow-moving counter-pressure; not yet dominant. **Long oil producers and integrated majors** with strong free cash flow and dividend yield.
- **Uranium:** Structural nuclear buildout demand makes this a multi-year thesis independent of fiscal dynamics. **Long uranium miners and physical uranium trusts.**
- **Natural gas:** European energy security mandates (post-Russia) create durable LNG demand. **Long LNG infrastructure and exporters.**

**Industrial Metals / Critical Minerals:**
- **Copper:** Long-horizon bull thesis. Supply constraints + electrification demand = structural deficit. Trade through copper miners or physical copper ETFs.
- **Lithium:** Volatile near-term (oversupply corrections ongoing) but long-term structural. Wait for supply cycle clarity before sizing.
- **Rare earths:** The geopolitical dimension (China control) makes this more policy-driven than price-driven. Track US/EU domestic refining investment as the signal.

**Real Assets / Infrastructure:**
- **Long** if you can access it. Private infrastructure is effectively unavailable to retail investors. For liquid exposure: utilities with regulated pricing power, midstream energy infrastructure (MLPs), REITs in data center / industrial / logistics segments.

**What to Underweight:**
- **Nominal long-duration bonds** in fiscally distressed sovereigns. The real return math is adverse in a fiscal dominance regime. Duration is a liability.
- **Cash in debasement-facing currencies.** Cash preserves nominal value; it cannot preserve real value against a credibility-eroding sovereign.

## 6. The Nuance: This Is Not an Apocalypse Trade

A critical discipline note for Bolo:

**Fiscal dominance does not mean collapse.** It means slow, grinding erosion of real value in paper assets, and slow, grinding appreciation of real assets — over years and decades, with enormous volatility along the way.

The investors who made real money in the 1970s were not the ones who predicted the exact trajectory. They were the ones who held energy companies and gold through multiple 30–40% drawdowns because they understood the structural regime.

**This is a portfolio tilt, not an all-in bet.** Overweighting real assets versus financial assets (bonds, cash) by 10–20 percentage points relative to a conventional allocation — maintained with discipline over 5–10 years — is the thesis. Not betting the portfolio on gold at $3,000/oz being $10,000 next year.

The falsifying conditions remain:
1. A credible global fiscal consolidation wave (austerity + structural reform at scale)
2. A deflationary shock (pandemic-style demand collapse) that overwhelms the fiscal dominance dynamic
3. Technological deflation (AGI-driven productivity) so powerful it offsets monetary expansion

None of these is impossible. All of them deserve monitoring. None looks dominant in the current regime.
