## Opening Question

*Lesson 342 covered the scenario where a client challenges your published Russia GRI score after a Treasury OFAC announcement — and you are right to stand behind the score because the methodology does not yet meet its own trigger threshold.*

*Now consider the harder scenario. Three days after that exchange, three more OFAC SDN designations drop — this time targeting Russia's central bank correspondent infrastructure. You run the updated inputs through your financial risk sub-component. The score moves from 74 to 81.*

*You need to revise the score. The same client who questioned you at 74 is now watching.*

**Here is the question: Is publishing a score revision a demonstration of analytical rigor — or an admission that the previous score was wrong?**

The answer is: it depends entirely on how you have built your revision protocol. A score change communicated correctly signals that the framework is working — it is updating on new information exactly as designed. A score change communicated poorly signals that you did not know what you were doing at 74. The analytical content is identical. The institutional outcome is not.

## I. The Fundamental Distinction: Revision vs. Error

Before building the protocol, you need to be precise about what kind of score change has occurred. There are three categories, and they require completely different responses:

### Category 1 — Trigger-Based Revision (Signal Working as Designed)
**Definition:** A new event has occurred that meets the pre-specified trigger conditions in your published methodology, and the score is updating in response.

**Example:** Russia GRI moves from 74 to 81 after SDN designations targeting central bank correspondents. Your methodology explicitly lists "designation of major state financial institutions or central bank infrastructure" as a Tier 1 trigger requiring 48-hour score review. This event qualifies. You updated within 48 hours. The score moved as a result of new inputs, not a reassessment of old ones.

**Client communication:** Proactive, transparent, confident. You are not correcting an error. You are demonstrating that the framework does exactly what you said it would do.

### Category 2 — Methodology Refinement (Framework Improvement)
**Definition:** No new geopolitical event has occurred, but you have identified a flaw, gap, or calibration error in the methodology itself — an input you weighted incorrectly, a sub-component you omitted, a proxy variable that was measuring the wrong thing.

**Example:** You discover that your political instability sub-component was using a governance index with a 2-year data lag, making it systematically insensitive to rapidly evolving situations. You recalibrate, and several existing scores shift.

**Client communication:** Transparent, methodological, forward-looking. This requires a methodology update notice, a revised score set, and an explanation of why the change improves accuracy. It does not require an apology — every serious analytical framework iterates — but it requires intellectual honesty about what the flaw was.

### Category 3 — Analytical Error (You Got It Wrong)
**Definition:** You made an error — a data input error, a calculation error, or a judgment call that, in retrospect, was clearly wrong given information that was available at the time of original publication.

**Example:** Your Russia financial risk sub-component failed to capture an SDN designation that had already been published when you set the score at 74. Not because the trigger threshold wasn't met — but because someone on your team missed the OFAC notice.

**Client communication:** Immediate notification, clear acknowledgment, corrected score, revised methodology for how your team monitors OFAC releases going forward. This is the most uncomfortable conversation. It is also the one that most determines your long-term institutional relationships. Firms that catch and acknowledge their own errors without being forced to are trusted. Firms that only revise when clients point out mistakes are eventually dropped.

**The single most important skill in institutional research is the ability to distinguish Category 1 from Category 3 — and to communicate each type correctly without conflating them.**

## II. The Score Revision Document: Architecture

Every GRI score revision — regardless of category — should be accompanied by a formal revision document. This is the artifact that separates a research operation from a newsletter. It should be brief (one page maximum), precisely structured, and appended to the standing GRI country file.

**Prospectra GRI Score Revision Notice — Required Fields:**

```
PROSPECTRA GEOPOLITICAL RISK INDEX
Score Revision Notice — [Country Name]

Previous Score: [X/100] — Published [Date]
Revised Score:  [Y/100] — Effective [Date]
Revision Type:  [Trigger-Based / Methodology Refinement / Analytical Error]

Trigger Event (if applicable):
[2–3 sentences describing the event that triggered the revision.]

Sub-Component Impact:
[Table showing which sub-components changed, previous weight, new score, delta]

Methodology Reference:
[Citation to the specific trigger condition in the published methodology document that
authorized this revision, or the specific flaw identified in a Methodology Refinement.]

Standing Coverage Note:
[Revised forward-looking assessment in 2–3 sentences. What is the GRI watching
for the next potential revision trigger? What would cause the score to move further?]

Issued by: Prospectra Geopolitical Risk Intelligence
Contact: ceo@prospectra.earth
```

This document serves three functions simultaneously:
1. **Client-facing communication** — they receive this alongside the revised score
2. **Internal audit trail** — it becomes part of the GRI's version history, accessible for any future methodology review
3. **Track record documentation** — over time, these revision notices build the evidence base that Prospectra's framework is responsive and accurate

## III. The Client Notification Protocol: Timing and Channel

Score revisions require proactive outreach. Do not wait for the client to notice. Do not mention it quietly in the next weekly briefing. Send a dedicated notification.

**Timing rules:**
- Trigger-Based Revision: Notify within 24 hours of determining the trigger has been met. Do not wait for the next scheduled publication.
- Methodology Refinement: Notify with the full methodology update document before or simultaneously with revised scores. Give clients 48 hours advance notice that scores are changing and why.
- Analytical Error: Notify immediately upon discovery, before the correction is even finalized. A message saying "we identified an error and are correcting it within 24 hours" arrives better than a corrected score that appears without explanation.

**Channel rules:**
- Tier 1 subscribers (institutional, >$5K/year): Direct email from ceo@prospectra.earth, personal tone. Not a system notification.
- All subscribers simultaneously: Follow-up standardized notice within 24 hours covering the same content.

**Tone guidance:**
The revision notification should never read defensively. The defensive version says: *"While the original score of 74 reflected our assessment at the time..."* — this sounds like a cover. The confident version says: *"The Russia GRI has been revised to 81/100 following three new SDN designations on February 14 that met the financial risk Tier 1 trigger condition in our published methodology. Here is exactly what changed and why."* The confident version is shorter, clearer, and projects more credibility — because it is anchored to a process, not to protecting a prior judgment.

## IV. The Compounding Value of a Clean Revision History

Here is the counterintuitive insight that most early-stage research firms miss: **a well-maintained revision history is one of Prospectra's most valuable long-term assets.**

Every institutional research buyer is buying track record. They want evidence that your analytical framework actually does something — that it responds to the right events, in the right direction, at the right time. A GRI score that never changes regardless of world events is worthless. A GRI score with a clean, documented, methodologically coherent revision history is demonstrably doing its job.

When Prospectra has 12 months of GRI revision notices — each one tied to a specific trigger event, each one showing the correct directional response, each one communicated proactively before the client noticed — that history is the sales document for the next institutional subscriber. It is more persuasive than any pitch deck.

The practical implication: **archive every revision notice permanently.** Not just the final score. The notice, the date, the trigger, the sub-component impact, and (eventually) what the market did in the 30/60/90 days following the revision. That last column — market outcome — is how the GRI eventually gets priced as a signal generator, not just a risk descriptor.

## V. When the Client Disagrees With the Revision

Occasionally an institutional client will push back on a revision — arguing that you should not have revised the score, or that it should have moved differently. This is healthy. It is the point where Prospectra's analytical independence gets tested.

The correct response structure:
1. Acknowledge their view directly: "You're raising a legitimate point about [specific sub-component or weighting decision]."
2. Explain the methodology as written: "Our financial risk sub-component treats SDN designations of sovereign financial infrastructure as a Tier 1 trigger because [reason]. That principle was published in the methodology document before this event occurred."
3. Offer the engagement: "If you believe the weighting or trigger threshold is miscalibrated, that's a substantive methodological question we're happy to discuss in our next quarterly review call. We take that input seriously for future methodology iterations."

What you never do: revise the score in response to client pressure. A score that moves because a client wants it to has ceased to be an analytical product. It is a client relationship management tool. Institutional clients — the good ones — understand this. A research firm that holds its methodology under client pressure is one worth renewing.

## Investment Implications

The score revision protocol connects directly to how geopolitical risk is priced in markets — and specifically to how mispricing occurs:

**The revision lag problem:** Markets price geopolitical risk based on information flow. When a credible analytical source (whether an institutional research firm or an internal risk team) is slow to update published risk assessments following material events, markets can remain mispriced for longer than the underlying fundamentals warrant. The practical example: if major institutional subscribers are using a GRI that is still at 74 for Russia when the true updated score is 81, their risk-adjusted position sizing is systematically off. That creates a predictable mispricing that corrects when the risk assessment updates.

**What to watch (asset class implications):**
- **EM sovereign debt spreads:** Slow-updating risk assessments are most consequential here. Spread widening tends to be lumpy and delayed relative to the underlying geopolitical trigger — creating both opportunity (buying too-wide spreads after overreaction) and risk (holding spread positions into revisions).
- **Commodity futures:** Energy and commodity prices are more sensitive to real-time information flow than to published risk indices — but index revisions create institutional rebalancing flows that affect basis between spot and futures.
- **FX carry trades:** Institutional risk assessments directly feed position limits on EM FX carry books. A GRI revision that triggers a risk limit breach forces systematic unwinding — often at prices that overshoot the fundamental revision.

**Directional view:** Building a GRI with a clean, transparent, real-time revision protocol is not just an analytical integrity choice. It is a structural edge: Prospectra's subscribers are working with more current risk assessments than the industry average, which over 6–18 month horizons generates measurable portfolio impact relative to firms using stale published indices.

## Databricks Angle

**The GRI Revision Log as a Structured Dataset**

Every revision notice described in this lesson is a structured event: country, date, previous score, new score, revision type, trigger event, sub-component delta. Over 12–24 months, this becomes a labeled dataset with significant analytical value:

**Pipeline idea:** `gri_revision_log` Delta table with schema:
```
country | revision_date | previous_score | revised_score | revision_type 
| trigger_event_id (FK to gdelt_events) | sub_component_deltas (JSON) 
| days_to_market_reaction | asset_class_movement_30d
```

The `days_to_market_reaction` and `asset_class_movement_30d` fields are filled retroactively from the market data pipeline — and over time, they allow Prospectra to measure whether GRI revisions are leading or lagging market moves. That analysis is the foundation of a formal signal validation study: the document that tells institutional subscribers not just that the GRI *should* work, but that it empirically *does* work.

**Relevant datasets:**
- GDELT for trigger event linkage (each SDN designation, election result, or conflict escalation that triggered a revision should map to a GDELT event ID)
- OFAC SDN list (direct API: `ofac.treasury.gov`) for financial risk sub-component inputs
- FRED for EM sovereign yield data (signal validation: did spreads widen when GRI revised upward?)
