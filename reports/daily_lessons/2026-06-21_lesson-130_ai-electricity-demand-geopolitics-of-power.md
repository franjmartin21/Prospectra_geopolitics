## Opening Question

*Global data center electricity consumption is on pace to approach 1,050 terawatt-hours in 2026. If data centers were a country, that load would rank fifth in the world — bigger than Russia, smaller than India. US data center IT load is on a path that roughly doubles from ~80 GW to ~150 GW between 2025 and 2028, depending whose model you trust. Microsoft has paid to restart a reactor at Three Mile Island. Amazon bought a data center campus in Pennsylvania specifically because it sits next to a nuclear plant. Utility interconnection queues — the waiting line to plug a new project into the grid — now stretch years, not months.*

**Here is the question: when every hyperscaler on earth is chasing the same scarce input, and that input is not chips, what is it — and who actually controls the supply?**

The export-control architecture you studied in Lesson 56 was built on the assumption that the chokepoint in AI is silicon: EUV lithography, HBM memory, CoWoS packaging. That chokepoint is real. But it is no longer the binding one. The binding constraint in 2026 is electrons — specifically, firm, dispatchable, gigawatt-scale electricity, delivered to a specific patch of land, on a timeline measured in quarters rather than years. That is a fundamentally different kind of scarcity than a chip fab. You cannot build a new transmission interconnection with a subsidy program the way you can subsidize a fab with the CHIPS Act. Power infrastructure has site-specific physics, multi-year permitting, and — increasingly — a geopolitical pecking order of who gets electrons first.

## I. The Decoupling: From Chip-Constrained to Power-Constrained

For three years, the dominant AI narrative was about compute supply: GPU allocation, export controls on advanced chips to China (Lesson 56), and the chip war as the defining axis of US-China techno-bloc competition (Lesson 57). That story is not wrong, but it has been overtaken by a second constraint that nobody fully modeled in 2023: **even with unlimited chips, you cannot run them without power, and power cannot be manufactured on a chip-fab timeline.**

The numbers illustrate the speed of the shift. Data center electricity demand has grown at roughly 12% per year since 2017 — more than four times the growth rate of global electricity consumption overall. US data center demand is projected to climb from roughly 76 GW in 2026 to over 130 GW by 2030. To reliably serve even the next 85 GW of incremental demand, the US grid needs roughly 100 GW of total generation capacity in reserve — but during the most constrained hours, national excess capacity is only about 70 GW. That gap is not a financing problem. It is a physical one: gas turbine order books are sold out years forward, nuclear reactors take a decade to build from scratch, and high-voltage transmission lines face permitting fights that can run longer than the data center itself takes to construct.

The result: site selection for new AI infrastructure is no longer driven primarily by tax incentives, fiber connectivity, or even chip supply. It is driven by **where firm power already exists or can be secured fastest** — and increasingly, by which company can lock up a nuclear plant's entire output before a competitor does.

## II. Historical Grounding: France, 1974, and the Last Time a Power Buildout Was an Emergency

The closest 20th-century analog to today's power scramble is not American — it is the **Messmer Plan**, France's response to the 1973 oil shock.

Before 1973, France generated most of its electricity from imported oil — a direct geopolitical vulnerability once OPEC weaponized supply (a preview of the energy-security logic in Lesson 4). Prime Minister Pierre Messmer's government made a decision in 1974 that looks almost reckless in hindsight: build out nuclear power at a pace no country had attempted before or has matched since. France constructed 56 reactors in roughly 15 years, converting from an oil-dependent grid to one that is still today 65–70% nuclear — the highest share of any major economy.

The mechanism that made it possible was **state-directed urgency**: a sovereign government, acting on an energy-security crisis, mobilized capital, regulatory approval, and skilled labor at a speed normal peacetime planning never achieves. It is the cleanest historical proof that a power buildout most people assume takes decades can be compressed into 15 years when a state treats it as existential.

**The structural difference today is who is doing the mobilizing.** France's nuclear buildout was a sovereign decision, financed by the state, justified by national energy security. The 2026 AI power buildout is being driven by Microsoft, Amazon, Google, and Meta — corporations financing nuclear restarts and gas turbines off their own balance sheets, justified by quarterly capex guidance, not national resilience. That is a genuinely new variable: the entity bearing the risk of a multi-decade power infrastructure bet is now a hyperscaler optimizing for shareholder returns and AI revenue timelines, not a state optimizing for the next fifty years. If the AI capex cycle turns — a real possibility this project has not yet had reason to dismiss — these are also the entities left holding twenty-year power purchase agreements signed at the top of the cycle.

## III. The Concrete Evidence: Reactors Are Already Being Restarted for This

This is not a forecast. It is already happening:

- **Microsoft / Three Mile Island (Crane Clean Energy Center):** Microsoft signed a 20-year power purchase agreement to restart the shuttered Unit 1 reactor at Three Mile Island, the site of the 1979 partial meltdown that froze US nuclear construction for a generation. The plant has been renamed and is targeting restart around 2027 — financed entirely to serve Microsoft's data center load, not the regional grid generally.
- **Amazon / Susquehanna:** Amazon paid roughly $650 million for a Pennsylvania data center campus specifically because it sits adjacent to the Susquehanna nuclear plant, securing direct, behind-the-meter power access rather than competing in the broader interconnection queue.
- **The Gulf states are doing the sovereign version of the same trade.** The Trump administration has cleared billions of dollars in Gulf investment into US AI and chip firms, and the Interior Department signed a memorandum of understanding with Abu Dhabi explicitly linking AI cooperation to energy cooperation. Saudi Arabia and the UAE are building gigawatt-scale AI data center campuses powered by stranded gas and, increasingly, nuclear ambitions of their own — a direct extension of the sovereign-wealth hedging behavior covered in Lesson 110 (Gulf States Hedging), now redirected at compute infrastructure rather than financial assets.
- **China is moving on the small modular reactor (SMR) track:** the Linglong One SMR is scheduled for commercial operation in the first half of 2026, part of a broader Chinese strategy to pair grid buildout with AI compute ambitions domestically, sidestepping the chip export-control regime by racing ahead on the power side instead.

BloombergNEF expects roughly 15 nuclear reactors to come online globally in 2026 alone, adding close to 12 GW of capacity — the fastest pace of nuclear additions in decades, and explicitly attributed to AI-driven demand rather than traditional grid decarbonization planning.

## IV. Why This Is Not a New Topic — It Is the Missing Foundation Under Two Open Positions

This project already holds two positions whose stated thesis depends directly on what this lesson just documented:

**Copper (COPX/SCCO/FCX, opened April 24, 2026):** The position-opening note explicitly named "AI data center demand confirmed as new primary demand driver, rivaling electrification" as a pillar of the thesis — but the lesson library never built out *why* that's true. Section I and III above are that foundation: data center buildout requires enormous quantities of copper for power distribution, cooling infrastructure, and the grid interconnection upgrades utilities are now racing to complete. Every gigawatt of new data center load requires roughly 5,000–10,000 tons of copper in associated grid and facility infrastructure, by industry rule-of-thumb estimates. This is a demand-side confirmation, not a change to conviction or sizing.

**Uranium (URA/CCJ/NXE, opened May 11, 2026):** The position was opened on a **supply-side** thesis — Sahel instability threatening Mali/Niger uranium output (Lesson 31, Lesson 115). This lesson documents an entirely separate, **demand-side** catalyst: AI-driven nuclear restarts and new-build commitments (Three Mile Island, Susquehanna, 15 reactors globally in 2026 alone) that have nothing to do with West African supply risk. A thesis resting on two independent catalysts pointing the same direction is structurally stronger than one resting on a single catalyst — this is the same independence-testing logic Lesson 129 used to stress-test the Hormuz framework, applied here in the opposite direction: two unrelated mechanisms reinforcing the same call, rather than two "independent" legs turning out to share a root cause.

**No position sizing or conviction change is being made today.** This lesson exists to document the demand-side mechanism underpinning both positions with the rigor the original opening notes did not have time to build out. Flag for the next portfolio review (following the L122/L123 cadence): the AI-power-demand catalyst should be added explicitly to the uranium position's thesis statement in the investment log, alongside the existing Sahel supply-risk language.
