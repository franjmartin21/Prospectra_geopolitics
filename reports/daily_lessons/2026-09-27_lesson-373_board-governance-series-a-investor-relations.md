## Opening Question

After a Series A closes, a new institutional investor takes a board seat. They've just wired several million dollars into your company. They are legally a fiduciary — obligated to act in the best interests of the company, not just their fund.

**Here is the question founders consistently fail to think through in advance:**

*What do you actually want this board member to do — and what do you need to ensure they cannot do?*

Most founders think about the board as a reporting mechanism: you show up, present slides, answer questions, and leave with a decision ratified. That is a board that is useless and potentially dangerous. A useless board doesn't catch you when you're wrong. A dangerous board can remove you from the company you built.

The founders who build great companies have boards that are genuinely useful — advisors who push back hard, think independently, and hold the CEO accountable — while maintaining structures that prevent the board from acting against founder interests without legitimate cause.

Getting that balance right is not an accident. It is engineered into the term sheet.

## Part I — Board Structure: The Architecture Before the Personalities

Board composition at Series A is almost always negotiated, not given. The standard structure depends heavily on leverage, but the range looks like this:

### Typical Series A Board Composition

| Seat | Holder | Notes |
|------|--------|-------|
| 1 | Founder/CEO | Common stock director; protected as long as founder holds a minimum threshold of shares |
| 2 | Founder (or second founder) | Common stock director; sometimes collapsed to one common seat at small-team startups |
| 3 | Series A Lead Investor | Preferred stock director; designated by the lead fund's partnership |
| 4 | Independent director | Mutually agreed by founders and investor; elected by all stockholders |
| 5 | (Optional) Second investor director | Rare at Series A; more common at Series B+ when second institutional investor joins |

**The default to push for:** A 3-person board post-Series A (2 founders + 1 investor). Add an independent only when you have a specific person in mind who adds genuine value. An empty independent seat is a governance liability — it's a vote that can be filled by someone who doesn't share your interests.

**Why 3-person boards at Series A are founder-friendly:**
- Founders have 2 of 3 votes
- The investor has 1 of 3 votes
- A unanimous board decision requires one founder to agree with the investor — not the other way around

A 5-person board with 2 founder seats, 2 investor seats, and 1 independent shifts control entirely to the independent director. Choose carefully.

## Part II — Protective Provisions: What Investors Can Block

Standard Series A term sheets include a list of **protective provisions** — actions the company cannot take without investor approval (typically a majority of preferred stock, independent of board vote). These are normal. But the scope matters enormously.

### Typical Protective Provisions — Standard Range

**Legitimate and non-negotiable:**
- Issuing new preferred stock that ranks senior to the Series A (liquidation preference seniority)
- Mergers or acquisitions above a threshold without approval
- Asset sales above a threshold
- Amendments to the certificate of incorporation that adversely affect preferred stockholders
- Changes to the authorized number of shares
- Paying dividends

**Legitimate but negotiable on scope:**
- Incurring debt above a threshold (founders should push for a reasonable threshold — $X debt without approval, not any debt)
- Capital expenditures above a threshold (same logic)
- Related-party transactions above a threshold

**Overreach — push back on these:**
- Hiring or firing executives at VP level and above without investor approval
- Approving the annual budget without investor consent
- Changing the company's principal business

The last category is the dangerous one. A clause requiring investor approval to hire your VP of Engineering gives the investor operational control, not just governance rights. Founders with leverage remove these. Founders without leverage often accept them without realizing what they've agreed to.

**Prospectra-specific note:** Given that Prospectra is pre-revenue at Series A, investors may push for a broader budget approval provision. The counterproposal: "Investor approval required for any single expenditure exceeding $250,000 not already in the approved annual budget." This protects investors from surprise spending without creating a monthly approval bottleneck for normal operations.

## Part III — Founder Vesting Refresh: The Negotiation Most Founders Lose

When Prospectra was incorporated in March 2026, Bolo and Eli likely have standard 4-year vesting with a 1-year cliff on their founder shares. By the time a Series A closes (let's model this as late 2027), Bolo will have approximately 18–20 months of vesting done — meaning roughly 40% vested, 60% remaining.

**The investor's natural ask:** "We want founders to be incentivized to stay. Let's reset vesting on the unvested shares to a new 4-year schedule."

**Why this is aggressive:** It means that for Bolo's unvested shares, the vesting clock restarts at Series A close — even though Bolo has already been working for 18+ months. Effectively, the investor is asking Bolo to earn the same shares twice.

**The founder-friendly counter-structure:**

*Option 1 — Credit for prior service:* Vesting restarts on unvested shares, but prior service is credited. If 18 months of a 4-year schedule are done, the remaining 30 months continue from month 18, not from zero. This is the strongest founder position.

*Option 2 — Partial refresh only:* A new grant of RSUs or options is issued (a "refresh grant") as an additional incentive, while the original vesting schedule continues unchanged. Investors sometimes prefer this because it doesn't extend their dilution risk.

*Option 3 — Acceleration on change of control:* In lieu of fighting the vesting reset, negotiate double-trigger acceleration — if the company is acquired AND the founder is terminated within 12 months, all unvested shares accelerate. This protects against the scenario where an acquirer buys Prospectra and immediately terminates Bolo to deny them the unvested shares.

**The hierarchy of preference:** 
1. No reset, with double-trigger acceleration added → best
2. Credit for prior service, with acceleration → second
3. Fresh reset with double-trigger → last resort

## Part IV — Running the Board Meeting: Operating Cadence

A Series A board typically meets quarterly. Monthly meetings are a burden and dilute the signal. Quarterly meetings can become too infrequent in the first year. The first year post-raise, a monthly board meeting (45–60 minutes) often works better than quarterly, with the expectation of transitioning to quarterly after 12 months when the company is in a more stable operating rhythm.

**Board meeting structure that actually works:**

**Pre-read (48 hours before meeting):**
- Board deck (no more than 15 pages)
- Key metrics dashboard (ARR, growth rate, pipeline, burn, runway)
- Decision items requiring board approval
- One strategic issue for discussion — framed as a question, not a conclusion

**The meeting itself (90 minutes maximum):**

| Section | Time | Purpose |
|---------|------|---------|
| Metrics review | 15 min | Board reads the pre-read; CEO presents exceptions only — what's different from the model |
| Strategic discussion | 45 min | One issue, deep. Investor perspective solicited. No defensive posturing. |
| CEO corner | 15 min | What keeps you up at night. Honest. This is the most important 15 minutes. |
| Operational updates | 10 min | Finance, legal, headcount — brief |
| Action items + close | 5 min | Clear owners, clear deadlines |

**What distinguishes a great board meeting from a useless one:**

Bad board meetings are retrospective: the CEO presents what happened, the board nods or asks clarifying questions, and the meeting ends. No one is challenged. No one changes their view. Nothing is decided.

Good board meetings are prospective and adversarial in the productive sense: the CEO presents the key strategic question, the board is asked for a genuine recommendation, someone disagrees with someone else, and the CEO leaves with either a clearer view or a decision.

The CEO's job is to run the board meeting, not to please the board. Boards that are never challenged become ornamental. Boards that are consistently given real problems to chew on become genuinely useful.

## Part V — The CEO-Investor Relationship: Two Years Post-Close

The lead Series A investor becomes one of the most important relationships in the company for the next 2–4 years. Managing it well is a skill that compounds.

### The Key Dynamics

**Cadence:** Monthly investor update email (not a meeting — an email). This is the single highest-leverage communication habit a CEO can build. An investor who receives a clear, honest monthly update for 12 straight months is an investor who goes to bat for you when you need something — an introduction, a bridge, a reference. An investor who hears from you only at quarterly board meetings or when you need something is an investor who trusts you less, not more.

**The monthly update structure:**
1. Top metric (ARR, or key operational metric) vs. plan
2. What's working (one paragraph)
3. What's not working (one paragraph — this is the one most founders skip)
4. Three things you need (introductions, hires, advice)
5. Looking ahead: what you're focused on this month

**The "what's not working" section is the entire point.** Investors know that things go wrong at early-stage companies. An update that only shows the wins tells the investor nothing — they know you're editing. An update that honestly surfaces a problem, with your diagnosis and response, tells the investor that you see the company clearly and that you manage problems rather than hiding them. That is the signal that builds deep investor trust.

**Hard conversations:** At some point in the two years after a Series A closes, you will have a conversation with your board member where you disagree fundamentally — about strategy, about a hire, about a product decision. How you handle that conversation determines the long-term relationship. The rule: state your view, hear theirs fully, decide, and then execute with full commitment regardless of which view prevailed. The investor sitting on your board has seen more companies fail and succeed than you have. They are worth hearing. You are the CEO. The decision is yours.

**When investors lose confidence:** The signal is subtle at first. Meeting attendance gets less engaged. Follow-up emails slow down. The investor stops making introductions. If you notice this, name it directly: "I get the sense our last few months haven't matched your expectations. I want to hear what's driving that." Leaving it unspoken lets it compound. Most investor-founder relationship problems are recoverable if addressed early and honestly.

## Investment Implications

**For Prospectra:**  
The governance decisions made in the Series A term sheet — board composition, protective provisions, founder vesting — will determine the degree of operational freedom Prospectra retains through its growth phase. A 3-person board with narrow protective provisions gives the management team the agility to execute without board approval on operational decisions. A 5-person board with broad protective provisions at a company Prospectra's stage creates governance overhead that slows execution.

**Recommendation for Prospectra's Series A:** Target a 3-person board (Bolo + 1 investor + 1 independent added only when a specific high-value candidate is identified). Narrow protective provisions to capital structure changes, M&A, and large debt. No budget approval clause. Double-trigger acceleration for founders.

**For the data intelligence sector broadly:**  
Governance structure affects acquisition optionality. Companies with clean, standard governance (no unusual protective provisions, no investor consent requirements for ordinary operational decisions) are easier to acquire — fewer parties to negotiate with, fewer consents to collect. If Prospectra is building toward an exit, governance cleanliness is a strategic asset that gets priced at close.

## CEO Note

Board governance is the least romantic and most consequential work in early-stage company building. Founders who get it right — clean structure, honest cadence, genuine board utility — build companies that run faster, raise better, and exit cleaner. Founders who get it wrong spend the years after the raise fighting governance overhead instead of building the product.

Prospectra's governance design principle: build the lightest structure that provides genuine accountability. Three board members who challenge each other honestly are more valuable than five who ratify without friction.

The investor update email is not an obligation. It is the best recurring investment in the company's most important relationships that a CEO can make. Thirty minutes, once a month. Every CEO who does it consistently says the same thing in retrospect: it was the most leveraged hour of the month.
