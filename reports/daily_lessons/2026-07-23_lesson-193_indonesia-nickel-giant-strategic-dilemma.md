## Opening Framing Question

*In 2020, Indonesia made one of the boldest resource nationalism moves of the decade: it banned all exports of unprocessed nickel ore, forcing the world to process nickel on Indonesian soil. The bet seemed to work — within four years, Indonesia controlled 60% of global nickel supply and attracted hundreds of billions in investment. But here is the problem: nearly all of those new factories are owned by Chinese companies, the trade deal Jakarta signed with Washington in February 2026 can't qualify Indonesian nickel for US EV subsidies because Chinese entities control the processing, and BYD just took 150,000 pre-orders for an SUV that doesn't use a single gram of nickel. Did Indonesia play the resource nationalism game brilliantly — or did it build the world's largest Chinese industrial park on its own territory while betting the house on a chemistry that is quietly becoming obsolete?*

This is the Indonesia question in 2026. The answer has profound implications for commodity markets, EM equity positioning, and the entire US-China critical minerals competition.

---

## I. THE STRUCTURAL FOUNDATION: WHY INDONESIA MATTERS

Indonesia is the world's fourth largest country by population (280 million), the largest Muslim-majority nation, and the anchor economy of Southeast Asia. It is also the single most important node in the global nickel supply chain.

**The numbers:**
- Indonesia holds approximately **21% of global nickel reserves** but now produces **60% of global nickel output** (up from 31.5% in 2020)
- Nickel is the essential ingredient in **NMC (Nickel Manganese Cobalt) batteries** — the premium chemistry used in high-range EVs
- Indonesia sits astride the **Malacca Strait** — the world's second busiest shipping chokepoint, through which 40% of global trade passes
- GDP of $1.4 trillion (13th globally), but 60% of export value still derives from raw commodities
- President Prabowo Subianto, inaugurated October 2024, commands the military establishment's loyalty and is reorienting Indonesian foreign policy more assertively

Indonesia is not a peripheral market. It is a pivotal state in the US-China competition for materials, trade routes, and regional influence simultaneously.

---

## II. THE RESOURCE NATIONALISM BET: THE 2020 ORE BAN

### The Play

In January 2020, Indonesia enacted a ban on the export of unprocessed nickel ore. This was not a modest policy — it was a declaration that Indonesia would no longer sell its birthright for extraction fees.

The logic was compelling: why export raw ore at $30-40/tonne when you can force the world to build smelters and HPAL (High Pressure Acid Leach) plants in Indonesia, capture the value-added processing, build domestic employment, and eventually anchor a full EV battery supply chain — from mine to cell?

The policy worked, in the narrow sense. Capital poured in. Chinese companies — Tsingshan Holding, Zhejiang Huayou Cobalt, Ningbo Lygend, Zhejiang Brunp — built HPAL plants at a pace that stunned the industry. Indonesian nickel processing capacity surged from near zero in 2018 to commanding 60%+ of global supply by 2024.

### The Hidden Cost: Chinese Industrial Capture

Here is what the headline numbers obscured: **over 90% of Indonesia's new nickel processing capacity is owned by Chinese entities.** Indonesia got the factories. China got the factories.

The investment model was straightforward: Chinese companies needed to secure nickel supply for their EV battery giants (CATL, BYD, SVOLT). Indonesian law required processing to happen domestically. The solution was to build processing in Indonesia — wholly or majority-owned by Chinese firms — and then export nickel intermediate products (MHP: Mixed Hydroxide Precipitate) to China for final battery manufacturing.

The result: Indonesia has *employment* and *tax revenue* from nickel processing. China has *control* over the supply chain. The strategic dependency runs in both directions, but it runs more decisively toward Beijing — especially when you need to sell your nickel intermediate product to exactly the buyers who own your factory.

**The Lowy Institute's verdict (2026):** Indonesia's "free and active" foreign policy is constrained, not liberated, by nickel dependence — because the sector's ownership structure means Indonesian resource nationalism is, functionally, Chinese supply chain nationalism operating on Indonesian soil.

## III. THE US-INDONESIA TRADE DEAL: WASHINGTON ARRIVES LATE

In February 2026, the Trump administration signed a bilateral trade agreement with Indonesia. The terms: US tariffs on Indonesian goods reduced from 32% to 19%, in exchange for preferential access to critical minerals (nickel, cobalt, copper) and fossil fuel investment rights.

**What this deal is not:**
- It is **not** an FTA that qualifies Indonesian nickel for the Inflation Reduction Act's EV tax credits. The IRA requires that critical minerals used in qualifying EVs be sourced from countries with a free trade agreement with the United States — a narrow definition that Indonesia does not meet with a tariff-reduction deal alone.
- The deal has also been criticized domestically as "extractive colonialism" (Mongabay's framing), with Indonesian civil society arguing Washington extracted resource access without offering reciprocal manufacturing or technology transfer.

**The deeper problem:** Even if the IRA qualification issue were resolved, US battery makers would be importing nickel intermediate products (MHP) from plants 90% owned by Chinese companies. Congress would classify that as qualifying the Chinese supply chain for US subsidies through an Indonesian loophole. Bipartisan resistance on Capitol Hill killed the critical minerals agreement discussion before it reached legislative stage.

**Bottom line:** The US arrived late to the Indonesian nickel story, found Chinese ownership deeply embedded, and signed a deal that looks meaningful on paper but solves little structurally. Indonesia extracted a tariff reduction without giving the US the supply chain security Washington actually needs.

## IV. THE EXISTENTIAL DISRUPTION: LFP AND THE BYD DATANG PROBLEM

This is where the lesson gets uncomfortable.

Indonesia's entire resource nationalism bet — the export ban, the HPAL buildout, the IRA negotiation, the trade deal — was predicated on a single assumption: **NMC batteries (which need nickel) would remain the dominant EV chemistry.**

That assumption is cracking.

**What happened:**
- Lithium Iron Phosphate (LFP) batteries — which use **zero nickel** — held less than 10% of the global EV battery market in 2020.
- By 2025, LFP accounted for **50% of the global EV battery market.**
- In July 2026, BYD's Datang SUV — running on second-generation Blade LMFP (Lithium Manganese Iron Phosphate) chemistry — recorded **150,000 pre-orders in 53 days** with zero nickel in the battery.
- Chinese automakers are structurally favoring LFP/LMFP for mass-market EVs because: (1) lower cost, (2) better safety profile (no thermal runaway), (3) longer cycle life, (4) domestic supply chain advantage (China controls lithium, iron, phosphate supply chains far more than nickel).

**The implication for Indonesia:**
- If LFP becomes the dominant EV chemistry, Indonesian nickel loses its primary growth driver.
- The EV battery demand narrative that justified $40B+ in HPAL investment becomes impaired.
- Nickel's remaining demand base shifts back toward stainless steel — a lower-growth, more cyclical market.
- LG Energy Solution's April 2025 withdrawal from its $8.4B Indonesian battery investment was an early signal: the economics of nickel-based battery manufacturing in Indonesia are deteriorating.

**Nickel price implications:** The combined effect of Indonesian supply growth (from the HPAL buildout) plus the demand-side LFP substitution pushes nickel prices toward a structural range of $14,000–$15,500/tonne — below the cost curve for many HPAL operators. This is a classic commodity trap: the supply buildout that caused the price collapse was itself enabled by the high prices that no longer exist.

Indonesia built a nickel empire at the moment nickel's EV narrative began to structurally weaken.

## V. PRABOWO'S FOREIGN POLICY: MULTI-VECTOR OR MULTI-EXPOSED?

Indonesia's foreign policy tradition is "bebas aktif" — free and active. Non-aligned, but not passive. Indonesia plays every major power simultaneously, extracting maximum value from strategic ambiguity.

Prabowo has continued this tradition with a more assertive, military-influenced edge. Key features:

1. **Maintaining China ties**: Chinese investment is too deep in Indonesia's economy to jeopardize. The nickel sector alone generates hundreds of thousands of Indonesian jobs in Chinese-operated facilities. Prabowo cannot afford to alienate Beijing.

2. **Engaging Washington**: The February 2026 trade deal signals Indonesia's willingness to be part of the US-led supply chain restructuring — but on Indonesia's terms, not as a US client.

3. **ASEAN leadership**: Indonesia sees itself as ASEAN's natural anchor. Prabowo is using Indonesia's ASEAN chairmanship cycle to project diplomatic relevance, even as ASEAN itself struggles with internal divisions over China policy.

4. **"Strategic incorporation" debate**: A July 3, 2026 Jakarta Post op-ed framed Prabowo's approach not as strategic autonomy but "strategic incorporation" — suggesting Indonesia is being absorbed into great power frameworks rather than remaining above them. The Chinese HPAL ownership structure lends this critique empirical weight.

**The multi-alignment dilemma (Lowy Institute):** Indonesia's structural exposure to China (via manufacturing ownership, export dependency, and debt) and its desire for US security guarantees creates a position where Jakarta must simultaneously reassure both powers — and neither one fully trusts Indonesia as a strategic partner. That is the price of multi-alignment in a sharpening bipolar competition.

## VI. INVESTMENT IMPLICATIONS

### Nickel: Structurally Bearish with Tactical Volatility

- **Long-run price target:** $14,000–$15,500/tonne. LFP substitution + Indonesian supply overhang + rising HPAL production costs form a structural bear case.
- **Risk to the bearish view:** Any significant NMC market share recovery (if solid-state batteries ultimately use nickel-rich cathodes at scale — a credible 2028+ scenario), or major Indonesian supply disruption (policy reversal, Chinese investment withdrawal, infrastructure failure).
- **Conviction level:** Moderate-high on structure; low on timing. Nickel can spike 20% on single supply disruption events before resuming the structural downtrend.

### Indonesian Equities (EIDO / JCI): Discounted but Structurally Complex

- Indonesian equity valuations are cheap relative to regional peers, partly reflecting the China ownership overhang and the nickel narrative deterioration.
- Genuine structural positive: Indonesia's domestic consumption story (280M people, median age 29, rising middle class) is not commodity-dependent.
- **Sector tilt:** Favor Indonesian banks (demographic tailwind), telcos, and consumer staples over resource extractors. Avoid pure-play Indonesian nickel equities.
- **Political risk:** Prabowo's military background raises long-run institutional quality concerns, but his first two years have been market-neutral.

### Indonesian Government Bonds (OBL): Carry With EM Risk Overlay

- Indonesian 10-year OBL yields ~6.8–7.2% (USD-hedged equivalent roughly 4.5-5%). Attractive carry for EM bond allocators.
- Risk: Rupiah weakness if Chinese investment sentiment deteriorates; current account sensitivity to commodity price swings.
- **Timeframe:** 12–18 month hold. Treat as tactical carry, not structural allocation.

### US Defense / Strategic Materials Plays: Indirect Exposure

- US failure to qualify Indonesian nickel accelerates incentives for domestic US nickel production (Talon Metals, Giga Metals) and FTA-compliant alternatives (Canadian, Australian producers).
- Australian ASX-listed nickel producers may recover if the LFP overhang thesis proves less severe than feared.

### CEO Portfolio Note

*The Indonesia thesis is a cautionary tale about the gap between resource endowment and value capture. Jakarta won the resource nationalism argument — it did force processing onshore. But it lost the ownership argument, the chemistry argument, and the supply chain argument. The net result is a country that is more strategically exposed to a single great power (China) than before its "liberation" from raw ore export dependency. This is not unique to Indonesia — it is the central paradox of EM resource nationalism in the Chinese-led investment era. For portfolio construction: be long the supply chain resilience trade (Western hemisphere nickel, Australian lithium, US rare earth processing) and tactically long Indonesian domestic consumption; be short or neutral Indonesian nickel equities and pure nickel commodity exposure.*
