## 1. The Core Concept: Geopolitics Forces Fiscal Expansion

The fiscal dominance series (Lessons 426–439) treated deficits and debt accumulation largely as financial/monetary phenomena. But zoom out and a different picture emerges: *the primary driver of today's synchronised fiscal expansion is great power competition*.

Great power competition creates inescapable fiscal pressure through five channels:

### Channel 1 — Defense Spending
Security competition demands military investment that compounds on itself. Each side's buildup justifies the other's. NATO's 2% GDP target is now a floor, not a ceiling — Germany, Poland, and the Baltic states are at 3–4%. The US FY2026 defense budget rose $48 billion year-on-year as the Pentagon retools for great power warfare against China and manages ongoing Ukraine/Middle East commitments. Russia's defense burden is now 8–9% of GDP — Soviet-era levels — and its Finance Ministry and central bank are openly warning that the Ukraine campaign is breaking the fiscal framework.

**Key insight:** Defense spending is the most politically durable form of government expenditure. It cannot be cut during a security competition without signaling weakness to adversaries. This makes it the stickiest driver of fiscal expansion.

### Channel 2 — Industrial Policy and Friendshoring
We covered the Return of Industrial Policy (Lesson 23) and Friendshoring (Lesson 59). Both are fiscally expensive. The US CHIPS Act, IRA, and associated programs commit hundreds of billions in subsidies and tax credits. Europe's Green Deal Industrial Plan, its chips sovereignty fund, and its defense industrial base programs replicate the pattern. China's Made in China 2025 successors and its semiconductor ecosystem investment are in the same category.

**Key insight:** Industrial policy is the domestic fiscal expression of the same competition that drives defense spending. The security motive (critical semiconductor supply, EV battery independence, weapons systems manufacturing) provides political cover for spending that would otherwise face budget constraints.

### Channel 3 — Debt-Service on the Previous Round
Security competition is not new. The Cold War (1947–1991) and its immediate aftermath generated large debt stocks. Post-2001 wars added more. The 2008–2009 crisis rescue added more. COVID added more. Each round of geopolitically-driven spending leaves a debt legacy whose interest consumes an ever-larger share of the budget — which itself drives the deficit even without new spending.

**US example:** US net interest payments exceeded $900 billion in FY2026, making it the third-largest line item in the federal budget, behind Social Security and defense. This is debt from previous strategic competitions compounding at current interest rates.

### Channel 4 — Climate Security (New Category)
Climate change is now framed as a security issue in every major government's strategic documents. This framing matters fiscally: it converts climate spending from discretionary (cuttable) to mandatory (security-critical). The EU's fit-for-55 package, US IRA green energy credits, and China's renewable buildout are all partially underwritten by security logic. Framed as "energy independence," climate investment becomes geopolitically non-negotiable.

### Channel 5 — Demographic Costs That Security Competition Makes Impossible to Reform
The actuarial crisis in Social Security, Medicare, and European pension systems is well-known. Normally, fiscal reformers would argue for benefit cuts. But security competition makes this politically suicidal: you cannot ask a population to accept benefit cuts *and* send their children to serve in an expanded military. The political coalition that supports security spending and the coalition that resists social spending reform are the same people. Governments choose the path of least resistance: spend on both.

## 2. Historical Grounding: The Cold War Fiscal Precedent

This is not novel. The US ran the largest peacetime deficits of the 20th century during the Reagan defense buildup of 1981–1989, explicitly as a strategy to outspend the Soviet Union. The theory: the Soviets could not afford the arms race. The US could. Eventually the USSR collapsed, partly under fiscal pressure from military competition with a vastly larger economy.

**The difference in 2026:** The US is no longer in a position of structural fiscal superiority relative to its adversary. China's economy has closed the gap significantly, and it retains command-economy capacity to direct resources toward strategic priorities without democratic accountability. The US must spend through a political system that also demands social insurance, healthcare, and interest payments. The fiscal arithmetic is harder.

**Russia's trajectory** shows what happens when a smaller economy tries to compete militarily with powers multiple times its size: the Finance Ministry and central bank begin warning about structural unsustainability. Russia's defense burden at 8–9% of GDP is the highest since Soviet collapse. This is the fiscal end game of punching above your weight in a security competition.

## 3. Concrete Example: FY2026 US Deficit

The US posted a $1.99 trillion deficit for FY2026 (year ending September 30, 2026) — the largest since FY2021 and driven by:

- Defense spending up $48 billion
- Net interest exceeding $900 billion (debt-service on previous spending)
- Tariff refund flows following a Supreme Court ruling (reversal of tariff revenues assumed in budget projections)

This is the fingerprint of the geopolitical-fiscal complex in real-time. Defense spending is geopolitical competition. Interest payments are the accumulated cost of previous geopolitical rounds. Tariff dynamics reflect the trade war architecture built over the last decade.

**The projection:** The CBO and independent forecasters project US deficits averaging $2–2.5 trillion annually through 2030, barring a major policy reversal. No such reversal is politically available during a great power security competition. The fiscal dominance regime is structurally locked in.

## 4. Investment Implications

Understanding *why* fiscal dominance exists changes the investment thesis in several important ways:

### 4a. Duration of the Regime
If fiscal dominance were the result of a temporary crisis (COVID, financial crisis), you would expect a reversion. If it is the result of great power competition, it persists as long as competition does — which is measured in decades, not years. **The fiscal dominance regime does not end when rates fall. It ends when security competition ends, which is not the base case.**

**Portfolio implication:** The hedges and positioning we built in Lessons 426–439 are not short-term trades. They are structural allocations for a multi-year, possibly multi-decade regime.

### 4b. Real Assets as Strategic Hedge
Governments running fiscal dominance need to finance themselves. The path of least resistance is inflationary: financial repression, yield curve control, eventually monetization. Hard assets — commodities, real estate, gold, energy infrastructure — preserve value against currency debasement better than nominal bonds.

**New angle:** Great power competition *directly* strengthens the commodity case. Defense spending is commodity-intensive (steel, aluminium, rare earths, copper for electronics). Friendshoring rebuilds domestic industrial capacity, which requires capital goods and raw materials. Energy independence drives LNG, nuclear, and critical mineral demand.

### 4c. Defense Sector as Secular Growth
This was covered in Lesson 414 and elsewhere, but the fiscal dominance lens adds precision. Defense spending is the *cause* of fiscal dominance, which means it is the most politically protected spending. Defense contractors are not cyclical — their revenues are supported by the same structural competition that makes fiscal cutting politically impossible.

**Directional view (6–18 months):** Long defense industrials (US, European — especially Polish and German defense build), long critical minerals tied to defense supply chains, long nuclear (dual-use: energy security AND submarine/weapons programs drive demand).

### 4d. Sovereign Credit Differentiation
Not all nations are equal competitors. Some face fiscal dominance from the outside (smaller NATO members who must spend on defense despite structural fiscal weakness). These are the most vulnerable sovereigns — their deficits are not optional but their borrowing costs reflect credit deterioration. **Short peripheral European sovereign spreads (Italy, Greece) vs. core Germany if defense spending mandates deepen.**

### 4e. The Adversary Asymmetry
China runs fiscal dominance differently — less through bond markets (which it largely controls) and more through directed credit, state bank balance sheet expansion, and local government financing vehicles. This makes China's fiscal dominance less visible in market prices but no less real. Watch for stress in China's LGFVs and property-sector recapitalization as proxy signals.

Build a country-level composite that measures the *geopolitical drivers* of fiscal pressure separately from standard fiscal metrics. Inputs:

| Feature | Source |

| Alliance spending obligations | NATO, AUKUS, QUAD burden-sharing commitments |

**Output:** A GFPI score per country that predicts the structural floor for fiscal deficits, not just the current level. Countries with high GFPI and rising GFPI scores are likely to see persistent fiscal dominance regardless of central bank intentions.

**Investment signal use:** Cross this with current sovereign spread levels. Countries where GFPI predicts high fiscal pressure but markets have not yet priced deteriorating credibility are the long-term short in sovereign credit.
