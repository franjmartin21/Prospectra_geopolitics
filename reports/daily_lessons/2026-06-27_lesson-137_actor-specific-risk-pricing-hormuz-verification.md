## Opening Question

On June 24–25, Strait of Hormuz traffic rose to roughly its highest level since the war began in late February — record numbers of commercial vessels, including large crude carriers, routing through a new IMO/Oman-coordinated corridor Iran had explicitly called "unacceptable" days earlier. Then, hours after a vessel was struck off the Omani coast in an attack the US attributes to Iran, the IMO didn't slow commercial traffic at all — it paused a *different*, narrower program: its own effort to evacuate stranded ships from the Gulf. Same attack, same day, two completely opposite reactions from two different actors. If you only tracked "is shipping getting safer or more dangerous," you'd get contradictory answers depending on which actor you watched. The job today is to figure out why that's not a contradiction — and what it tells an investor about which signals to trust.

## Section 2: The Concept — Risk Tolerance Is Actor-Specific, Not Threat-Specific

L136 introduced cry-wolf fatigue: repeated threats that don't produce sustained disruption progressively lose price-moving power, because market participants update toward "this actor's threats don't materialize." Today's data is a clean illustration of that mechanism *and* a necessary correction to it. The correction: fatigue is a property of return-seeking actors who can price risk into a number (freight rate, insurance premium, equity multiple) and keep operating once that number clears their hurdle. It does not transfer automatically to actors whose objective function has no such hurdle.

Look at what actually happened around the June 25 attack. **Commercial shipowners** — whose entire business is moving cargo at a profit — read the same threat environment, priced it (war-risk insurance is running near 4% of a vessel's value for a seven-day Gulf transit, against a pre-crisis baseline near 0.001%), and concluded the trade was still worth making at record volume. They are pricing risk, not ignoring it — the 4,000x insurance markup *is* the price of the cry-wolf-discounted-but-not-zero risk. **The IMO**, by contrast, runs an evacuation program whose entire purpose is protecting stranded crews who have no insurance hedge against being killed. Its objective function has no "acceptable casualty rate that clears a hurdle" — a single attack against a vessel near its corridor was sufficient to pause the program outright, regardless of what the same week's traffic data said about aggregate risk.

**The 20-year precedent: Somali piracy, 2008–2012.** The split is almost exactly reproduced. International naval task forces (EU NAVFOR, Combined Task Force 151, NATO's Ocean Shield) treated piracy as a zero-tolerance problem and intervened with force whenever they had the opportunity, regardless of cost per intervention — a state-safety objective function with no profit hurdle to clear. Commercial shipping, facing the same pirates, initially just paid: ransoms averaged $2–3 million per ship by 2010-2011, insurance premiums for the High Risk Area rose sharply, and traffic continued because the expected cost still cleared the profitability hurdle for most routes. Only when the *cost calculus itself* shifted — ransom amounts climbing, insurers tightening capacity, the Best Management Practices armed-guard model proving cheap and effective by comparison — did commercial behavior change, and it changed through a pricing adjustment (hiring private armed guards became standard by 2012), not through the zero-tolerance logic the navies were already running on day one.

**Why this matters for reading any future headline in this conflict:** a UN agency pausing a program is genuine, real news — but it is news about an actor with a near-zero risk tolerance reacting to a single incident, not a referendum on the aggregate risk level commercial markets are actually pricing. The investor mistake is treating "the UN paused something" and "Brent should reprice" as the same signal. This week, they weren't: the IMO paused; Brent kept falling; commercial traffic hit a wartime high. Reading institutional caution as a market signal — or market calm as proof an institution is overreacting — both require collapsing two different objective functions into one, and that collapse is exactly what generates the over- and under-reactions retail narratives are full of.

## Key Concepts Covered

- Actor-specific risk tolerance: why the same threat (an attack on Gulf shipping) produced opposite reactions from commercial shippers (record traffic, continued use of a contested route) and the IMO (an immediate program pause) — because each actor's objective function prices risk differently, not because either reaction is irrational
- Somali piracy (2008–2012) as the 20-year precedent: zero-tolerance naval task forces vs. cost-pricing commercial shippers, and how commercial behavior eventually changed through a pricing shift (ransom and insurance costs rising, then BMP/armed guards), not through adopting the navies' zero-tolerance logic
- TRACE-V evidentiary tiers applied to a live data problem: a recurring, multi-source figure ($74.43) that is highly likely correct but not independently re-confirmed against a primary settlement print is Tier 2, not Tier 1 — and grading proceeds on that basis explicitly, not silently
- The investor's collapse-of-objective-functions error: mistaking an institution's near-zero-risk-tolerance reaction (the IMO's pause) for a market-wide repricing signal, when the actual market-pricing actor (commercial shipping, insurers) moved the opposite direction the same day
