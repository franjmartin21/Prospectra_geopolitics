## 1. The Framework: Three Types of Reallocation

Before naming winners, clarify what is actually being reallocated. There are three distinct categories of manufacturing migration, and they have very different investment profiles:

**Type 1 — Tariff Arbitrage (Transshipment)**
Assembly or minor processing in a third country to obscure Chinese origin. Goods are "made in Vietnam" but contain 80–90% Chinese content. This generates nominal FDI flows and some employment, but creates almost no deep industrial capability. It is also explicitly targeted by US Customs and Border Protection with country-of-origin audits. Type 1 creates short-term statistical noise, not investable structural change.

**Type 2 — Capacity Duplication**
A company builds a genuine second factory in Vietnam or Mexico, but keeps the China factory running. This is "insurance manufacturing." The second factory runs at lower utilization and higher per-unit cost; margins compress. The company has paid a geopolitical insurance premium. The investment signal here is mixed: it is a real commitment to diversification but a drag on near-term returns.

**Type 3 — Structural Migration**
Manufacturing ecosystems — including suppliers, tooling, logistics, and skills — actually transplant to a new geography. This takes 7–12 years to complete and requires host-country policy support (land, power, customs infrastructure, worker training). Type 3 is rare, transformative, and creates compounding advantage for the host nation.

**The investor error:** Markets price Types 1, 2, and 3 identically in the short run. The discipline is identifying which type is actually happening.

## 2. The Three Major Destinations — A Comparative Analysis

### Mexico: The USMCA Advantage

**What Mexico offers:** Tariff-free access to the US market under USMCA, geographic proximity (1–3 day truck shipment vs. 30-day ocean freight from Asia), time-zone alignment, and a mature automotive manufacturing base. The "nearshoring" thesis is most compelling for sectors where proximity matters: automotive, medical devices, aerospace, and consumer goods requiring rapid replenishment.

**The numbers:** Mexico attracted $40.8B in FDI in 2025. New manufacturing investment was up nearly 200% year-over-year in the first nine months of 2025. Monterrey, Saltillo, and Ciudad Juárez are the primary industrial corridors.

**The constraint:** Mexico's infrastructure is the binding constraint. Industrial parks are at near-capacity in key corridors. Power grid reliability is poor (government-owned CFE cannot keep pace with industrial demand). Water scarcity in northern Mexico is structurally underpriced as a risk. And the 2026 USMCA review — which is ongoing — creates policy uncertainty about rules of origin requirements, particularly for Chinese-owned factories operating in Mexico.

**The investment angle:**
- Mexican industrial REITs (FIBRAs) — particularly FIBRA Prologis and VKNG (Vesta) — benefit directly from industrial real estate demand
- Mexican construction and infrastructure companies
- Long MXN on a 12–24 month horizon IF infrastructure investment materializes (caveat: this thesis requires Pemex and CFE reform progress)
- Watch: US-Mexico tension over Chinese-owned manufacturing operations — this is the USMCA review's core political fault line

### Vietnam: The Electronics Assembly Powerhouse

**What Vietnam offers:** Low labor costs (though rising), deep integration into Asian electronics supply chains, government aggressiveness in attracting FDI, and geographic positioning within existing Asian logistics networks.

**The numbers:** $15.2B in FDI in Q1 2026 (+42.9% YoY). Vietnam exports roughly $350B in goods annually, with electronics (Samsung, Intel, LG) representing over 30% of that. Samsung alone makes ~60% of its global smartphones in Vietnam.

**The hidden risk:** Vietnam's manufacturing miracle is more concentrated than it appears. Five multinational anchors (Samsung, Intel, LG, Hon Hai/Foxconn, and Luxshare) account for a disproportionate share of export value. This creates single-point-of-failure exposure. If Samsung were to relocate a major facility — due to input cost increases, labor issues, or a new geopolitical shock — Vietnam's export profile would contract sharply. Additionally, Vietnam's component supply chain is still largely Chinese: domestic value-add on many "Made in Vietnam" electronics products is 20–35%.

**The real risk in 2026:** Vietnam faces rising input costs, power grid stress (electricity shortage was a drag on manufacturing in 2023–2024, partially addressed with new capacity), and the political risk of being caught between US pressure to decouple from China and economic dependence on Chinese intermediate goods.

**The investment angle:**
- Vietnamese equity market (VN-Index) is a direct play — dominated by real estate and banking, which benefit from FDI-driven urbanization
- Vietnam infrastructure ETFs (power, ports, industrial parks) — where to look if direct market access is available
- Watch: Global logistics companies expanding Vietnam capacity (DHL, Maersk, Kerry Logistics)

### India: The Long-Duration Thesis

**What India offers:** Scale (1.4B population, enormous domestic market), government ambition (PLI — Production-Linked Incentives — schemes covering 14 sectors), English-language business environment, and a growing engineering talent base. India is the only country that can match China's scale over a 10–20 year horizon.

**The honest assessment:** India's supply chain integration is early-stage, and the gap between India's aspiration and reality is significant.

A useful data point: 71% of the components in India-assembled iPhones (assembled by Tata Electronics and Foxconn's India operations) still come from China. Local value addition is 18–20% against a government target of 35–40%. Apple has been assembling in India since 2017. Seven years of effort produced 20% local content. This is not a criticism — it reflects the genuine difficulty of building a component ecosystem — but it is a reality investors must price.

**The sectors where India IS competitive today:**
1. Pharmaceuticals — India supplies ~40% of US generic drug consumption. The geopolitical case for onshoring critical drug supply chains benefits Indian API (Active Pharmaceutical Ingredient) manufacturers
2. Defense electronics and aerospace components — government procurement bias toward domestic industry
3. Textiles and apparel — lower labor costs than Vietnam or China
4. Specialty chemicals — growing sector with PLI support

**The investment angle:**
- Indian equities have priced in significant optimism (Nifty 50 trades at a premium to most EM peers)
- More surgical: Indian capital goods companies, infrastructure plays, and select industrial conglomerates
- 10-year thesis: India is the structural beneficiary of supply chain diversification at scale — but requires patience and tolerance for policy volatility

## 3. The Meta-Framework: What to Actually Invest In

After the country-by-country analysis, a more powerful framework emerges: **invest in the infrastructure of reallocation, not just the production capacity.**

When a major manufacturing ecosystem migrates, the bottlenecks are:
1. Industrial real estate and power (Mexico FIBRAs, Vietnam industrial park developers)
2. Logistics infrastructure (port expansion, inland freight, cold chain)
3. Capital equipment suppliers to the host-country manufacturers
4. Financial services in the host country (local banks, trade finance)

This is the "picks and shovels" approach to supply chain reallocation — less exposed to which country wins the most investment and more exposed to the structural reality that *all* of them need more infrastructure.

**The portfolio signal:**
- Long: Industrial REIT exposure in nearshoring corridors; logistics infrastructure operators in Southeast Asia
- Long: Capital equipment exporters to Asia (Japanese and German industrial machinery companies are underowned proxies)
- Cautious on: Pure-play country equity bets until Type 3 structural migration (see Section 1) is verified
- Short (or underweight): Companies that announced supply chain diversification in 2021–2023 based on Type 1 arbitrage — they face margin compression and scrutiny as rules-of-origin enforcement tightens

## 4. The Closing Question Answered

*A company moves 20% of production to Vietnam. Stock rises 4%. Three years later, the Vietnam factory is half-utilized and margins are worse.*

What went wrong:
1. They built Type 2 capacity (insurance) without reducing the China factory — they now run two factories at higher combined cost
2. The Vietnam factory was dependent on Chinese components (Type 1 underneath the surface)
3. They didn't build the full supplier ecosystem — so the Vietnam plant can't operate independently
4. The market priced the *announcement* of diversification, not the outcome

What the investor should have asked before buying the 4% move: "What percentage of their BOM (Bill of Materials) components are now sourced outside China? What is the utilization plan for both factories?" Without those answers, the announcement is sentiment, not signal.

## Databricks Angle

**Dataset:** UN Comtrade bilateral trade data — track the China share of intermediate inputs in "Made in Vietnam" or "Made in Mexico" exports to the US. A rising share of Chinese components in third-country exports is the signature of Type 1 transshipment; a falling share indicates genuine ecosystem migration.

**Pipeline idea:** Build a "Supply Chain Authenticity Index" — for a given product category, measure the ratio of country-of-origin intermediate goods to total export value. Join to FDI flows, equity performance of listed industrial companies in the destination country, and tariff enforcement actions by CBP. Flag companies with high China-origin BOM that are marketing themselves as "diversified supply chains" — these are potential underweights.

**Feature engineering:** Month-over-month change in CBP anti-dumping enforcement actions by country, as a signal of Type 1 scheme tightening. Historically, this precedes margin compression for companies relying on tariff arbitrage.
