## Opening Framing Question

Here is a simple arithmetic problem with enormous consequences:

> The United States government pays **~5% interest** on its debt. Its nominal GDP grows at **~4% per year**.
> Interest rates are higher than growth. The debt-to-GDP ratio therefore rises automatically — even if the government runs a balanced primary budget.

This is not a crisis scenario. **This is the current baseline.**

The question is not whether this is sustainable indefinitely — it isn't. The question is: **how does it end, how fast, and what does that mean for every asset class on earth?**

---

## 1. The Core Concept: r > g

In the canonical debt dynamics framework:

**Debt/GDP changes = Primary Deficit + (r − g) × Debt/GDP**

Where:
- `r` = real interest rate on government debt
- `g` = real GDP growth rate
- Primary deficit = spending minus revenues (excluding interest)

When **r > g** (interest rates exceed growth), debt grows faster than the economy even with a balanced budget. The only way to stabilize debt is to run a **primary surplus** — i.e., revenues must exceed non-interest spending. The surplus required is proportional to how far above growth the interest rate sits.

**Historical context:**
- 1950s–1970s: r < g was common (financial repression, capped interest rates, high postwar growth). Debt ratios fell effortlessly.
- 1980s–2010s: Mixed. OECD economies ran r ≈ g roughly, with periodic crises.
- 2020s: Zero rates collapsed the r − g gap to negative briefly, making debt "free." Then the inflation shock of 2022 inverted it hard. Now: r > g across most major sovereigns.

The era of "debt was free" is over. We are now in the **r > g regime** for the first time since the early 1980s — but with debt levels 2–3x higher than then.

## 3. The Concrete Example: The US Debt Trap in Real Time (2023–2026)

As recently as 2021, the US paid about 1.5% on its debt on average — its existing stock was financed at near-zero rates. As the Fed hiked to 5.25–5.5% and held there, something mechanical happened:

**Debt rollover.** The US Treasury must refinance ~$7–8 trillion of existing debt each year as short-term bills and notes mature. Each refinancing replaces 2021 vintage debt (1–2% coupon) with 2024–2026 vintage debt (4.5–5%). The average cost of the entire debt stock *ratchets upward*, even without issuing a dollar of new debt.

The result: US net interest payments have gone from ~$300bn/yr in 2020 to over **$900bn/yr in 2026** — now exceeding the entire defense budget. This is not a projection. It happened.

The trajectory:
- 2025: ~$950bn interest cost
- 2030 (CBO baseline): ~$1.3 trillion/yr
- 2035 (CBO baseline): ~$1.7 trillion/yr (larger than Social Security)

At some point, interest becomes the budget. New spending crowds out defense, entitlements, and investment — or requires more borrowing, which increases interest further. **This is the debt trap spiral.**

## 4. The Three Exits (and Their Investment Implications)

Every sovereign debt supercycle must eventually resolve. History gives us three mechanisms:

### Exit 1 — Fiscal Consolidation (Austerity + Growth)
**What it requires:** Primary surpluses large enough to bring r−g under control, plus structural reforms that lift the growth rate.

**Historical precedent:** Canada in the 1990s; several Eurozone periphery countries after 2012 under IMF programs.

**Probability in the US:** Low. No political coalition currently supports large enough primary surpluses. Defense, entitlements, and interest are all growing. Discretionary spending is already compressed.

**Investment signal if this happens:**
- Bullish for long-duration Treasuries (r falls as deficit shrinks)
- Bullish for USD
- Bearish for gold

### Exit 2 — Financial Repression
**What it requires:** Keep nominal interest rates below nominal growth through regulation, capital controls, or central bank suppression of yields. Savers bear the cost.

**Historical precedent:** US and UK after WWII. Debt/GDP fell 80+ points over two decades without a single default — through sustained negative real returns on government bonds.

**The mechanism:** If r can be held below g, the debt ratio falls automatically. Inflation helps too — by inflating away the real value of fixed-rate debt.

**Probability going forward:** Moderate. Not in a politically explicit form, but through yield curve control (Japan's model), regulatory requirements for financial institutions to hold sovereigns (Basel III), and tolerance for above-target inflation.

**Investment signal if financial repression returns:**
- Bearish for long-duration bonds (real returns suppressed)
- Bullish for real assets: gold, commodities, real estate, TIPS
- Bullish for equities with pricing power
- Negative for retirees and insurance companies holding fixed income

### Exit 3 — Inflation Monetization (Default by Stealth)
**What it requires:** The central bank prints money to buy debt the market won't buy at acceptable yields. Inflation surges. The real value of debt is inflated away.

**Historical precedent:** Weimar Germany (extreme), post-WWI France (moderate), Turkey (ongoing), Argentina (chronic), some EM examples.

**Is this possible in a reserve currency country?** The honest answer is: we don't know. The US has never been in a fiscal position this stressed with interest rates this high. The reserve currency status gives more room than any other country. But it is not infinite room.

**Investment signal if monetization begins:**
- Severe bear market for Treasuries (yields spike as market loses confidence)
- Dollar crash
- Gold and commodities surge
- EM assets: mixed (those with own currency production gain; those with dollar-denominated debt destroyed)
- Equities: initially surge (nominal), then crash in real terms if inflation runs past 10%

## 5. The Geopolitical Dimension

The US debt supercycle is not just a domestic fiscal story. It is a **geopolitical event.**

**Why:**

1. **Adversaries are watching.** China, Russia, and the GCC understand that the US fiscal position constrains its ability to fund military commitments indefinitely. Every wargame about Taiwan includes a financial warfare component — can the US sustain military spending at 3.5% of GDP for a decade-long standoff?

2. **The dollar's reserve status is at stake.** The dollar is trusted because US Treasuries are the world's safest asset. If the r > g dynamic destroys confidence in Treasuries, the entire dollar system is at risk. This is the connection back to Lessons 275–279.

3. **Allies are in the same position.** The UK, France, Italy, and Japan face versions of the same problem. NATO's collective fiscal capacity is being consumed by interest payments. Defence expansion requires either higher taxes, less spending elsewhere, or more debt — all difficult.

4. **Emerging market creditors.** Countries that hold Treasury reserves (Japan, China, Gulf states) face a choice: hold an asset whose real value is eroding, or diversify. Their decisions *are* the bond vigilante story from Lesson 279.

5. **The IMF cannot save a G7 country.** Unlike with EM crises, there is no lender of last resort for the US if confidence breaks. The Fed can print dollars — but printing dollars to buy Treasuries that foreigners don't want is the inflation monetization path above.

## 6. Japan as the Stress Test

Japan is the world's longest-running sovereign debt experiment. At 260% debt/GDP, Japan should have collapsed by orthodox economic models.

**Why hasn't it?**
- Domestic savings dominated the buyer base (90%+ domestically held)
- Yield curve control kept r near zero for decades
- Deflation meant real rates were actually positive — savers didn't flee
- The BoJ bought ~50% of outstanding JGBs through QE

**Why it matters now:**
- YCC is breaking. The BoJ abandoned the 0.5% cap in 2023, then the 1% cap in 2024. JGB yields at 10yr have approached 2%+.
- Domestic savers are retiring, drawing down savings rather than buying more bonds.
- Inflation has arrived — real rates remain negative but less so.
- The carry trade unwind (Lesson 276) creates periodic yen surges that destabilize global markets.

**Japan is a living beta test** for what happens when a r < g regime suddenly flips. If the BoJ loses control of JGB yields, it is the largest sovereign bond market crisis since the Eurozone 2012 — except the BoJ cannot be bailed out.
