## 1. The Structural Setup: Why This Region Is Irreplaceable

The energy transition is, at its core, a **material transition**. Every solar panel requires silver and silicon. Every wind turbine requires copper and rare earths. Every EV battery requires lithium, cobalt, nickel, and manganese. And every transmission line requires more copper.

The concentration of these materials in a single geographic arc — the **Andean copper belt** overlapping with the **lithium triangle** — creates one of the most consequential resource geography facts of the 21st century:

| Mineral | Region's Global Share | Key Countries |
|---|---|---|
| Copper | ~45% of reserves | Chile (27%), Peru (10%), Ecuador (3%), Argentina (3%) |
| Lithium | ~58% of reserves | Bolivia (24%), Argentina (21%), Chile (11%) |
| Silver | ~35% of production | Mexico, Peru, Bolivia |
| Tin | ~25% of production | Bolivia, Peru |

**This is not diversifiable.** Unlike oil, where reserves are spread across the Middle East, Russia, West Africa, and North America, copper and lithium cannot be sourced elsewhere at scale — not in the timeframes required by the IEA's net-zero pathways. The Andean corridor is structurally necessary.

## 2. The Three-Way Competition: US, China, and Local Nationalism

### China's Position: First Mover Advantage

China moved first. Beginning in the early 2000s, Chinese state enterprises (Codelco partnerships, Tianqi's stake in SQM, CATL's investments in Argentina and Bolivia) built a commanding position across the full value chain:

- **Upstream:** Chinese entities now hold significant stakes in copper mines in Peru and Chile, and dominate lithium extraction in Argentina.
- **Processing (the decisive chokepoint):** 65-70% of global lithium is **refined** in China, regardless of where it is mined. Chile can mine raw lithium, but unless it builds processing capacity, it exports a commodity and China extracts the value.
- **Downstream:** CATL, BYD, and their peers consume a majority of the world's battery-grade lithium and copper cathode.

China's strategy is elegant: **own the processing, secure the supply, and let everyone else fight over digging holes in the ground**.

### The US Response: The IRA and Friend-Shoring

The 2022 Inflation Reduction Act was, among other things, a **minerals policy**. The IRA's EV tax credits require an escalating percentage of battery minerals to come from either US soil or free-trade agreement (FTA) partners. Latin America enters here:

- **Chile and Peru have FTAs with the US** — meaning their minerals qualify.
- **Bolivia and Argentina do not** — creating a competitive disadvantage.
- The US signed a preliminary "Critical Minerals Partnership" with Argentina in 2024 and is pushing a formal agreement. The strategic logic: bring LatAm minerals into the IRA-eligible supply chain before Chinese processors capture the full value.

This is **minerals diplomacy**, and it is contested. Chile's left government under President Boric has simultaneously nationalized lithium (creating a new state lithium company in 2023) while pursuing both Chinese and US partnerships. Bolivia has historically reserved lithium for state exploitation — YACIMIENTOS DE LITIO BOLIVIANOS (YLB) — and has been slow to attract the foreign capital needed to industrialize.

### The Third Actor: Resource Nationalism

The region's political economy complicates both superpowers' calculations. Since 2020, virtually every Andean government has drifted left:

| Country | Government | Mineral Policy Posture |
|---|---|---|
| Chile | Boric (left) | State lithium company (Codelco partnership); new royalties on copper |
| Peru | Rotating governments post-Castillo | Persistent community conflict; mine blockades; +15 mines in dispute |
| Bolivia | Arce (MAS, socialist) | State monopoly on lithium; YACIMIENTOS deals with Russia, China |
| Argentina | Milei (libertarian) | *Exception*: aggressive deregulation, FDI-friendly, USD-izing economy |

The resource nationalism thesis is not that these governments will expropriate assets (the 1970s era of hard nationalization is mostly over). It is subtler: **royalty increases, local content requirements, community consent delays, and export restrictions** that raise the effective cost of extraction and reduce investment returns without requiring formal expropriation.

**Peru is the clearest example.** Despite holding the world's largest copper reserves at a single site (Quellaveco, operated by Anglo American), Peru has seen over 50% of its mining output disrupted by community conflicts since 2021. Las Bambas — owned by Chinese MMG — has faced road blockades totaling hundreds of days. The mine is profitable; the political environment is chronically unstable.

## 3. The 2026 Investment Landscape: What Has Changed

**Three developments since 2024 have resharpened the thesis:**

**1. The IRA has survived the 2026 midterms (narrowly).** The Republican Congress modified but did not repeal the domestic content requirements. EV credits remain intact. This keeps North American demand for IRA-eligible minerals elevated — and LatAm FTA partners competitive.

**2. Argentina under Milei has become the region's most FDI-friendly environment in a generation.** The RIGI (Régimen de Incentivos para Grandes Inversiones) law passed in 2024 provides 30-year tax stability, reduced royalties, and free capital repatriation for investments over $200M. This has catalyzed a rush of lithium investment in the Puna region (Salta, Jujuy, Catamarca provinces). BHP, Rio Tinto, Eramet, and POSCO all have active projects.

**3. Copper supply has tightened structurally.** The global copper deficit — deferred for years by recessions and demand weakness — has arrived. Goldman Sachs, in its November 2026 commodity outlook, projects a 2.5 million tonne shortfall by 2030 at current investment levels. The marginal ton of new copper supply increasingly comes from projects with 15-year development timelines and $5B+ capex requirements. This means the price signal for new investment has finally arrived — and LatAm is where it must respond.

## 4. Investment Implications

### Directional Views (6–18 month horizon)

**Copper: Structurally bullish, cyclically noisy**
- The long-run copper thesis is among the highest-conviction macro plays available. Supply constraints are geological and regulatory (not solvable in 2–3 years). Demand from energy transition is structural, not cyclical.
- Risk: China property slowdown reduces near-term industrial copper demand. This is a *timing* risk, not a *thesis* risk.
- **Asset expression:** Copper mining equities (FCX, Antofagasta, Freeport, Southern Copper/SCCO), copper futures, Chile equity index (ECH ETF has ~15% mining weight).

**Lithium: Volatile, differentiated, requires selectivity**
- Lithium is not like copper. It has **shorter development timelines** (brine deposits can be developed in 3–5 years) and **more substitutable end-uses** (sodium-ion batteries are coming). The 2022–2023 lithium price collapse taught that lithium can overshoot on the upside and crash hard.
- The differentiation: **hard rock lithium** (Australia, most of which goes to China for processing) vs **brine lithium** (Andes, cheaper to produce, more geographically constrained). Argentine brine producers with tolling arrangements outside China are the highest-quality exposure.
- **Asset expression:** Lithium Royalty Corp, Lithium Americas (Argentina-focused), Allkem/Livent merger entity, sector ETF (LIT — but watch the Chinese processing weight).

**Argentine peso/assets: Structural reform, execution risk**
- Milei's deregulation program has dramatically improved Argentina's investment climate. The parallel market ARS/USD gap has collapsed. Sovereign bond yields have fallen 1,500+ bps from crisis peaks.
- The tail risk: Argentina has defaulted on sovereign debt **nine times**. Milei's reforms are real, but the political coalition is fragile and the social pain is significant. This is a **long thesis with binary risk** — size accordingly.
- **Asset expression:** Argentina sovereign bonds (AE38, GD35), ADRs of Argentine companies (YPF for energy, MercadoLibre for technology), real assets in the Puna.

**Chilean peso (CLP): Moderate conviction long**
- Chile has the most stable institutional framework in the region. Codelco's nationalized lithium model is investor-skeptical but politically stable. The royalty increase on copper (legislated in 2023) is now priced in.
- CLP is a commodity currency with strong beta to copper price. As the structural copper deficit materializes, CLP should appreciate — but watch for political risk around Boric's approval ratings and 2025 elections.

**Peruvian sol (PEN): Avoid until political stabilization**
- Peru has had 6 presidents in 7 years. The Congress-Executive tension remains acute. Mining conflicts continue. PEN has been surprisingly stable (BCRP has been credible) but equity exposure to Peru mining assets carries severe political risk premium.

## 5. The Framework: Resource Nationalism Premium

The key analytical tool for this region is the **Resource Nationalism Premium (RNP)** — the discount applied to an asset's NPV due to the probability of policy change that degrades future cash flows.

The RNP is not binary (nationalize/don't nationalize). It is a spectrum:
1. **Full nationalization** (expropriation at below-market compensation) — tail risk
2. **Royalty increases** — ongoing, gradual, priceable
3. **Local content requirements** — raises cost structure by 10–30%
4. **Community conflict risk** — delays, blockades, insurance costs
5. **Export restrictions** — Bolivia's occasional threats to restrict lithium exports

**The investment implication:** when RNP is at its peak (resource nationalist government newly elected, commodity prices elevated, public pressure for revenue redistribution), **that is often the best time to buy** — because the market overprices the risk. When the government is four years into its term, the commodity price has moderated, and mines are operating normally, **that is when RNP is underpriced**.

This is a classic contrarian political risk framework: buy when the narrative is worst, sell when it's best.
