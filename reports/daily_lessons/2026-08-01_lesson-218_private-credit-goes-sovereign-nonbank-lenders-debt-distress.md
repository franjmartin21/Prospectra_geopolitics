## Opening Framing Question

> *In 2023, Ghana became one of the first countries to negotiate a restructuring deal simultaneously with the IMF, the Paris Club, a Chinese state bank committee — and a consortium of private credit funds that had lent Ghana $750 million in a commodity-linked structured loan at 14% interest backed by gold export receivables.*

> *The private credit lenders had one structural advantage none of the other creditors shared: their loan agreement included a "ring-fenced" repayment mechanism — gold revenues flowed directly into an offshore account controlled by a New York trustee, bypassing Ghanaian government accounts entirely. Ghana's government could suspend payments on Eurobonds. It could negotiate with China. But it could not touch the gold-backed credit facility without triggering a collateral seizure that would paralyze its entire gold export industry.*

> **Here is the question that opens this lesson:**

> **When a sovereign government borrows from a private credit fund at 14% with its export revenues ring-fenced as collateral, has it gained financing flexibility or surrendered a piece of its economic sovereignty? And when that country eventually needs to restructure — as Ghana did — does the existence of "super-senior" private creditors with collateral rights make the sovereign debt problem easier to solve, or structurally harder?**

## 1. Why Private Credit Entered Sovereign Markets — The Structural Gap

### 1.1 The Retreat of Traditional Lenders

Before 2015, frontier and emerging market sovereigns could tap three reliable pools of capital:
1. **Multilateral lenders** (IMF, World Bank, regional development banks) — cheap but conditional on policy reform
2. **Paris Club bilateral lenders** (Western government-to-government lending) — declining as development lending budgets were cut
3. **Commercial banks** (syndicated loans) — retreating from frontier markets due to Basel III capital requirements that made illiquid sovereign loans more expensive to hold
4. **Bond markets** (Eurobonds) — available to countries with investment-grade or near-IG ratings, but closed to frontier markets below B-/B3

The result: a growing financing gap for roughly 50-70 frontier market sovereigns that were too risky for bond markets, too politically inconvenient for Western bilaterals, and too dependent on Chinese BRI lending — which itself started contracting after 2016 as China confronted its own debt sustainability questions about the BRI portfolio.

**Private credit filled this gap.** By 2026, private credit funds — led by names like Amundi, Ashmore, Gramercy, ExWorks Capital, and specialized boutiques — had deployed an estimated $200-400bn into sovereign and quasi-sovereign markets, predominantly in Sub-Saharan Africa, Central Asia, and the Caribbean.

### 1.2 What Private Credit Offers That Others Don't

Private credit lenders offer sovereigns three things traditional creditors cannot:
- **Speed:** A private credit deal can close in 6-8 weeks. An IMF program takes 12-18 months of negotiations.
- **No conditionality (or light conditionality):** Private lenders generally don't impose structural reform conditions — they just want their money back, and they structure the deal so they can get it.
- **Tailored structures:** Commodity-backed deals, revenue-share arrangements, and receivables financing are all possible — creative financial engineering that multilaterals won't touch.

**The cost:** Private credit comes at yields of 12-20% (vs. 3-5% for multilateral loans and 6-10% for Eurobonds), and with structural features that prioritize repayment in ways that can undermine the sovereign's future flexibility.

## 2. The Structural Architecture of Private Sovereign Credit

### 2.1 Commodity-Backed and Revenue-Linked Structures

The dominant structure in private sovereign lending is the **commodity-backed or receivables-linked loan**, which works as follows:

**Step 1 — Identify a reliable export revenue stream.** Gold, oil, cocoa, copper, diamonds — any commodity with a predictable, foreign-currency-denominated export stream.

**Step 2 — Ring-fence the revenue.** Export proceeds flow into an offshore escrow account (typically in London, New York, or Luxembourg) controlled by a trustee, not the government.

**Step 3 — Service the loan from the escrow.** The trustee automatically deducts interest and principal from the escrow before releasing residual proceeds to the government.

**Step 4 — Embed a remedies clause.** If the government interferes with the escrow mechanism or tries to redirect revenues, the lender can trigger an enforcement mechanism — typically including attachment of commodity export shipments or termination of the export contract that feeds the escrow.

**Real-world example — Republic of Congo (2018):**
Congo-Brazzaville borrowed $1.2bn from commodity trader Trafigura secured against oil export receivables. The deal gave Trafigura effective control over a portion of Congo's oil export proceeds. When Congo entered debt distress in 2019-2020, restructuring its Eurobonds required negotiating with Trafigura first — because Trafigura's oil-backed facility was structurally senior to everything else. Eurobond holders couldn't get meaningful recovery until Trafigura's position was addressed, which created years of delay.

### 2.2 The Super-Senior Problem

By design, commodity-backed and receivables-linked private credit facilities are **structurally senior** to all other creditors — including the IMF in some respects. The IMF has preferred creditor status, meaning sovereigns must pay the IMF before everyone else. But the IMF's preferred status is based on political convention, not legal contract. A commodity-backed credit facility with an offshore escrow is enforceable by law — the trustee holds the money before it ever reaches the government.

**This creates a new creditor hierarchy in sovereign restructurings:**

| Priority | Creditor Class | Basis |
|---------|----------------|-------|
| 1 | IMF/World Bank | Political convention, IMF "preferred creditor" doctrine |
| 2 | Commodity-backed private credit (effectively) | Contractual escrow — money never reaches government |
| 3 | Senior secured bonds | Legal contract |
| 4 | Paris Club bilateral | Negotiated treatment |
| 5 | Chinese bilateral | Negotiated separately |
| 6 | Eurobonds (unsecured) | Legal contract, but structurally subordinated to all above |
| 7 | Holdout litigants | Legal but depends on execution |

**The implication for Eurobond holders:** In any restructuring where private credit with escrow structures exists, Eurobond holders are absorbing more of the haircut than their nominal seniority would suggest. They are subordinated by contractual mechanics to creditors they never anticipated being behind.

### 2.3 The Information Problem

Unlike Eurobonds (which require public disclosure of terms) and Paris Club lending (where aggregate numbers are published), private credit loans to sovereigns are systematically **opaque**:
- Terms are typically confidential
- Loan amounts may not be disclosed to the public or even parliament
- Collateral arrangements (which commodities are ring-fenced, how much) are known only to counterparties
- The existence of some facilities only becomes public when restructuring proceedings reveal them

**This creates a catastrophic information problem for other creditors.** When a bondholder buys a Ghanaian or Congolese Eurobond, they model recovery rates based on publicly available debt stock — which may materially understate the effective seniority claims of private creditors they can't see.

The IMF has repeatedly flagged this as a systemic vulnerability. In its 2025 Global Financial Stability Report, the IMF estimated that **30% of frontier market sovereign debt structures contain undisclosed collateral or escrow provisions** that are invisible to bond market participants.

## 3. The Systemic Implications — Does Private Credit Make Crises Better or Worse?

### 3.1 The Case For: Filling a Genuine Gap

Defenders of private sovereign credit make three arguments:

**Argument 1 — Better than the alternative.** If frontier markets cannot access private credit, they either borrow from China (with its own set of opacity and dependency problems) or don't get the financing they need for infrastructure and budget support. A country that can't fund a hospital or a road because it can't access international credit markets is worse off than one that pays 15% to a private lender.

**Argument 2 — Market discipline.** Private lenders impose discipline that multilaterals often don't — they price risk honestly (hence the high yields) and their escrow structures ensure repayment even when governments are fiscally undisciplined. This creates a forcing function for fiscal management.

**Argument 3 — Speed in crisis.** During a liquidity crisis (not a solvency crisis), a sovereign needs funding fast. A private credit facility can bridge a government through a temporary shock without the 18-month negotiation of an IMF program. Bridge financing preventing default is, net, positive.

### 3.2 The Case Against: Structural Subordination and Moral Hazard

**Argument 1 — They make restructurings harder.** When private creditors with escrow structures exist, restructuring requires either paying them in full (consuming resources that would otherwise be available for macroeconomic stabilization) or fighting them in court (causing protracted delays). Ghana's restructuring was complicated, in part, because private creditors with commodity-backed structures had to be addressed before the Eurobond negotiation could proceed.

**Argument 2 — They create moral hazard for governments.** A finance minister who is politically pressured to balance the budget this quarter can access a commodity-backed credit facility without IMF conditions or parliamentary oversight. This removes the external discipline that multilateral lending provides — the same discipline that might have prevented the borrowing spiral in the first place.

**Argument 3 — They extract from the most vulnerable.** At 14-20% yields with commodity collateral ring-fenced, private credit extracts maximum value from countries with limited alternatives. When a sovereign pays 17% interest on a gold-backed facility while its population lacks basic services, the distributional question is not academic.

**Argument 4 — They pollute the creditor ecosystem.** The super-senior position of commodity-backed creditors means that each new private credit facility reduces the implied recovery rate for existing Eurobond holders. The sovereign is, in effect, issuing new senior debt that subordinates existing creditors — something prohibited in corporate bond markets but largely unregulated in sovereign markets.

### 3.3 The Regulatory Vacuum

Unlike corporate lending, there is **no regulatory framework** governing private sovereign credit:
- No disclosure requirements (in most jurisdictions)
- No seniority rules (each deal negotiates its own priority)
- No comparability requirements in restructuring (private lenders argue they are not subject to Paris Club or Common Framework rules)
- No international body with enforcement authority

The IMF has called for greater transparency and a "Sovereign Debt Roundtable" to include private creditors in restructuring coordination. Progress as of 2026 is minimal. Private credit funds argue that disclosure requirements would kill their market, which is their primary business justification.

## 4. The Investment Angle — Opportunities and Traps

### 4.1 The Opportunity: Being the Private Creditor

For investors who can access these structures (typically via specialized funds rather than directly), private sovereign credit offers:
- **Yield:** 12-20% nominal in hard currency, before any restructuring discount
- **Structural protection:** Collateral rights are real — Congo's Trafigura deal was paid even during the Eurobond restructuring
- **Low correlation:** Private sovereign credit returns are driven by legal contract enforcement, not market sentiment — structurally uncorrelated with public markets

**The risks:**
- **Liquidity:** These positions cannot be sold. You hold until repayment or litigation.
- **Political risk:** A government that decides not to honor contractual obligations has tools beyond legal — nationalization, export licensing, diplomatic pressure on trustees
- **Precedent risk:** As private sovereign credit becomes more visible, regulatory crackdown or Common Framework inclusion becomes more likely

**Accessible expressions:** Dedicated private credit vehicles (Amundi Frontier Markets Credit, Gramercy Opportunity Fund), specialized distressed EM funds, or co-investment in structured transactions alongside development finance institutions (OPIC/DFC in the U.S. context).

### 4.2 The Trap: Holding Eurobonds in Countries with Hidden Private Credit

The more actionable insight for most investors is negative: **avoid Eurobonds of countries with significant undisclosed private credit exposure.**

**Warning signs to look for:**
- Rapidly growing "other external debt" line items in IMF Article IV reports
- Declining FX reserve coverage despite apparently healthy balance of payments
- Commodity export statistics that show volumes but not revenue (suggesting revenue diversion to offshore escrow)
- Debt sustainability analyses that show "below the line" financing items without clear counterparty identification
- Rating agency reports that note "opaque debt structure" or "limited information on non-bonded debt"

**Countries where this is a known or suspected issue (2026):** Republic of Congo (Trafigura), Mozambique (historical tuna bonds scandal evolved into private credit structures), some CPEC-related Pakistani quasi-sovereign entities, several Central Asian states where Chinese commodity-backed financing is partially disclosed.

### 4.3 The Emerging Opportunity: Post-Restructuring Recovery Plays

After a sovereign completes restructuring — and has resolved its private credit positions — the residual Eurobonds often trade at dramatically depressed levels relative to the newly sustainable debt load. Post-restructuring entry has historically been the highest-return sovereign credit play:

- **Ghana 2025:** Post-restructuring bonds issued at 35 cents (implied yield: ~22%) traded up to 60-65 cents within 12 months as IMF program stabilized fiscal position
- **Zambia 2025-2026:** Similar trajectory — new bonds at deep discount, recovering as copper revenues and IMF disbursements confirmed sustainability
- **Sri Lanka 2024-2025:** Most dramatic recovery — bonds at 25 cents in 2023, above 60 cents by late 2024 as tourism recovery + IMF program created credible trajectory

**The entry criterion:** Restructuring is complete, IMF program is locked, primary fiscal balance is in surplus, and the private credit positions have been resolved (no more hidden senior claims). At this point, the risk is macro, not structural.

## 5. A Concrete Example — Mozambique's Tuna Bond Scandal and Its Private Credit Afterlife

### 5.1 The Original Scandal (2013-2016)

In 2013, the government of Mozambique secretly borrowed $2.2bn through two state-owned companies (EMATUM for tuna fishing, MAM for maritime surveillance) — a debt hidden from parliament, the public, and the IMF. The loans were organized by Credit Suisse and VTB Capital (Russia), with $350mn in fees for the banks.

The borrowed money largely disappeared — into corruption, overpriced contracts, and military equipment of questionable purpose. EMATUM's tuna fishing fleet mostly sat idle.

When the hidden debt was revealed in 2016, the IMF suspended its program. Mozambique's currency collapsed 50%. Inflation surged. The country entered a prolonged period of fiscal crisis and restructuring negotiations that lasted until 2019.

### 5.2 The Private Credit Aftermath

During Mozambique's restructuring, international investors avoided the country. But from 2020-2022, a new wave of private credit — specifically commodity-backed financing tied to Mozambique's massive natural gas deposits (Total and Eni's offshore fields) — entered the market.

The LNG projects in Cabo Delgado required bridging finance while construction proceeded. Several private credit facilities were structured against future LNG export receivables. These facilities carried 12-16% yields and ring-fenced LNG revenue in offshore accounts.

**The complication:** Cabo Delgado was also the center of a jihadist insurgency (backed, reportedly, by ISIS Mozambique Province). When the insurgency disrupted LNG construction in 2021, the LNG receivables underpinning the private credit facilities were delayed indefinitely. The private credit funds that had lent against those receivables found themselves holding illiquid positions in a country whose primary new revenue source was surrounded by active conflict.

**The investment lesson:** Even well-structured private sovereign credit (commodity-backed, offshore escrow, robust legal documentation) is exposed to force majeure political and security risks that no amount of financial engineering can hedge. The collateral is only as good as the country's ability to export the commodity that backs it.

**CEO Portfolio Note:** The primary actionable insight from this lesson is *negative selection* — avoiding frontier bonds where private credit contamination subordinates Eurobond holders invisibly. This is a rare case where the best alpha is in what you don't own.

## 8. Reflection Questions

1. **The sovereignty question:** A country that borrows from the IMF must accept structural reform conditions — reduced subsidies, currency flexibility, public sector reform. A country that borrows from a private credit fund accepts no conditions but ring-fences its export revenues. Which is a greater infringement on sovereignty: policy conditions you can negotiate and argue about, or structural cash-flow controls you cannot override? Does your answer change depending on whether the country is a democracy?

2. **The disclosure dilemma:** Private credit funds argue that if they are forced to disclose loan terms publicly, borrowers will be exposed to adverse market reaction and the deals will become uneconomic. Eurobond holders argue that undisclosed senior debt structures that subordinate them invisibly is a form of market manipulation. Who is right, and what regulatory framework would you design to balance these interests? Consider: could you require disclosure to a multilateral body (IMF) without public disclosure?

3. **Apply the framework to Nigeria:** Nigeria has $30bn in external Eurobonds, a reformed oil sector generating increased revenue, but also a history of quasi-sovereign borrowing by NNPC (the national oil company) through opaque off-balance-sheet structures. Before buying Nigerian Eurobonds at 700bps spread, how would you assess the private credit contamination risk, and what indicators would give you confidence that the bonds are not structurally subordinated by hidden NNPC commodity-backed facilities?
