## Part I: The Architecture of Russia's War Economy — What Five Years of Mobilization Have Built

### The Numbers (as of Q1 2026)

Russia's war machine is not fragile. It is also not healthy. It is something more dangerous for investors: **stable enough to persist, stressed enough to distort everything around it**.

**Military spending:**
- Planned defense budget for 2026: 14.9–15.5 trillion rubles (~6.3–7.2% of GDP)
- Military and national security now consume approximately **50% of Russia's federal budget**
- Russia has more than doubled its military spending over the past five years
- Result: Russia now holds the world's **third-largest defense budget**, behind only the US and China

**Economic performance:**
- GDP growth: Ministry of Economic Development initially forecast 1.3% for 2026; revised down to **0.4%** by May 2026 — effectively stagnation
- Central Bank rate: **16%** — the highest in the post-Soviet era outside of crisis episodes
- Inflation: 5.6% end of 2025, projected 5.2% in 2026 — but structural price pressures are severe: food prices +21% year-on-year in early 2026, services +14%, fuel +11%
- Oil and gas revenues: projected at 8.7 trillion rubles in 2025, **25%+ below plan** due to ruble appreciation and the tightening Western sanctions regime
- Brent crude: World Bank forecasts $60/barrel in 2026, down from $68 in 2025 — the oil price floor that Russia's budget math depends on

**What this means structurally:** Russia is running a war economy that grows the defense sector while squeezing everything else. Defense production output grew 20–30% annually in 2023–2024 — but has now decelerated to 5–7% in 2026 as production capacity hits physical limits: tooling, skilled labor, component supply from sanctioned suppliers.

### How Russia Is Financing the War

Russia's war is funded through three mechanisms, each with a distinct sustainability profile:

**1. Oil and gas revenues (primary, but declining)**
Pre-war, oil and gas revenues funded ~40–45% of the federal budget. Sanctions, the G7 price cap, and Russia's pivot to discounted sales to China, India, and Turkey have maintained volumes — but at deeply discounted prices. The Urals-Brent spread widened to $25–30/barrel at peak sanctions tightening. Even with China and India absorbing diverted flows, the revenue base has compressed materially.

**2. Domestic borrowing and the National Wealth Fund (shrinking buffer)**
Russia has tapped its sovereign wealth fund (the National Wealth Fund) and expanded domestic government bond issuance (OFZ bonds) at increasingly punishing interest rates. With the central bank at 16%, servicing new debt is expensive. The National Wealth Fund's liquid assets have declined from ~$113 billion pre-war to estimated $50–60 billion — a runway that exists but is finite at current burn rates.

**3. Tax increases and household transfer**
VAT has risen from 20% to 22% as of January 2026. More businesses have been brought into the VAT system. Real income growth has slowed from 7.7% in 2025 to a projected 1.6% in 2026. Consumer spending growth eased from 4% to 1.2%. The Kremlin is transferring the cost of the war onto households — slowly, but structurally. This is the **political risk variable** investors underweight: the domestic tolerance threshold for declining living standards.

### The Sanctions Regime: How Much Is It Doing?

As of April 2026, the EU imposed its **20th round of sanctions** on Russia — targeting energy revenues, the military-industrial complex, trade, and financial services including crypto. The cumulative effect is real but slower-acting than initially anticipated:

- Russia has rerouted trade through Central Asia, Turkey, UAE, and China — "parallel import" channels for sanctioned goods
- The ruble has remained more stable than early sanctions models predicted — partly because of capital controls, partly because Russia runs a current account surplus (it still exports more than it imports, just to different buyers)
- Technology denial — particularly semiconductor and advanced manufacturing equipment restrictions — is showing up in production quality constraints and the slowdown in defense output growth from 25% to 5-7% per year

**The honest assessment:** Sanctions have degraded Russia's war economy but not broken it. The timeline for sanctions delivering decisive economic pressure has repeatedly been extended. The mechanism exists; the speed of impact has disappointed optimists.

## Part II: The Four Endgame Scenarios — A Structural Framework

Analysts across CSIS, Chatham House, OIIP, and ORF have converged on **four plausible scenarios** for how the Russia-Ukraine war resolves. Each has a distinct probability distribution and distinct investment implications.

### Scenario 1: Russian Breakthrough / Ukrainian Military Collapse
**Probability: 10–15% (low but non-zero)**

Russia achieves a decisive military breakthrough, Ukrainian frontline defenses collapse, and Kyiv is forced to negotiate under duress — ceding substantial territory, agreeing to neutrality, and abandoning NATO accession.

**How it happens:** Sustained attrition exhausts Ukraine's manpower and materiel faster than Western resupply can compensate. Trump administration reduces or pauses military aid. Russian artillery and drone campaigns degrade Ukrainian logistics to the point of frontline collapse in one or more key sectors.

**Investment implications:**
- **European defense:** Surge — threat perception maxes out; NATO spending commitments accelerate
- **European assets broadly:** Risk-off; EUR weakens, European equities sell off, spreads widen for Eastern European sovereigns (Poland, Baltic states re-price dramatically)
- **Russian assets (if sanctions relief follows):** Theoretically positive — but the sanctions architecture does not automatically lift on a Russian victory; this is not a ceasefire where both sides can claim progress
- **Commodities:** Oil does not necessarily drop — Russian production under sanctions is already discounted. European gas demand for non-Russian alternatives stays elevated.
- **Emerging markets:** EM sovereign risk reprices as the signal that deterrence has failed in Europe ripples globally — Taiwan risk premium rises, Korean peninsula premium rises

**The signal to watch:** Ukrainian frontline stabilization metrics — kilometers gained/lost per week, artillery ammunition availability, manpower replacement rates. These are the leading indicators of Scenario 1 becoming live.

### Scenario 2: Prolonged Attritional Stalemate
**Probability: 40–50% (base case)**

Neither side achieves decisive advantage. The conflict grinds along a relatively stable frontline — with periodic offensives and retreats but no strategic breakthrough — for another 2–4 years. This is the Korean War model: active conflict that eventually hardens into a frozen conflict, possibly with a ceasefire that is de facto but not de jure.

**How it happens:** Western military aid sustains Ukraine's defensive capacity; Russian war economy sustains offensive operations; neither political leadership can accept the domestic cost of capitulating. Negotiations periodically surface and collapse because Moscow's terms (territorial concessions, Ukrainian neutrality, no NATO) remain unacceptable to Kyiv.

**Investment implications:**
- **European energy:** Elevated but normalizing. TTF gas prices have stabilized around €30/MWh with new LNG capacity coming online globally. The EU's LNG import infrastructure buildout (now completed for practical purposes) has structurally reduced the war's energy leverage over Europe.
- **European defense:** Multi-year tailwind continues. Stalemate justifies continued NATO spending commitments — Germany's Sondervermögen remains committed; France's defense industrial policy intact.
- **Russian oil markets:** Continued discount to Brent; China and India remain the primary buyers; oil price sensitivity for EM importers persists but is priced-in
- **EM sovereign debt:** Differentiated — countries most dependent on Russian energy or exposed to grain market disruption (Egypt, Pakistan, sub-Saharan Africa) face ongoing structural pressure; others have adjusted

**The signal to watch:** NATO member defense budget commitments vs. actual execution. Stalemate is only a positive for defense equities if political commitments translate into procurement contracts — delivery lags matter.

### Scenario 3: Negotiated Ceasefire ("The Georgian Scenario")
**Probability: 25–35%**

Diplomatic pressure — particularly from the US under Trump, combined with Russian economic stress and Ukrainian exhaustion — produces a ceasefire along approximately current lines of contact. Russia retains de facto control of occupied territories (Donbas, Zaporizhzhia, Kherson, Crimea); Ukraine retains sovereignty over the remainder but does not formally cede territory; security guarantees remain ambiguous. Named after the 2008 Russia-Georgia war outcome: a frozen conflict with Russian-recognized breakaway regions that the international community does not formally accept.

**How it happens:** The Trump administration's transactional approach prioritizes a deal — any deal — that can be marketed as "ending the war." European allies reluctantly endorse because the alternative (continued escalation) is worse. Ukraine accepts under duress with informal security guarantees from France, UK, Poland, and Germany.

**Investment implications:**
- **European energy:** Gas prices ease further — ceasefire reduces immediate supply disruption risk; TTF could fall toward €25–26/MWh. But the EU's LNG import infrastructure and diversification remain permanent (no return to Russian pipeline dependence is politically viable)
- **Ukrainian reconstruction:** The most significant new investment theme post-ceasefire. Ukraine's reconstruction is estimated at €400–550 billion over 10 years (World Bank/EU Joint Needs Assessment). Key sectors: infrastructure, housing, energy, agriculture, logistics. Germany, Poland, and the Baltic states become the reconstruction gateway economies.
- **Commodity markets:** Wheat and grain markets normalize as Black Sea export routes stabilize. Ukraine's agricultural exports — sunflower oil, corn, wheat — recover over 12–18 months as infrastructure is rebuilt.
- **Russian assets:** Sanctions do not automatically lift on ceasefire — the EU has made this explicit. Russia's frozen assets (~$300B in European financial institutions) remain in limbo. Partial sanctions relief might be negotiated but not immediate.
- **Ruble:** Modest appreciation pressure if oil market confidence improves; but structural constraints (sanctions, capital controls) prevent a full normalization

**The signal to watch:** US-Russia diplomatic contact intensity. A ceasefire requires American diplomatic cover — Trump calling Putin is the leading indicator, not the lagging one.

### Scenario 4: Comprehensive Peace Agreement ("The Finnish Scenario")
**Probability: 5–10% (low horizon probability, rising over 5+ years)**

A negotiated peace agreement with defined borders, formal territorial provisions, and a security architecture — potentially involving Ukrainian neutrality, guaranteed by multiple powers, with partial sanctions relief for Russia. Named after the 1940 Winter War outcome: Finland lost territory (Karelia) but retained sovereignty and democratic governance; the settlement was stable.

**How it happens:** Both sides exhaust political will and economic capacity simultaneously; a credible third-party guarantor (potentially China, Turkey, or a multilateral framework) structures a deal that gives each side a face-saving narrative. This requires Putin to accept Ukraine's continued existence as a sovereign state — which he has not yet signaled.

**Investment implications:**
- **Global risk appetite:** Material positive — a "war ends" headline would be one of the most significant risk-on catalysts since COVID vaccines
- **European equities:** Structural positive for reconstruction-oriented sectors; DAX and CAC40 would rally significantly on reduced tail risk
- **Commodities:** Mixed — oil stabilizes or weakens slightly (Russian supply could partially normalize); wheat, sunflower, corn ease; critical minerals outlook improves as Ukrainian production recovers
- **EM FX:** Risk-on broad EM bid; Turkish lira, Polish zloty, Czech koruna, Romanian leu benefit from reduced proximity risk
- **Russian equities/bonds:** Conditional on sanctions architecture — a full peace deal would require phased relief to generate investable Russian assets; currently uninvestable for Western institutions

## Part III: The Macro Overlay — What Russia's War Economy Tells Us About the Global Investment Regime

The Russia-Ukraine war is not a regional conflict with localized investment implications. It is a **structural test of the post-Cold War economic order** — and that test has now run for five years with conclusions investors should internalize:

### Conclusion 1: Sanctions Work Slowly, and Markets Price Them Wrong

The initial market reaction to 2022 sanctions was to price "Russia collapses in 6 months." That was wrong. Russia's economy absorbed the shock, pivoted its trade flows, and sustained military operations. The lesson: **economic coercion is a slow-acting instrument** — it degrades capacity over years, not quarters. Investors who price geopolitical economic sanctions as binary events (either they work immediately or they fail) will misprice both the weapon and the target.

### Conclusion 2: Defense Spending Is Sticky Once Started

Five consecutive years of 6–7% of GDP defense spending has created an entrenched constituency — the Russian military-industrial complex, defense towns, veterans organizations. Even if the war ends, Russian defense spending will not revert to pre-2022 levels. This creates a structural floor for Russian government expenditure. The same logic applies to European defense commitments — the political economy of rearmament is asymmetric: it is easier to increase than to decrease.

### Conclusion 3: Energy Diversification Is Now Permanent

The war has permanently restructured European energy supply chains. The EU's LNG import terminals, renewable buildout, and demand reduction programs were investments made under crisis — they do not get unbuilt when the crisis ends. This means:
- European energy security is structurally better than 2021 but at higher structural cost
- The TTF-Henry Hub spread has normalized but remains elevated vs. pre-war
- The EU gas ban on Russian LNG by December 2026 and pipeline gas by September 2027 is politically irreversible — regardless of ceasefire

### Conclusion 4: War Economies Have a Breaking Point — But It Is Later Than You Think

Every historical war economy has eventually broken down: World War I economies collapsed from 1917–1918; Soviet economics of the Afghan war contributed to the USSR's collapse in the late 1980s. Russia's war economy will eventually face a reckoning — falling oil revenues, declining real incomes, central bank rates destroying credit growth, and deferred capital investment creating a structural productivity deficit. But "eventually" is not a tradable timeframe. The correct positioning is **not** to bet on an imminent Russian economic collapse; it is to position for the structural consequences of a prolonged war economy in Russia affecting global commodity, energy, and EM markets.

### CEO Portfolio Note — Base Case (Scenario 2: Stalemate)

In the base case, the current regime persists:
- **European defense equities remain a structural long.** Rheinmetall (RHM), KNDS, BAE Systems, Leonardo, Thales, Saab — all benefit from the sustained NATO spending ramp regardless of whether the war resolves.
- **Ukrainian reconstruction debt/bonds** — wait for a ceasefire signal before positioning. The opportunity is enormous but requires a political catalyst that has not yet materialized.
- **European natural gas (TTF):** Near-term normalizing due to new LNG capacity; but the EU LNG infrastructure buildout has created structural demand for US LNG exports — a secondary beneficiary (Cheniere Energy, NextDecade, Venture Global).
- **EM FX:** Polish zloty and Romanian leu remain sensitive to frontline developments — hedge EM Eastern European exposure against escalation scenarios.
- **Gold:** Remains a regime-change hedge. As long as Scenario 1 probability is non-trivial, gold deserves its premium.

5. Defense production index (proxy from procurement data)

**The analytical output:** A weekly "Russia War Economy Composite" score. When the score deteriorates past a threshold, it signals increasing probability of an endgame scenario transition — either toward ceasefire (negotiation from weakness) or toward escalation (pressure-release through military action). Pairs directly with your European energy pipeline (TTF, LNG terminal capacity) and the European defense procurement tracker.
