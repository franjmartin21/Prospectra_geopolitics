## Opening Framing Question

*On February 25, 2026 — three days before the bombs fell on Tehran — Prime Minister Narendra Modi stood in Jerusalem and signed a framework upgrading India-Israel ties to a "special strategic partnership."*

*Three days later, the US and Israel launched coordinated strikes on Iranian military infrastructure, launching the 2026 Iran War.*

*Iran's response to Modi's timing was precise and non-military: it gave blanket permission for Pakistani-flagged tankers to transit the Strait of Hormuz. Not Indian-flagged. Pakistani.*

*Eighteen Indian crude oil tankers and LPG carriers sat trapped in the Persian Gulf. India's crude basket surged from $69/bbl in February to $113/bbl in March. The Indian Navy launched an emergency maritime security operation — Operation Urja Suraksha — deploying five frontline warships to escort Indian-flagged vessels out of the Strait. And as the ceasefire negotiations began in June, the back-channel interlocutors were Egypt, Turkey, Saudi Arabia, and Pakistan. Not India.*

*India, the world's largest democracy, the self-proclaimed Vishwaguru of a multipolar world, the fulcrum of the Quad, the country that had spent years positioning itself as the indispensable swing state between the US-led and China-Russia blocs — woke up diplomatically isolated from both sides of a war it was energetically being taxed to fund.*

*The question for today's lesson is not what went wrong for Modi. The question is: what does India's structural position in this war tell you about how major emerging market powers navigate great-power conflicts — and what does that tell you about where to invest?*

## 1. The Strategic Autonomy Doctrine: Background

India's foreign policy has operated under the "strategic autonomy" doctrine since Independence — the explicit rejection of formal alliances in favor of relationships with all major powers simultaneously. It is the heir to the Non-Aligned Movement that Nehru, Nasser, and Tito launched in 1961.

But 2026 India's version of strategic autonomy is qualitatively different from Nehru's. It is not ideological non-alignment. It is *transactional multi-alignment*: India simultaneously holds:

- **Membership in the Quad** (US, Japan, Australia, India) — explicitly framed as a democratic security architecture against Chinese maritime expansion
- **Membership in the SCO** (Shanghai Cooperation Organisation, with China, Russia, Pakistan, and Iran) — Eurasia's premier security bloc dominated by the revisionist powers
- **Membership in BRICS+** — the emerging-market financial coordination mechanism with explicit de-dollarization ambitions
- **A deepening defense partnership with the US** — including the iCET (Initiative on Critical and Emerging Technologies) signed in 2023, covering semiconductor partnerships, defense co-production, and AI cooperation
- **Ongoing energy imports from Russia** (deeply discounted Urals crude since 2022 sanctions on Russia)

This is the architecture of a country that has decided it can simultaneously be in every room. The 2026 Iran War has tested whether that is a sustainable strategy — or a strategy that fails when forced to choose.

## 2. Modi's Israel Tilt: The Strategic Miscalculation

Prime Minister Modi visited Israel on February 23-25, 2026. The visit produced:
- A "Special Strategic Partnership" framework with Israel
- An agreement expanding defense co-production (joint drone manufacturing, missile defense cooperation)
- A bilateral trade target of $50 billion by 2030
- A joint statement that included language supportive of Israel's "right to self-defense"

The timing was disastrous in retrospect. US and Israeli intelligence assessments of the imminent Iran operation were among the most closely held secrets of the early 2026 period. Whether Modi's government had been briefed on the operation's imminence is contested — but the optics were devastating. India's head of government signed a defense partnership with Israel, then Israel launched a war three days later. From Tehran's perspective, India had chosen a side.

**What Iran did in response was geometrically precise:**

Iran did not break diplomatic relations with India. It did not expel Indian workers. It imposed no formal sanctions. Instead, it did something far more painful: it administered a *selective Hormuz penalty*. Iranian naval vessels and maritime authorities made clear — through informal coordination rather than formal decree — that Pakistan-flagged vessels would be given passage through the strait that Indian-flagged vessels would not.

The result:
- 18 Indian tankers stranded in the Persian Gulf
- India's LPG imports effectively halted for several weeks in March 2026
- India's crude basket surged 64% in four weeks (from $69 to $113/bbl)
- The Indian Navy was forced to launch Operation Urja Suraksha — an embarrassing deployment of five warships to protect Indian energy tankers in a region India had claimed as part of its natural maritime sphere

India was being taxed by a war it had accidentally endorsed and could not influence.

## 3. The Pakistan Shock: Six Reasons This Is a Strategic Catastrophe

Pakistan's emergence as a primary US-Iran back-channel interlocutor (alongside Egypt, Turkey, and Saudi Arabia) is — from India's perspective — not simply a diplomatic setback. It is a structural reversal of a decade of Indian foreign policy investment.

Here is why:

### 3.1 — The Isolation Project Collapsed
Modi's government had spent years pursuing a systematic strategy to isolate Pakistan diplomatically: labeling Pakistan-based militant groups as terrorist organizations in international forums, pressuring partners to reduce security cooperation with Islamabad, and building India's narrative as the responsible democratic power versus Pakistan's failing state narrative. The 2026 war handed Pakistan a high-visibility diplomatic role that directly inverts this narrative. Pakistan is now a peace-broker in a major geopolitical crisis. India is a bystander.

### 3.2 — The Strait of Hormuz Asymmetry
Iran's preferential treatment of Pakistani-flagged vessels through the Strait of Hormuz demonstrated that Pakistan had maintained functional diplomatic and religious relationships with Tehran that India had sacrificed by its Israel tilt. Pakistan is majority Sunni but has a significant Shia population and cultural-religious connections to Iran; Pakistan's military and ISI have maintained back-channel relationships with the Iranian Revolutionary Guard for decades, grounded partly in shared opposition to Sunni extremist groups that threaten both states. India's transactional multi-alignment had not built this depth of relationship.

### 3.3 — The Quad's Limits Were Exposed
India joined the Quad as a hedge against Chinese maritime aggression — it was never intended to turn India into a US security ally in Middle East conflicts. But the perception from Tehran was different: an India that signs security frameworks with Israel three days before the bombs fall is functionally an aligned state. The Quad membership that was supposed to give India leverage in its relationship with the US instead became a constraint on India's relationship with Iran.

### 3.4 — The SCO Membership Proved Worthless
India is an SCO member alongside Iran. The SCO's founding principles explicitly include non-interference and resolution of disputes through dialogue. India could theoretically have leveraged its SCO membership to position itself as a mediating voice. Instead, India's SCO membership became a source of embarrassment: how does the world's largest democracy explain being a security partner of the US (the belligerent) and an SCO partner of Iran (the target) while having nothing to show for either relationship in terms of diplomatic influence?

### 3.5 — The Pakistan Rehabilitation Risk
India has consistently argued in international forums that Pakistan is a state sponsor of terrorism that cannot be trusted with nuclear weapons or diplomatic responsibility. Pakistan's back-channel role in the Iran war — confirmed by US officials, reported widely, and acknowledged by the Pakistani military — is a direct rehabilitation of Pakistan's international standing. India's narrative has been set back years.

### 3.6 — The China Shadow
China maintained its strategic relationship with Iran throughout the war (the China-Iran 25-year cooperation agreement signed in 2021 remained in force). China did not join the back-channel but gained enormous influence through its role as Iran's primary economic lifeline during the sanctions period. India, which had explicitly sought to position itself as an alternative to Chinese influence in the developing world, is now watching China emerge from this crisis with deeper Iranian relationships — while India has damaged its own.

## 4. India's Energy Exposure: The Structural Vulnerability

India is the world's third-largest oil importer. This is the macro fact that makes every geopolitical miscalculation enormously expensive.

**The numbers:**
- India imports approximately 85% of its crude oil requirements
- Pre-war, the Indian crude basket (a blend of the grades India actually imports) averaged $69/bbl
- Post-Hormuz disruption, the basket surged to $113/bbl — a 64% increase in four weeks
- Every $10/bbl increase in oil prices widens India's current account deficit by approximately $12-14 billion annually
- 91% of India's LPG (used for household cooking by 300 million+ consumers) comes from the Gulf
- Qatar supplies approximately 50% of India's LNG requirements
- India's total energy import bill at pre-war prices was approximately $180-200 billion/year; at $110+ crude, that approaches $270-300 billion

**The rupee channel:**

Oil price surges work through the Indian economy in a specific transmission mechanism that the Databricks platform should model precisely:

1. Higher oil → higher import bill → larger current account deficit
2. Larger CAD → rupee weakening (need to sell more rupees to buy dollars for oil)
3. Rupee weakening → imported inflation (everything from electronics to food inputs becomes more expensive)
4. Imported inflation → RBI forced to choose between rate hikes (to fight inflation) or rate cuts (to support growth)
5. Rate hike uncertainty → foreign institutional investors pull capital from Indian equities → Nifty falls
6. Nifty falls → wealth effect → consumption slowdown → corporate earnings miss

This is the full transmission chain from $10/bbl oil increase to 1-2% Nifty 50 correction. The July 24 market action — Sensex -331 points, Nifty below 23,750 — was this chain in real time, with Brent above $92/bbl.

**Operation Urja Suraksha as strategic signal:**

The Indian Navy's deployment of five frontline warships to escort tankers out of the Strait of Hormuz (launched March 25, 2026) is strategically significant beyond its immediate energy security purpose. It demonstrates:
- India is willing to deploy hard power to protect energy supply lines
- India has the naval capability to operate in the Gulf of Oman/Arabian Sea (its traditional maritime zone)
- India did not seek US permission or coordination for this deployment — a deliberate signal of independent operational capability

But it also reveals the limits: India could escort tankers *out* of the Strait, but could not compel Iranian permission for Indian-flagged vessels *into* the Strait. The Navy could protect the exit; it could not fix the diplomatic relationship that created the problem.

## 5. India's Strategic Response: Adaptation Under Constraint

Faced with energy disruption, diplomatic isolation, and a Pakistan-shaped wound, India has moved on multiple fronts:

### 5.1 — Iran Oil Imports Resumed
After a 7-year hiatus (India had complied with US secondary sanctions on Iran post-2018 maximum pressure campaign), India resumed oil and gas imports from Iran in the war's aftermath. This is a significant signal: it tells Washington that India's compliance with US sanctions has limits, and that energy security will override alliance politics when forced to choose. The CNBC headline — "India turns to Iran for oil and gas after 7-year hiatus, signaling limits to US tilt" — captures the strategic message precisely.

### 5.2 — Strategic Petroleum Reserve Expansion
India has announced plans to expand its strategic petroleum reserves from current levels (approximately 5 million tonnes) and deepen supply partnerships with non-Gulf producers. Target suppliers: US, Russia, Guyana, and Brazil. This is the structural response to the Hormuz vulnerability — reduce dependence on Gulf routes by diversifying the supply base.

### 5.3 — Refining Capacity Expansion
India's refining capacity is set to expand approximately 20% to 6.2 million barrels/day by 2030. This is partly an economic play (India wants to become an Asian refining hub, processing crude from diverse sources for domestic use and re-export), and partly a strategic hedge: with more refining capacity, India can process heavier, cheaper grades from sanctioned producers (Russia, Iran) without being dependent on any single supply source.

### 5.4 — Import Diversification (US, Guyana, Brazil)
India has accelerated purchase agreements with Guyana (which came online as a major crude producer in the early 2020s), Brazil (particularly pre-salt deepwater crude from Petrobras), and US Gulf Coast crude. This is the long-term answer to Gulf dependence.

## 6. Investment Implications: India as the Net Energy Importer Case Study

India is the canonical example of a major EM economy that *suffers* from oil price increases rather than benefiting from them. The Gulf Arc positions (long energy, long GCC) have an inverse relationship with Indian portfolio positions.

### 6.1 — The Structural Short: Indian Broad Equities in High Oil Environments

**Thesis:** Every $10/bbl increase in oil above India's fiscal baseline ($75-80/bbl) creates a predictable earnings compression across the Indian market, concentrated in consumer discretionary, industrials, and financials (via the rupee-inflation transmission chain). The Nifty 50 at 23,750 (July 24, 2026) with Brent above $92 is structurally under pressure until oil comes down OR the rupee stabilizes through RBI intervention.

**Vehicles:** Short iShares MSCI India ETF (INDA) or short Nifty futures (via derivatives) as a hedge against long energy positions. Alternatively, underweight India in EM allocation.

**Falsification condition:** Oil falls below $80/bbl (the war premium collapses), or India negotiates a bilateral energy arrangement that insulates it from spot price volatility (e.g., a multi-year Iranian oil contract at fixed prices).

### 6.2 — The Sector Opportunity: IT Relative Strength

Within India, the IT sector is structurally insulated from the oil-inflation transmission chain. Infosys, TCS, Wipro, and HCL Tech generate the majority of their revenue in USD from US clients. When the rupee weakens (due to high oil), their USD-denominated revenues become *more* valuable in INR terms — a natural hedge. The July 24 market data showed IT and media sectors gaining while energy, auto, and metals fell. This sector rotation pattern is systematic in high-oil environments.

**Long thesis:** Indian IT as a relative value long within India allocation during elevated oil periods. The rupee weakness that hurts the broad market mechanically improves IT earnings in INR terms.

**Vehicles:** iShares India 50 ETF filtered for IT; direct exposure via Infosys ADR (INFY), Wipro ADR (WIT).

### 6.3 — The Defense Play

India's inability to influence the Iran war's trajectory has accelerated domestic consensus that strategic autonomy requires military self-sufficiency. India's defense budget has been expanding; the war is likely to accelerate procurement. The "Make in India" defense program — Hindustan Aeronautics Limited, Bharat Electronics Limited, and domestic defense supply chains — will receive additional funding.

**Long thesis:** Indian defense industrial complex as a structural beneficiary of the war's strategic lessons. This is a 2-4 year thesis, not an event trade.

**Vehicles:** BSE-listed defense contractors via India-focused mutual funds; Bharat Electronics (BEL IN) if accessible; Hindustan Aeronautics (HAL IN).

### 6.4 — The Strategic Petroleum Reserve and Storage Infrastructure

India's announced expansion of its strategic petroleum reserves creates demand for storage infrastructure, pipeline capacity, and refining expansion projects. Indian energy infrastructure companies and the refiners (Indian Oil Corporation, BPCL, HPCL) will receive government-directed investment.

**Caveat:** PSU (public sector undertaking) energy companies in India trade at persistent discounts because of government subsidy obligations — the government often forces PSU refiners to sell petroleum products below market prices to control inflation. This political intervention risk partially negates the infrastructure expansion thesis.

### 6.5 — The Rupee as the Indicator

INR/USD is the cleanest real-time signal of India's geopolitical stress in this war. When oil rises → rupee weakens → INR/USD rises (fewer dollars per rupee). Track INR/USD as the leading indicator for when to reduce Indian equity exposure.

**Rule:** If INR/USD crosses 86 (rupee below 86 per dollar) and oil remains above $100/bbl, India's current account math becomes structurally problematic and a broader equity correction follows. Use this as a trigger for reducing India allocation.

## 7. The Broader EM Lesson: What India Teaches About Non-Aligned States in Great Power Conflicts

India's experience in the 2026 Iran War is the textbook case study for a lesson that will apply to every emerging market in the curriculum:

**Structural non-alignment is not a free lunch.** Every EM country that attempts to maintain relationships with both the US-led and China/Russia-led blocs pays a cost: it cannot fully benefit from alignment with either, and in a crisis, it can be punished by both. India's version of this cost was precise and humiliating — strategic isolation from the one war where it had the most economic exposure.

**Energy dependence is the structural constraint that overrides all strategic posturing.** India's "strategic autonomy" doctrine is sophisticated, well-articulated, and genuinely valued by Indian policymakers. But when Iran blocked the Strait to Indian tankers, strategic autonomy collapsed into a single question: where is the crude coming from? Energy dependence is not a policy variable — it is a geological and infrastructure constraint that takes 10-15 years to structurally reduce. Every EM strategic calculation has this constraint at its base.

**Diplomatic relationships require maintenance between crises.** India had not maintained the depth of relationship with Iran that its energy dependence required. Pakistan had. The asymmetry was not ideological — Pakistan is not more pro-Iran than India — it was institutional: Pakistan's military-intelligence apparatus had maintained quiet, functional channels with Iran that Indian diplomacy had neglected since the 2018 sanctions compliance. When the crisis hit, Pakistan had an instrument. India did not.

**For investors:** Non-aligned EM countries carry a "strategic ambiguity discount" — markets discount their equities for the uncertainty of their alliance positioning. When a crisis forces the ambiguity to resolve, that discount either collapses (if the country successfully navigates the crisis) or widens dramatically (if the country is caught in the wrong position, as India was). The resolution of strategic ambiguity is a binary event for EM equity investors.
