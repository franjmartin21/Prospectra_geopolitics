## Opening Question

*If every major economy simultaneously decides to rebuild domestic manufacturing capacity — and all of them announce enormous government subsidies to do it — who actually wins: the governments spending the money, the corporations receiving the incentives, or the suppliers of the inputs those factories require?*

Sit with that for a moment. The answer is not obvious, and getting it right is worth significant alpha.

---

## 1. The Reshoring Thesis in Plain Language

After three decades of offshoring driven by labor arbitrage and containerization economics, the geopolitical ruptures of the 2020s triggered a structural reversal. The logic is simple: supply chains optimized purely for cost are fragile. When China-Taiwan tensions, COVID, Red Sea disruptions, and export controls destroyed the assumption of frictionless globalization, corporations and governments concluded that **resilience is worth paying for**.

The result is a global reshoring capex cycle — the largest reallocation of industrial capital since post-WWII reconstruction. But this cycle has an unusual structure: it is **simultaneously driven by security imperatives, subsidy competition, and genuine economic logic**, and these three forces point toward different outcomes.

**The three drivers, distinguished:**

| Driver | Primary Actor | Asset Class Affected |
|--------|--------------|----------------------|
| National security / strategic autonomy | Governments (CHIPS Act, IRA, EU AI Act, Made in China 2025) | Defense contractors, semiconductor fabs, energy storage |
| Supply chain resilience | Multinationals (Apple, TSMC, Toyota) | Industrial real estate, robotics, advanced manufacturing |
| Genuine nearshoring economics | Mid-size manufacturers | EM beneficiaries (Mexico, India, Vietnam) + domestic infrastructure |

These three streams flow simultaneously but at different rates — and critically, they have different **duration** as investment theses.

---

## 2. The Historical Precedent: What Capex Cycles Actually Look Like

The reshoring thesis is real. But most investors misunderstand how **slowly** it unfolds relative to the announcement cycle.

**The pattern from prior industrial capex cycles:**

1. **Announcement phase** (Year 0–1): Political commitments, subsidy frameworks, corporate pledges. Stock prices for beneficiaries run.
2. **Groundbreaking phase** (Year 1–3): Construction begins. Delays compound. Input costs rise. Skilled labor shortages appear.
3. **Completion phase** (Year 3–6): Facilities come online. Actual productivity and economics become visible.
4. **Normalization phase** (Year 6–10): Competition compresses margins. Winners and losers separate.

**Example: US Semiconductor Revival**
The CHIPS Act passed in August 2022. By 2026, TSMC's Arizona fab (Fab 21) had reached commercial production after delays, but at costs roughly 30–50% above equivalent Taiwan production. Intel's Ohio fabs faced prolonged construction timelines. The announced capacity was real — but the timeline was measured in half-decades, not quarters.

**Investment implication:** The equity market tends to price the *announcement* of reshoring, not the *completion*. The real money is in the **enabling infrastructure** — not the headline manufacturers.

## 3. The Three Investment Layers of Reshoring

Think of reshoring capex as a pyramid:

```
           [Headline Manufacturers]
          (TSMC, Intel, auto OEMs)
         ↑ High visibility, often already priced
        
        [Enabling Ecosystem]
       (equipment, robotics, industrial RE)
      ↑ Better risk/reward, longer duration
     
     [Input Supply Chain]
    (copper, specialty chemicals, power infrastructure)
   ↑ Most underpriced, least obvious, highest conviction
```

**Layer 1 — Headline Manufacturers**
These are the semiconductor fabs, EV gigafactories, and pharmaceutical production facilities receiving government subsidies. They are well-covered, heavily subsidized, and often carry significant execution risk. The market has largely priced the tailwind. *Approach with caution.*

**Layer 2 — Enabling Ecosystem**
This is where the risk/reward improves:
- **Industrial real estate**: Demand for industrial parks, logistics hubs, and manufacturing zones near onshored facilities. Class A industrial vacancy rates in US Sun Belt and Southeast Asian industrial zones compressed sharply 2023–2026.
- **Automation and robotics**: Reshoring to high-wage markets only works if labor costs are offset by automation. The global robotics market is a direct beneficiary — FANUC, Yaskawa, ABB, Rockwell Automation. No labor arbitrage means robot arbitrage.
- **Power infrastructure**: Every new semiconductor fab, EV battery plant, or data center is an enormous electricity consumer. The power grid is the binding constraint. Transmission infrastructure, grid storage, and generation capacity are secular beneficiaries regardless of which factories are built.

**Layer 3 — Input Supply Chain**
The most systematically underpriced layer:
- **Copper**: The backbone of electrification. A semiconductor fab or EV plant requires multiples of the copper of a comparable conventional industrial facility. Long-cycle supply deficits are structural.
- **Specialty gases and chemicals**: Semiconductor fabrication requires ultra-pure process gases (argon, neon, nitrogen, HF). Supply concentration in China and Russia created acute vulnerabilities; Western domestic supply buildup is capital-intensive.
- **Skilled trades and engineering**: The human capital constraint is real. Welders, electricians, and industrial engineers are scarce — a constraint that plays out in construction cost inflation, not just completion delays.

## 4. The Subsidy Competition Dynamic: A Race to the Bottom?

A critical risk to the reshoring thesis is the **subsidy competition trap**.

When multiple governments simultaneously subsidize the same industries — semiconductors, batteries, solar — the economic logic can break down:

1. Global overcapacity emerges (see: solar panels 2010–2015, where Chinese subsidies + Western tariffs produced a decade-long commodity price collapse)
2. The companies receiving subsidies become dependent on them — structurally uncompetitive without state support
3. When political winds shift, the capex cycle reverses and stranded assets appear

**The test:** Is the reshored capacity genuinely competitive at market prices, or does it require perpetual government support?

For semiconductors: advanced logic (sub-3nm) has genuine security value and limited substitutability — the subsidy case is strong. For solar: capacity is now commoditized, and overcapacity risks are real. For EVs: the outcome depends heavily on whether battery cost curves continue improving and whether consumer demand holds.

**Investment implication:** Not all reshoring is equal. Thesis-grade positions require **identifying which sectors have genuine long-term competitive logic, not just political momentum.**

## 5. The Geographic Winners: Where Does Reshored Capacity Go?

Not all reshoring goes "home." Most of it goes *closer*, not fully domestic. The geography matters enormously for investment positioning.

**Tier 1 — Full Friend-shoring (Domestic Rebuild)**
- United States: Semiconductors (CHIPS Act), defense production, some pharmaceutical API
- European Union: Battery gigafactories, green hydrogen, critical minerals processing
- Japan: Semiconductor equipment, some advanced chip packaging

**Tier 2 — Nearshoring to Allies (the largest flow by volume)**
- Mexico: Largest beneficiary of US nearshoring — automotive, aerospace, electronics assembly. Borderlands industrial corridor is a multi-decade structural thesis.
- Poland/Czech Republic: EU manufacturing hub — auto components, machinery, some electronics.
- India: Electronics assembly, pharmaceutical generics, data center build-out. Longer timeline than Mexico but larger total addressable market.

**Tier 3 — China+1 Redistribution (covered in Lesson 454)**
- Vietnam, Thailand, Malaysia: Electronics assembly, apparel, consumer goods migrating out of China.
- Indonesia: Resource processing, EV battery supply chain.

**The overlooked play:** The countries that *process the inputs* for reshored manufacturing — especially critical minerals processing. Africa (DRC cobalt, Zambia copper) and Latin America (Chile, Argentina lithium) sit at the foundation of the entire reshoring capex pyramid. The geopolitics of securing that supply is the upstream constraint on the entire thesis.

## 6. The Inflation Paradox

Here is the macroeconomic wrinkle that most reshoring bulls underappreciate:

**Reshoring is structurally inflationary for the economies doing the reshoring.**

The logic is direct:
- High-wage domestic labor replaces low-wage offshore labor
- Construction of new facilities at scale competes for the same steel, copper, cement, and skilled trades
- Government deficits financing subsidies expand money supply
- Result: **goods inflation becomes sticky** in ways that challenge the post-2022 central bank narrative of transient supply shocks

This connects directly to the fiscal dominance lessons (447–452): the reshoring capex cycle is partly a *consequence* of the geopolitical fragmentation that created fiscal dominance pressures. The two themes are the same coin: reshoring drives spending that drives deficits that drives inflation that constrains monetary policy.

**Investment implication:** Real assets, inflation-linked securities, and commodities are beneficiaries of both themes simultaneously — this is not a coincidence. It is structural.

## 7. Investment Implications — The CEO's Directional View

**Highest conviction, 3–7 year horizon:**

| Position | Thesis | Key Risk |
|----------|---------|----------|
| **Long copper** (physical or miners) | Every factory requires electrification infrastructure; supply deficit is structural and long-cycle | Demand destruction in China construction offsets EM build |
| **Long industrial automation** (robotics, factory software) | Reshoring to high-wage markets requires automation to be economical; no labor arbitrage = robot arbitrage | Technology substitution risk (AI robotics disrupting incumbents) |
| **Long US/EU power infrastructure** (transmission, grid storage) | The electricity demand from reshored manufacturing + AI data centers is the binding constraint | Regulatory/permitting delays extend timelines indefinitely |
| **Long Mexico industrial real estate / nearshoring plays** | Multi-decade structural beneficiary of US supply chain reorientation, with USMCA anchor | Political risk (AMLO 2.0 style resource nationalism; security environment) |
| **Cautious on headline manufacturers** receiving subsidies | Already priced; subsidy dependency risk; execution risk on greenfield fabs | |
| **Cautious on solar/battery manufacturers** | Overcapacity risk from subsidy competition; commoditization ongoing | |

**The signal to watch:** Real US manufacturing construction spending. This series (from the Census Bureau) leads actual capacity online by 2–3 years and gives the clearest early signal of capex cycle momentum or reversal.

## Market Connection

The reshoring capex cycle is **the most durable multi-year investment theme running today.** It is structural (driven by security imperatives, not just economics), it is backed by government spending that is politically difficult to reverse, and it has compounding demand implications for inputs and enabling infrastructure. The key discipline is **selecting the right layer** of exposure — enabling infrastructure and input commodities over headline manufacturers — and maintaining **6–18 month minimum horizons** to avoid being shaken out by construction delays and quarterly earnings noise.
