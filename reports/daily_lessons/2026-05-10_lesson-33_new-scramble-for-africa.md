## OPENING QUESTION

If you had to identify the single geographic theater that will most determine the global balance of power over the next 30 years, where would you look?

Not Ukraine. Not Taiwan. Not the South China Sea.

The answer that serious strategists are converging on: Sub-Saharan Africa.

It holds 60% of the world's uncultivated arable land. The largest concentration of critical mineral reserves on the planet. 40% of global working-age population by 2100. And it is currently the most actively contested geopolitical battleground between the major powers — with China, the US, Russia, and the Gulf states all moving simultaneously.

Most investors treat Africa as a backwater of political noise and idiosyncratic risk. That is a framework error. Here is how to see it clearly — and what it means for the portfolio.

## 1. HISTORICAL GROUNDING: THE FIRST SCRAMBLE

The Berlin Conference of 1884–1885 is one of the most consequential events in modern history that almost no one studies. European powers — Germany, Britain, France, Belgium, Portugal, and others — sat around a table and drew lines across a continent. No African representative was present. By 1914, 90% of Africa was under European colonial control.

The scramble was driven by three forces:
1. **Resources** — rubber, ivory, gold, diamonds, agricultural land
2. **Strategic geography** — control of maritime trade routes, coaling stations, interior access
3. **Prestige** — great power status required a colonial portfolio

The formal colonial era ended between the 1950s and 1970s. But the economic structures — extraction-oriented, export-dependent, debt-financed — largely persisted. And now, 140 years after Berlin, a new scramble is underway. The players are different. The logic is identical.

## 2. THE FOUR PLAYERS — AND THEIR PLAYBOOKS

### China — The Infrastructure Lender
China's Belt & Road Initiative (BRI) has deployed over $150 billion in loans to African nations, with 52 countries signed up. The playbook:

- **Infrastructure loans** at commercial rates, often with natural resource collateral
- **Chinese construction companies and labor** build the projects, limiting local employment multiplier
- **Port-to-mine corridors**: railways, roads, and ports that connect interior extraction zones to export terminals

Key projects: the Standard Gauge Railway in Kenya and Ethiopia, Mombasa port expansion, DRC mining joint ventures, Zambia copper debt financing. The strategic masterpiece: China now **processes approximately 70% of the world's cobalt**, while the DRC mines roughly 70% of global cobalt supply. China doesn't need to own the mine — it owns the refinery.

### United States — The Reluctant Competitor
The US woke up late. The G7's Partnership for Global Infrastructure and Investment (PGII) committed $600 billion globally, with major Africa focus. AFRICOM maintains presence across 29 bases and outposts. The flagship economic project: the **Lobito Corridor** — a railway connecting Zambia and DRC copper/cobalt mines to the Angolan port of Lobito, directly competing with Chinese-built export routes.

The problem: the US keeps losing basing rights. Mali, Niger, Burkina Faso, and Chad — key Sahel partners hosting US counterterrorism infrastructure — expelled American forces as Russian-aligned military governments took power between 2021 and 2024. The US strategy assumes stable, pro-Western governments. That assumption is failing in real time.

### Russia — The Cheap Security Provider
After the Wagner Group's internal crisis in 2023, its African operations were absorbed into **Africa Corps** (Russian Ministry of Defense controlled). The model is elegant in its simplicity:

- **Security services** provided to coup-installed military governments (counter-insurgency, presidential guard, intelligence)
- **In exchange for**: mining licenses (gold in Mali and Sudan, diamonds in CAR), petroleum rights (Libya, Mozambique), and political alignment (anti-Western votes at the UN, expulsion of French/US forces)
- **Cost to Russia**: relatively low — a few thousand fighters, some hardware, and a sustained information warfare operation feeding anti-French, anti-colonial sentiment

Africa Corps is currently embedded in Mali, Burkina Faso, Niger, Sudan, CAR, Libya, and Mozambique. The Russian bet is simple: instability is cheap to create and expensive for others to contain. And they are right.

### Gulf States — The Quiet Accumulation
UAE, Saudi Arabia, and Qatar are less visible but strategically significant:
- **DP World** (Dubai) controls major ports across East and West Africa — Berbera, Dakar, Maputo, and others
- **Sovereign wealth funds** are acquiring African agricultural land (food security hedge against MENA import dependency)
- **Qatar** is financing Islamic infrastructure in the Sahel, building soft power
- **Turkey** has a military base in Somalia, sells Bayraktar drones to African governments, and is expanding trade relationships across the continent

This is not abstract great power posturing. There are specific, quantifiable strategic assets driving the competition:

| Cobalt | DRC | ~70% | EV batteries, aerospace |
| Copper | DRC, Zambia | ~15% | Energy transition infrastructure |

The critical insight: **Africa is the world's primary reserve of the materials required for the energy transition.** Without African cobalt, copper, lithium, and PGMs, there is no EV revolution, no clean energy infrastructure buildout, no semiconductor supply chain independence. Every major power that has done the math on this is moving.

## 4. THE COUP WAVE — A GEOPOLITICAL REALIGNMENT IN REAL TIME

Between 2021 and 2024, the Sahel experienced an unprecedented wave of military takeovers:

- **Mali** (August 2021): French forces expelled, Wagner invited in
- **Burkina Faso** (September 2022): France expelled, Russian alignment
- **Niger** (July 2023): US and French bases effectively shuttered
- **Gabon** (August 2023): Bongo dynasty ended in a coup
- **Senegal** (2024): Democratic reset, but sharply anti-French new government

The pattern is not coincidence. It is driven by three overlapping forces:
1. **Genuine popular resentment** of France's post-colonial economic grip (the CFA franc, French military presence, French corporate extraction)
2. **Russian information warfare**: coordinated disinformation campaigns portraying France as the colonial oppressor and Russia as the liberator — amplified on social media across the Sahel
3. **Failure of French/US counterterrorism strategy**: 10+ years of operations in Mali and Burkina Faso produced no security improvement, giving coup leaders a credible grievance

**France has effectively lost the Sahel.** This is the most significant geopolitical defeat for a Western power in Africa since the Cold War.

## 5. THE CFA FRANC — A COLONIAL RELIC UNDER ATTACK

Fourteen African nations use the CFA franc — a currency **pegged to the Euro and backed by the French Treasury**, requiring member nations to deposit 50% of their foreign exchange reserves with the Banque de France. This arrangement, a direct inheritance of French colonial finance, is now under active political attack from coup governments in Mali, Niger, and Burkina Faso, who have signaled intent to abandon it.

If the CFA peg breaks:
- **Exchange rate risk** for all businesses and investors with exposure to CFA-zone countries (Côte d'Ivoire, Senegal, Cameroon, and others would face currency chaos)
- **Inflation spike** in departing countries as the Euro anchor is removed
- **Opportunity for alternative settlement mechanisms**: Chinese yuan, Gulf currencies, or even a BRICS settlement unit — deepening the de-dollarization/de-euroization dynamic
- **Contagion risk** to stable CFA economies (Côte d'Ivoire is a major cocoa and coffee producer)

This is the same structural dynamic as BRICS de-dollarization (Lesson 29), but playing out at the regional level with a colonial-era monetary institution as the target.

## 6. INVESTMENT IMPLICATIONS

### A. Critical Minerals — Structural Long (6–18 month+ horizon)
The great power competition over African resources structurally **increases supply disruption risk** through three channels:
- **Resource nationalism**: coup governments renegotiating Chinese and Western mining deals
- **Political instability**: conflict in mining zones (eastern DRC is chronically contested)
- **Export route vulnerability**: rail and port infrastructure can be weaponized or physically threatened

This means higher structural commodity prices for cobalt, copper, and PGMs are justified. The question is not whether — it is when and by how much.

Key names to track:
- **Glencore (GLEN.L)**: dominant DRC cobalt exposure
- **Ivanhoe Mines (IVN.TO)**: Kamoa-Kakula copper complex, DRC — one of the world's largest copper discoveries
- **Anglo American Platinum / Amplats (AMS.J)**: South Africa PGMs
- **Sibanye-Stillwater (SBGL)**: PGMs, South Africa
- **First Quantum Minerals (FM.TO)**: Zambia copper — but note Zambia royalty dispute risk

**Watch list, not buy list today.** The thesis is structural; the entry requires monitoring political risk events as signals.

### B. African Sovereign Debt — Structural Caution
African eurobonds have been a serial underperformer: Zambia defaulted (2020), Ghana restructured (2023), Kenya under IMF pressure, Ethiopia in default. The combination of dollar-denominated debt + restrictive US monetary policy + commodity export price volatility = EM sovereign stress cycle.

Adding geopolitical instability (coup risk, CFA fracture) makes African sovereign exposure a **structural underweight** for the portfolio. Spreads may compress periodically, but the structural risk is not priced for the right reasons.

### C. Defense and Security — Indirect Long
Instability in resource-rich Africa benefits:
- **Drone manufacturers** with African government sales (Turkish Bayraktar; US and European equivalents)
- **Private security firms** (publicly traded equivalents: G4S/Allied Universal, though mostly private)
- **Satellite surveillance**: commercial satellite imagery is increasingly used by mining companies and governments for security monitoring

### D. Agricultural Commodities — Watch
Sahel instability affects millet, sorghum, sesame production. Gulf land acquisitions in Ethiopia and Sudan represent sovereign food security hedges — the same commodity exposure logic as our energy thesis but for agricultural land.

### E. Infrastructure — Selective and Long-Horizon
The Lobito Corridor, if built to spec, would reshape the economics of DRC/Zambia copper export by reducing transport costs significantly. This is a multi-year infrastructure investment thesis. Watch for public-private partnership announcements — this could create private equity and infrastructure fund plays.

## 7. SIGNAL VS. NOISE

**Signal:**
- Structural competition for African critical minerals is intensifying, not stabilizing
- Supply disruption risk for cobalt, copper, and PGMs is a structural feature, not a cyclical event
- African sovereign debt distress has not fully priced in the geopolitical realignment
- The Sahel is becoming a Russia-China aligned zone — this is a decade-long structural shift, not a recoverable blip

**Noise:**
- Month-to-month coup politics (which general is in power in Niamey this week)
- Individual China loan defaults (headline risk, not structural — China renegotiates, rarely forces asset seizures)
- Western development finance announcements (PGII, EU Global Gateway) — the gap between commitment and disbursement is massive; don't trade the press release

China's loans to Africa are frequently described as "debt trap diplomacy" — the accusation being that China intentionally lends beyond repayment capacity in order to seize strategic assets (the Hambantota port in Sri Lanka is the canonical example). But several researchers have pushed back: most Chinese loans are renegotiated, not asset-seized, and the terms are often more flexible than IMF austerity programs.
