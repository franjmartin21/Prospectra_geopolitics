## I. The Problem That Made Nuclear Suddenly Relevant Again

### The AI Electricity Demand Shock

The energy transition narrative of 2015–2022 was built on one assumption: electricity demand growth in developed economies would be *flat to modest* — maybe 1–2% per year. This made the transition to intermittent renewables (solar, wind) mathematically tractable. You could build enough storage and grid flexibility to cover the gaps.

That assumption is wrong. **AI infrastructure has broken the demand model.**

| Data Point | Scale |
|---|---|
| ChatGPT query energy use | ~10× a Google search |
| A large AI training run (GPT-4 class) | ~50 GWh — enough to power a small city for a year |
| US data center electricity demand (2023) | ~130 TWh/year (~3.3% of US consumption) |
| Projected US data center demand (2030) | 300–500 TWh/year — a 2–4× increase in 7 years |
| Microsoft's electricity procurement target | 100% carbon-free, 24/7 matched (not just annual offset) |

**The critical phrase: "24/7 carbon-free energy."** This is the constraint that breaks renewables alone. Google, Microsoft, and Amazon have committed to matching every hour of electricity consumption with carbon-free generation — not just buying annual renewable energy credits. Solar doesn't generate at night. Wind doesn't blow on calm days. Neither is dispatchable on demand.

This creates a specific demand signal: **always-on, baseload, carbon-free power.** There is exactly one technology that reliably delivers all three attributes: nuclear.

### The Grid Reliability Premium

Beyond hyperscalers, the broader grid is becoming less reliable as coal and gas plants retire faster than firm replacements come online. The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) documented that the US is facing a capacity crunch in key regions by 2028–2030. Data centers require 99.999% uptime ("five nines") — something that utility grids in stressed conditions cannot guarantee without firm generation backstop.

Nuclear provides the firm capacity that renewables cannot. This is not an ideological argument — it's a physics constraint.

## II. The Old Nuclear Problem Has Not Gone Away

Before getting too bullish, a discipline check: **nuclear's fundamental economics remain deeply challenged.**

### The Cost Disease

Nuclear plants in the Western world suffer from a cost escalation pathology that has no parallel in other industries. Consider:

| Project | Country | Announced Cost | Final/Current Cost | Delay |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Vogtle Units 3&4 | USA | $14bn (2008) | $36bn (2023) | 7 years |
| Hinkley Point C | UK | £18bn (2016) | £46bn (2024) | 5+ years |
| Flamanville 3 | France | €3.3bn (2007) | €13.7bn (2023) | 12 years |
| Olkiluoto 3 | Finland | €3bn (2005) | €11bn (2023) | 14 years |

Every large-scale Western nuclear build in the past 20 years has been delivered massively over budget and late. This is not a regulatory accident — it reflects the **loss of institutional knowledge** after two decades of no construction, combined with regulatory environments that allow late design changes and lack serial production learning curves.

**China and South Korea are the exceptions:** KEPCO (South Korea) delivered the UAE's Barakah plant (four reactors, 5.6 GW) on budget and close to schedule. China is building 20+ reactors on standard designs with serial production economics. The Western nuclear industry cannot replicate this — its supply chain is atrophied.

**Implication:** Large-scale conventional nuclear builds in the US and Europe are *not* the investable opportunity unless they're under state sponsorship with political risk contained. The opportunity is smaller, faster, and different.

## III. The SMR Thesis: Promise, Reality, and Hype-Discount

Small Modular Reactors (SMRs) are the proposed solution to the large-nuclear cost problem. The thesis:

1. **Smaller size** (10–300 MWe vs. 1,000–1,600 MWe for conventional) → lower upfront capital, modular construction
2. **Factory manufacturing** → serial production learning curves drive cost down (like aircraft manufacturing, not bespoke construction)
3. **Simplified design** → passive safety systems, fewer operators, potentially lower regulatory burden
4. **Flexibility** → can be sited near industrial users, data centers, military bases

### The SMR Landscape (2026)

| Developer | Technology | Status | Target Market |
|---|---|---|---|
| **NuScale** | Light Water SMR (77 MWe/module) | First project cancelled (Idaho); regulatory approval complete | US utilities, export |
| **X-Energy** | High-temperature gas reactor | Amazon PPA signed; construction 2025+ | Data centers, industrial heat |
| **Kairos Power** | Molten salt/pebble bed | Google PPA (500 MW by 2035) | Data centers |
| **TerraPower** | Natrium (sodium-cooled fast reactor) | First plant under construction in Wyoming; Bill Gates-backed | Grid baseload |
| **Rolls-Royce SMR** | Light Water SMR (470 MWe) | UK government contract; export orders | Utilities, export markets |
| **GE-Hitachi BWRX-300** | Boiling water SMR | Orders from Canada, Poland, Estonia | Grid baseload |

**The honest assessment:** SMRs are real — the technology works and regulatory approvals are advancing. But every SMR project is still expensive relative to promised costs, and the "factory manufacturing" learning curve only kicks in at scale. The first 10 plants in any design will cost significantly more than the next 100. **Who bears the cost of those first 10?**

In practice: governments bear first-mover cost risk (UK, Canada, US, Poland), and commercial buyers (hyperscalers) sign long-term PPAs at above-market rates that effectively subsidize the learning curve. This is the model that makes SMRs possible — but it means SMR returns depend heavily on policy continuity and corporate off-take commitments lasting 15–20 years.

### The NuScale Warning

NuScale won the first NRC approval for an SMR design in US history (2022), then watched its flagship project collapse in 2023 when construction cost estimates rose from $58/MWh to $89/MWh, making the economics non-competitive with other options available to the Idaho co-op customers. This is the archetypal risk: regulatory approval is a necessary but not sufficient condition for commercial viability.

## IV. Historical Grounding: The French Model and What It Took

France built 56 nuclear reactors between 1974 and 1999 — the largest sustained nuclear build program outside the Soviet Union. By 1990, France got 75% of its electricity from nuclear at the lowest electricity cost in Europe.

**How they did it:**
1. **Standardization:** EDF (state utility) built only two reactor designs (PWR 900 MW and 1,300 MW) — no custom engineering per site
2. **State ownership:** No capital market discipline on project risk; the French state absorbed cost overruns
3. **Serial production:** Workforce built 56 plants; by plant 20, construction times and costs were falling
4. **Political insulation:** No NIMBY veto; siting decisions made centrally

**The lesson:** Large-scale nuclear success requires either a) an authoritarian political system that can override local opposition (China), b) a developmental state model with total state ownership and no capital market discipline (France 1974–1999), or c) a new technology paradigm (SMRs) that changes the cost structure and siting constraints.

The West is pursuing option (c). The question is whether hyperscaler demand provides enough economic gravity to pull that option across the commercialization threshold before the next political cycle turns.

### The CEO Portfolio Note

The nuclear investment thesis in 2026 has **three distinct timeframes** that require different instruments:

1. **Near-term (1–3 years):** Uranium spot price leverage (Cameco, Sprott Physical Uranium Trust) + nuclear utility pricing power (Constellation, CEZ). This is the clearest risk/reward — demand is real, supply is constrained, Russian enrichment decoupling is multi-year.

2. **Medium-term (3–7 years):** SMR construction supply chain (BWX Technologies, Centrus, grid infrastructure). SMR first plants will be operational by 2028–2032; supply chain benefits accrue before first power.

3. **Long-term (7–15 years):** SMR developers themselves (NuScale, X-Energy if they go public, TerraPower). This is optionality on the commercialization thesis; size accordingly (small, high-risk allocation).

**What would make this wrong:** A rapid breakthrough in long-duration battery storage (e.g., iron-air or sodium-ion at grid scale costs below $50/MWh) that allows renewables to deliver 24/7 firm power without nuclear. This is the key bear case to monitor — it's the technology risk that doesn't appear in uranium supply/demand models.

## VI. The Geopolitical Layer: Who Controls the Nuclear Renaissance?

This is the dimension most energy investors miss.

**China's nuclear buildout is a geopolitical competition, not just an energy policy:**
- China has 55 operational reactors and 25 under construction — the world's most aggressive buildout
- CNNC and CGN (Chinese state nuclear companies) are pursuing reactor exports to BRI countries: Pakistan, Argentina, Romania (aborted), Bangladesh, Kenya
- Chinese-designed reactors create 60-year dependencies: fuel supply, maintenance contracts, fuel cycle services
- The US attempted to block Chinese nuclear deals in Eastern Europe (Romania's Cernavoda plant redirected to US-led consortium)

**The Export Competition:**
South Korea (KEPCO), France (EDF/Framatome), Russia (Rosatom), and China are all competing for reactor export orders in Africa, Southeast Asia, and Eastern Europe. The winner of each contract wins a 60-year supply chain relationship. This is infrastructure imperialism at the nuclear level — and the US has been losing ground because it lacks a competitive state-backed commercial nuclear offering.

**ADVANCE Act (2024):** US legislation to streamline Nuclear Regulatory Commission licensing and explicitly enable American nuclear exports. Part of a broader US strategy to keep allied countries from defaulting to Russian or Chinese nuclear technology for their first reactor builds.
