## Core Concept: The Analytical Hiring Problem

### Why Standard Hiring Fails for Research Roles

In most industries, you hire for demonstrated execution: "Here are your past outputs; they look like the outputs we need." In research, the outputs that matter are *future calls* on *events that haven't happened yet*. Past credentials — PhD, Goldman tenure, CFA — weakly predict whether someone will generate alpha in your specific analytical context.

The empirical literature on forecasting (Tetlock, Kahneman, Meehl) is unambiguous: **credentials predict confidence, not accuracy.** Academic economists with strong credentials routinely underperform simple quantitative models. Former intelligence analysts frequently fall prey to confirmation bias and anchoring to their agency's house view. The credential signals quality of past work in a defined institutional context — not the specific skill you need.

The specific skill you need is what Philip Tetlock calls **"Superforecaster" thinking**: calibrated probabilistic reasoning, active updating on new information, comfort with uncertainty, and the ability to aggregate evidence across domains that specialists keep siloed.

**The implication:** You are not hiring credentials. You are hiring a cognitive style.

### The Four Cognitive Markers You're Hiring For

**1. Fox > Hedgehog**
Isaiah Berlin's distinction: hedgehogs know one big thing and apply it everywhere; foxes know many small things and synthesize them. Geopolitical investing requires fox thinking — the ability to hold Iran's nuclear program, Japan's yield curve, and Congo's cobalt politics simultaneously and see how they interact. Hedgehogs produce coherent narratives that miss inflection points. Foxes produce messier but more accurate forecasts.

**Screening signal:** Ask candidates to explain how an event in their area of expertise affects something outside it. A hedgehog cannot do this naturally. A fox lights up.

**2. Calibration Over Conviction**
You want analysts who say "I'm 65% confident" and mean it — and who track whether their 65% calls hit 65% of the time. This is rarer than it sounds. Most analytical cultures reward confident assertions that sound persuasive, not calibrated probability estimates that track. The institutional incentive in most banks and think tanks is to be interestingly wrong rather than boringly right.

**Screening signal:** Ask candidates about a call they made that was wrong. How do they explain it? A calibrated thinker says "I underweighted X factor" or "my base rate on Y type of event was too high." An overconfident thinker says "the data was bad" or "it was unpredictable" — externalizing the error rather than owning the miss.

**3. Evidence Updating Without Flip-Flopping**
Good analysts change their minds when new evidence warrants it. They do not change their minds because the market moved against them (that is capitulating to price, not updating on evidence). This distinction is subtle but critical: Bayesian updating based on evidence is a virtue; opinion drift based on market feedback is a vice that destroys your edge.

**Screening signal:** Present a scenario where the market moved sharply against a position. Ask the candidate how they would decide whether to hold or reverse. Listen for the distinction between "the market disagrees with me" (irrelevant) and "here is new evidence that changes my probability estimate" (actionable).

**4. Intellectual Honesty Over Institutional Loyalty**
The best research organizations protect analysts from pressure to converge to the house view or the client's preferred view. But analysts also must be selected for the willingness to hold a contrarian position under social pressure. This is a personality trait as much as an intellectual one.

**Screening signal:** Ask about a time they disagreed with their organization's consensus view. Did they hold the dissenting view? Did they raise it? What happened? You want someone who disagreed *and told their boss.* The analyst who quietly harbored a contrarian view but wrote the consensus call is a high-credential low-value hire.

## The Hiring Process: Three Stages

### Stage 1: The Research Brief Test (Filter)

Do not start with interviews. Give every finalist candidate a structured research brief test:

**Brief format:**
- Provide 48–72 hours
- Give a specific geopolitical scenario with curated data: two or three public sources (GDELT output, a news feed excerpt, a market data snapshot)
- Ask for: (1) a geopolitical risk assessment with probability ranges, (2) the top investment implication with direction and timeframe, (3) a falsifiability statement — what evidence would make them revise the call

**What you're assessing:**
- Does the analysis go beyond summarizing the data? (Synthesis > aggregation)
- Are probability estimates present and specific? ("There is a meaningful risk" = fail; "I assign 40% probability to X within 90 days" = pass)
- Is the investment implication actionable and directional, or vague? ("Bearish for EM" = fail; "Long Brazilian real against Turkish lira on a 6-month horizon because..." = pass)
- Is the falsifiability statement genuine? ("If I'm wrong about everything" = fail; "If IAEA confirms no enrichment activity above 20% in the next 30 days, I would revise my 60% probability down to 25%" = pass)

Reject anyone who cannot produce a falsifiable, directional, calibrated output. You can teach domain knowledge. You cannot teach this cognitive style.

### Stage 2: The Live Analytical Conversation (Assessment)

With finalists who pass Stage 1, conduct a 90-minute structured analytical session:

**Part A (30 min):** Walk through their brief test together. Ask probing questions: "Why 40% and not 30%? What would move it to 60%? Which assumption in your model is most fragile?" You are assessing how they think in real time, not just whether their written output was polished.

**Part B (30 min):** Present a scenario *outside* their submitted topic. If they wrote on Iran, now ask about South Korea. If they wrote on oil, now ask about the Brazilian election. You are testing the fox vs. hedgehog dimension and how they reason without domain comfort.

**Part C (30 min):** "Here is a call Prospectra made three months ago. Here is how it played out. What would you have done differently?" Assess: Do they see the error clearly? Do they understand the falsifiability condition we set? Do they improve on the original reasoning or simply backward-infer from the outcome?

### Stage 3: The Values Check (Decision Gate)

Final-stage conversation, CEO to candidate. Not about analytical skill — Stage 1 and 2 settled that. This is about:

**Intellectual honesty under pressure:** "Describe a time you had to publish or deliver a view you disagreed with. What did you do?" You want the analyst who pushed back or disclosed the dissent — not the one who silently complied.

**Ego calibration:** Research roles attract high-ego candidates. High ego correlates with overconfidence, poor updating, and difficulty taking the "challenger role" to the house view. Screen for the candidate who is proud of being wrong in the right way — who can say "I got that call wrong, and here is exactly why" with specificity and no defensiveness.

**Commitment to the methodology:** "Here is the GRI methodology. What's wrong with it?" The candidate who has no answer is not intellectually engaged. The candidate who gives a thoughtful critique and then says "but here is how I'd use it" is the hire.

## Investment Implication: Research Quality Compounds Like Capital

There is a direct analogy between portfolio management and research team construction:

- **Diversification by analytical style** beats concentration on one star: three analysts with distinct cognitive approaches (one macro-economist, one political scientist, one intelligence community veteran) catch more inflection points than three people with the same framework.
- **Track record tracking creates accountability and compounds improvement**: force analysts to log every call with probabilities. Review quarterly. The act of tracking changes behavior — calibrated analysts become more calibrated; overconfident ones either adjust or self-select out.
- **The cost of a bad hire in research is not just salary**: a poorly calibrated analyst generating wrong signals at scale — with your brand attached — is a reputational liability that can undo client relationships that took years to build. The cost of a wrong hire is asymmetric and long-tailed.

**The asymmetric implication for Prospectra:** Moving too fast to hire (before the process above is in place) compounds negatively. Moving too slowly (staying founder-dependent past the 12-month post-Series A window) caps growth. The optimal timing: hire the first Tier 2 analyst within 90 days of close, using the full process. That analyst's first six months is a trial period with explicit performance criteria tied to the GRI output.

## Concrete Example: What a Bad Hire Looks Like

Gavekal Research made a celebrated mishire in its early scaling phase: an analyst with impeccable academic credentials in Chinese political economy who consistently produced consensus-aligned research that could have been written by any Bloomberg Economics contributor. He was never wrong enough to fire but never right enough to be cited. He occupied a research slot for three years that could have been a differentiating voice. The opportunity cost — not the salary — was the real loss.

The tell in the hiring process: he had no falsifiable statements in his work. Every call was framed as "risk remains elevated" or "uncertainty persists" — language designed to be unfalsifiable and therefore never wrong. Prospectra's brief test would have caught this immediately.

## Databricks Angle

**Track Record Infrastructure for Analysts:**

Build a shared analyst performance table in Databricks as part of the post-Series A build:

```sql
-- Analyst call log schema
CREATE TABLE prospectra.analyst_performance.calls (
    call_id         STRING,
    analyst_id      STRING,
    call_date       DATE,
    thesis          STRING,
    asset_class     STRING,
    direction       STRING,      -- LONG / SHORT / NEUTRAL
    probability     FLOAT,       -- stated at time of call
    horizon_days    INT,
    falsifiability  STRING,
    outcome_date    DATE,
    outcome         STRING,      -- CORRECT / INCORRECT / INCOMPLETE
    outcome_notes   STRING,
    resolution_price FLOAT
);

-- Calibration score: did 65% calls hit 65% of the time?
SELECT 
    analyst_id,
    ROUND(probability * 20) / 20 AS prob_bucket,  -- bucket by 5%
    COUNT(*) AS n_calls,
    AVG(CASE WHEN outcome = 'CORRECT' THEN 1.0 ELSE 0.0 END) AS hit_rate
FROM prospectra.analyst_performance.calls
WHERE outcome != 'INCOMPLETE'
GROUP BY analyst_id, prob_bucket
ORDER BY analyst_id, prob_bucket;
```

This schema lets you run calibration curves on every analyst quarterly. It makes the "calibrated probabilistic reasoning" requirement measurable, not just a hiring preference. It also creates the audit trail needed to demonstrate analytical quality to institutional clients who will ask for exactly this when they do due diligence.

**Pipeline idea:** Feed analyst call log to the Signal Performance Review dashboard (Lesson 303), creating a unified track record view across CEO calls and analyst calls. This becomes a competitive asset in institutional sales.
