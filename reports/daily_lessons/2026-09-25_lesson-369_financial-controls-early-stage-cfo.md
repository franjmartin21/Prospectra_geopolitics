## Opening Question

*You've closed a seed round. $2.5M in the bank. You have a founder who used to track burn on a spreadsheet, a bookkeeper who comes in once a week, and a Stripe account connected to QuickBooks. Your first board meeting is in 6 weeks.*

**What does your financial reporting architecture look like — and what does it tell your investors about you?**

This is not a trivial question. The founders who answer it badly are not stupid — they are founders who made the reasonable mistake of treating financial controls as a compliance function rather than an operating function. Financial discipline is not primarily about satisfying your board. It is about understanding your business in real time, making resource allocation decisions with actual data, and building the institutional credibility that attracts Series A investors before you ever pitch them.

Most early-stage founders underinvest in financial infrastructure until a crisis forces their hand: a quarter where burn was 40% higher than they thought, an audit finding that delays a round, a key hire who asks to see cap table documents that turn out to be wrong. The founders who avoid these crises are not luckier — they built financial controls deliberately, early, as a competitive advantage.

This lesson is that architecture.

## Part I — What Financial Controls Actually Are

The phrase "financial controls" sounds like bureaucracy. Founders who haven't worked in finance often imagine it as the thing that slows down purchase orders and requires three approvals for a $200 software subscription. That is not financial controls — that is financial dysfunction masquerading as rigor.

Real financial controls are a system with four components:

**1. Accurate, timely reporting.** You know, within 5 business days of month-end, what you spent, what you earned, and what you have left. Not approximately. Not "I think it's around there." Precisely, by category, reconciled to the bank.

**2. Budget vs. actuals tracking.** You have an operating plan with monthly targets. You compare actuals to that plan every month. You understand the variance — not just that you're $30K over budget, but *why* and *whether* that variance is a problem or a signal.

**3. Cash visibility.** You know your runway at any given moment within a two-week precision window. You have a 13-week cash forecast that you update weekly. You see problems before they become crises.

**4. Authorization controls.** There is a clear policy for who can commit company funds, at what amounts, through what process. This is not about distrust — it is about preventing errors and establishing accountability before you're large enough that errors are catastrophic.

None of this requires a CFO. None of it requires a finance department. At Prospectra's stage, all four can be implemented with:
- A competent bookkeeper (12-15 hours/month, outsourced)
- QuickBooks or Xero (correctly configured from day one)
- A monthly reporting template in Google Sheets or Notion
- A simple authorization policy written in one page

The founders who don't do this are not saving time. They are deferring the cost and paying interest on it — in the form of board credibility lost, decisions made on bad data, and financing delays.

## Part II — The CFO Question

When do you hire a CFO? The most common answer in startup circles is "Series B" — which is approximately right and approximately useless, because the problem you need solved doesn't wait for Series B.

Here is the actual decision framework:

### What a CFO Does (That Others Can't)

A CFO at an early-stage company is doing four things that are genuinely distinct from bookkeeping and accounting:

1. **Financial strategy.** How do you structure the business to maximize unit economics, manage burn against milestones, and position for the next raise? This requires someone who understands capital markets, not just accounting.

2. **Investor relations.** Managing the board's financial expectations, preparing board packages, navigating covenant conversations, and representing the financial story to future investors. This is a communication and relationship function as much as a finance function.

3. **Financial architecture.** Making the strategic decisions about entity structure, tax strategy, revenue recognition, and equity accounting that are extremely expensive to undo once made incorrectly.

4. **Risk management.** Identifying where the business is exposed — concentration risk, contractual risk, covenant triggers — and building mitigation into the operating model.

A bookkeeper does none of this. A controller does some of it. A CFO does all of it.

### The Timing Matrix

| Stage | What You Need | Who Provides It |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-seed / seed | Accurate books, monthly close, basic reporting | Outsourced bookkeeper + founder |
| Late seed / pre-A | More sophisticated reporting, board prep, cap table maintenance | Part-time fractional CFO (10-20 hrs/month) |
| Series A | Strategic finance, investor relations, FP&A | Full-time VP Finance or CFO |
| Series B+ | Full finance function | CFO + team |

For Prospectra: you are currently in "late seed / pre-A" territory. The right move is not to hire a full-time CFO — the ROI is not there yet. The right move is to engage a **fractional CFO** for the specific problem you're trying to solve: board prep, fundraising narrative, and financial architecture decisions that need to be made correctly.

A fractional CFO engagement typically runs $3,000–$8,000/month for 15-20 hours. For a company with $2-5M in the bank, this is not a cost to minimize — it is one of the highest-return professional services investments available.

### What "Fractional" Actually Means in Practice

A fractional CFO is not a consultant who reviews your books once a quarter. The way it works well is:

- They own the monthly close process and board package preparation
- They sit in on board meetings and manage follow-up items
- You call them when you're making a significant financial decision (a new hire, a pricing change, a contract structure question)
- They are proactively flagging issues before you see them

The mistake is treating a fractional CFO as a vendor. The founders who get value treat them as a part-time executive — with real ownership of the function and real accountability for outcomes.

## Part III — Building the Reporting Architecture

Whether you have a fractional CFO or not, you need a reporting architecture. Here is what Prospectra's should look like at this stage.

### The Monthly Board Package (Core Financial Section)

Every board package should contain, in this order:

**1. Cash and Runway Summary** (1 page)
- Current cash balance
- Current monthly net burn (actual, last 3 months average)
- Runway at current burn (months)
- Runway at projected burn (months, based on plan)
- Key cash events in next 90 days (large payments, expected revenue)

**2. P&L vs. Budget** (1 page)
- Revenue: actual vs. plan, month and YTD
- COGS: actual vs. plan
- Gross margin: actual vs. plan
- OpEx by category: actual vs. plan
- Net burn: actual vs. plan
- Variance notes: explain anything > 10% off plan in one sentence

**3. KPI Dashboard** (1 page, tied to operating plan milestones)
- ARR and ARR growth
- Customer count and net new
- CAC (if you have enough data)
- Churn / NRR
- Key operational metrics specific to the business

**4. 13-Week Cash Forecast** (can be an appendix)
- Week-by-week cash position
- Revenue assumptions clearly stated
- Material discretionary spending flagged

This is not a large document. A well-run board package financial section is 3-4 pages. The founders who send 40-slide financial decks are either hiding something or don't understand what their board actually needs.

### The Monthly Close Process

By the 5th business day of each month, you should know:
- Exact cash balance
- All invoices sent and outstanding AR
- All bills paid and outstanding AP
- P&L for the prior month, reconciled to bank statements
- Updated burn and runway calculation

This requires a bookkeeper who takes this seriously and a founder who reviews it within 24 hours. The single most common failure mode is a monthly close process that produces numbers the founder doesn't read until the board meeting. That's not a financial controls problem — it's a leadership problem.

## Part IV — Prospectra-Specific Architecture

For Prospectra specifically, the financial architecture should be built around three dynamics:

**1. Revenue is not yet linear.** Early customers will be large contracts with payment timing that creates lumpiness. Your financial model needs to show investors that you understand this — that you're tracking ARR and committed revenue separately from cash received, and that your runway calculation accounts for payment timing risk.

**2. R&D costs have option value.** The Databricks infrastructure you're building is not just an operating expense — it is an asset that creates the analytical capability that justifies the product price. How you account for this (capitalizing software development costs vs. expensing) has real P&L and valuation implications. This is a conversation for a fractional CFO or tax attorney, not a bookkeeper.

**3. You will raise again.** Every financial decision you make now is visible to the Series A investor who will do diligence on you in 12-18 months. Clean books, correct cap table, well-documented equity grants, and an auditable financial history are not compliance requirements — they are assets on your Series A pitch.

The founders who close Series A rounds cleanly are not the ones who had the best product (though that matters). They are the ones whose due diligence process took three weeks instead of three months, because the data room was organized, the financials were clean, and there were no "oh, that's going to be a problem" moments for the VC's finance team.

## Part V — The Authorization Control Architecture

One page. That's all this needs to be. For Prospectra at current stage, the authorization policy should read roughly as follows:

---

*Prospectra Financial Authorization Policy — v1.0*

**Under $500:** Any founder can approve without pre-authorization. Log in QuickBooks.

**$500–$5,000:** Any founder can approve. Other founder notified by Slack within 24 hours.

**$5,000–$25,000:** Both founders align before commitment. Document rationale in Notion.

**Over $25,000:** Both founders plus board lead align before commitment. Full memo required.

**Equity grants:** Board approval required. Cap table updated within 5 days. Counsel reviews.

**Revenue contracts:** Standard terms in CRM. Non-standard terms require founder review and legal sign-off if over $50K.

---

This is not bureaucratic. This is what a well-run company looks like. When you show this to a Series A investor, it reads as founder maturity — which is one of the things they are explicitly evaluating.

## Investment Implications

Financial discipline has direct investment signal value — both as an internal operating tool and as a lens for evaluating companies you might invest in.

**For portfolio companies you evaluate:** The quality of a company's financial reporting is a leading indicator of operational quality broadly. Founders who can't explain their burn with precision are typically also imprecise about customer economics, product priorities, and hiring decisions. Due diligence on financial controls is due diligence on management quality.

**For the macro portfolio:** The same principle applies at the sovereign level. Countries with transparent, credible financial reporting — central bank independence, reliable fiscal data, consistent accounting standards — carry a structural premium in fixed income markets. The "governance premium" in sovereign bonds is real and measurable: countries with strong institutions consistently borrow at lower rates than their economic fundamentals alone would justify. This is the same logic as startup financial discipline, applied at sovereign scale.

**Asset classes to watch:**
- EM sovereign bonds: governance quality is directly priced in spread differentials. Countries that adopt IMF reporting standards and independent fiscal councils see spread compression over 2-3 year horizons.
- Private equity / venture: the firms that consistently outperform are operationally sophisticated — they build financial infrastructure in portfolio companies immediately post-close, not 18 months later when the problem is acute.
- Small-cap equities: internal controls quality is the single best predictor of accounting restatements, which are catastrophic for equity values. Governance screens that include internal controls quality add genuine alpha.

The financial reporting architecture at Prospectra can itself become a Databricks pipeline.

**Immediate opportunity:** Connect your QuickBooks/Xero API to a Databricks ingestion layer. Build a dashboard that shows real-time cash position, burn by category, and ARR metrics — updated daily rather than monthly. Cross this with GDELT political risk scores to identify markets where governance is improving or deteriorating — that divergence is where the alpha lives.
