## 1. The Concept: What Is Geoeconomics?

**Geoeconomics** is the use of economic instruments to achieve geopolitical objectives — and the study of the geopolitical consequences of economic decisions.

The term was coined by strategist **Edward Luttwak** in a 1990 essay: *"From Geopolitics to Geo-Economics: Logic of Conflict, Grammar of Commerce."* His thesis was almost prophetic: with the Cold War ending, great powers would increasingly compete through trade flows, investment, financial infrastructure, and technology — not primarily through military force. The logic of conflict would remain, but the grammar would shift from weapons to economic tools.

Thirty-five years later, Luttwak looks like he was ahead of the curve by three decades.

**The three-phase shift:**

| Era | Primary Instrument of Great Power Competition |
|---|---|
| Cold War (1947–1991) | Military alliances, nuclear deterrence, proxy wars |
| Liberal Unipolar Moment (1991–2008) | Trade liberalization, FDI, multilateral institutions |
| Geoeconomic Competition (2008–present) | Tariffs, sanctions, export controls, industrial policy, financial exclusion |

The 2008 financial crisis cracked the consensus that globalization was politically neutral. China's rise through that crisis (it barely flinched while Western economies collapsed) made policymakers in Washington, Brussels, and Tokyo ask an uncomfortable question: *Are we subsidizing our own strategic competitor?* The answer they arrived at — eventually, slowly, then all at once — was yes.

## 2. The Geoeconomic Toolkit: Full Synthesis

We've studied each of these instruments individually over the past ten lessons. Here is the complete map:

| Tool | Strategic Logic | Recent Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Sanctions** | Cut target from financial system to coerce behavior | Russia 2022 SWIFT exclusion; Iran maximum pressure |
| **Tariffs** | Tax imports to protect strategic industries or punish rivals | US Section 301 tariffs on China (2018–present) |
| **FDI Screening** | Block foreign capital from acquiring strategic assets | CFIUS blocking Chinese M&A in semiconductors, biotech |
| **Export Controls** | Deny rivals access to critical technology | NVIDIA AI chip restrictions; Dutch ASML EUV controls |
| **Outbound Investment Controls** | Prevent domestic capital from building up rivals' strategic capacity | Biden EO (2023) restricting US investment in Chinese AI/chips/quantum |
| **Industrial Policy** | Subsidize domestic strategic sectors to build competitive advantage | US CHIPS Act ($52B); EU Chips Act; IRA ($369B) |
| **Financial Exclusion** | Deny access to dollar clearing, correspondent banking | Secondary sanctions forcing third-country banks to choose |
| **Technology Decoupling** | Fragment global technology ecosystem into rival blocs | Techno-bloc formation around US vs. China AI/telecoms stacks |
| **Market Access Conditionality** | Use market access as bargaining leverage | EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism |
| **Entity Lists / Unreliable Entity** | Designate specific firms as national security threats | US Entity List; China's Unreliable Entity List (76 new additions in 2025) |

This is not a grab-bag of unrelated policies. **It is a coherent strategic system** — each tool reinforces the others. Export controls slow a rival's technological development. Industrial policy accelerates your own. Outbound investment controls dry up the capital that would fund the rival's catch-up. Financial exclusion limits their ability to trade around your restrictions. Entity lists isolate specific firms and force the global private sector to choose sides.

The synthesis is deliberate. The US government calls it "a small yard with a high fence" — targeting precisely the highest-leverage technologies while avoiding a full decoupling that would harm US companies and allies.

## 3. Three Operating Logics

All geoeconomic tools map to one of three strategic logics:

### Logic 1 — Coercive Geoeconomics
**Goal:** Inflict economic pain to force a behavioral change.
**Timeframe:** 1–5 years.
**Works when:** The target is more dependent on the sanctioning country than vice versa, and has limited capacity to absorb costs or find alternatives.
**Example:** Iran sanctions — sustained financial exclusion forced Iran back to the negotiating table (temporarily).
**Fails when:** Target diversifies (Russia post-2022 pivot to China, India, UAE) or coalition fractures (secondary sanctions fatigue).
**Investment signal:** Coercive campaigns create sustained dislocations in targeted-country assets — currencies, sovereign bonds, equities. The dislocation can be an entry point if the coercion eventually reverses.

### Logic 2 — Competitive Geoeconomics
**Goal:** Build structural advantages that compound over time, shifting the long-run balance of power.
**Timeframe:** 5–20 years.
**Works when:** Industrial policy is disciplined and targets genuine comparative advantage gaps (US semiconductor manufacturing was genuinely hollowed out; the CHIPS Act addresses a real vulnerability).
**Fails when:** Subsidy races trigger overcapacity (see: solar panels, EVs, steel — China's playbook creating global gluts).
**Investment signal:** Competitive geoeconomics is the most powerful source of durable thematic investment: defense, domestic manufacturing, critical minerals, AI infrastructure, clean energy supply chains.

### Logic 3 — Defensive Geoeconomics
**Goal:** Reduce vulnerability to economic coercion from rivals. Build strategic autonomy.
**Timeframe:** 3–10 years.
**Works when:** The dependency being addressed is genuine (EU on Russian gas was a real vulnerability; diversification was strategically necessary regardless of cost).
**Investment signal:** Defensive geoeconomics drives supply chain rewiring — LNG infrastructure, reshored manufacturing, alternative supplier countries (India, Vietnam, Mexico as "friend-shoring" beneficiaries), domestic energy resilience.

## 4. The Live Case: Trump-Xi Beijing Summit, May 2026

This week, the most important geoeconomic story in the world reached a fragile inflection point. Trump and Xi met in Beijing — and the outcome was precisely what geoeconomics predicts:

- **No breakthrough.** The structural competition cannot be resolved in a summit. The technology decoupling, export controls, and investment restrictions are not bargaining chips to be traded — they are strategic choices driven by perceived security imperatives.
- **A tactical truce.** China agreed to buy 200 Boeing jets — a targeted economic concession designed to give Trump something domestically useful (manufacturing jobs, export numbers) while conceding nothing strategically.
- **Tariff architecture shifting.** The Supreme Court ruled Trump's IEEPA tariffs unlawful in early 2026. The administration is now rebuilding the tariff regime under Section 122, 301, and 232 — slower, more legally durable, but the economic effect is similar. The average US household faces ~$1,500/year in tariff costs.
- **Export controls deepening.** US restrictions on NVIDIA AI chips are expanding, not retreating. China's Unreliable Entity List continues to grow.

**The CEO read:** The Beijing summit is a signal that both sides want to manage the relationship — not that geoeconomics is reversing. The grammar of commerce is being used to contain the logic of conflict. This is exactly the geoeconomic model in action.

**The investment implication:** Do not confuse a tactical truce with a structural reversal. The thematic investment plays driven by geoeconomic competition (defense, semiconductors, critical minerals, friend-shoring supply chains) remain structurally intact.

## 5. Investment Framework: The Geoeconomics Map

| Strategic Logic | Duration | Asset Classes | Directional View |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coercive (sanctions/tariffs) | 1–5 yr | EM FX of targeted countries; commodity rerouting beneficiaries | Short targeted-country FX on escalation; long commodity infrastructure |
| Competitive (industrial policy) | 5–15 yr | Defense equities, semiconductor equipment, domestic manufacturing, AI infrastructure | Long structurally — CHIPS, defense spending, critical minerals |
| Defensive (supply chain rewiring) | 3–10 yr | LNG infrastructure, alternative supply chain countries, domestic energy | Long friend-shoring beneficiaries: India, Vietnam, Mexico; LNG exporters |

**The key risk:** Geoeconomics has a reversal mode. Sanctions get lifted (Iran 2015 JCPOA). Trade deals get struck. The CHIPS Act could be defunded. Every geoeconomic thesis needs a falsification condition: *"I am wrong if [specific political/policy event] occurs."*

**The CEO Portfolio Note:** Our investment thesis is structurally aligned with the competitive geoeconomics logic — long defense, semiconductors, critical minerals, and energy transition supply chains. The 2026 Beijing summit is not a reason to reduce those positions. It is a data point confirming that the competition continues and that both sides are managing it, not resolving it.

## 6. The Intellectual History: Why Geoeconomics Is Different From Mercantilism

Students sometimes confuse geoeconomics with mercantilism — the 17th-century doctrine that nations should maximize exports and minimize imports to accumulate gold. They are related but distinct:

**Mercantilism:** Economics serves the state's wealth accumulation. Trade is a zero-sum competition for treasure.

**Geoeconomics:** Economics serves the state's *strategic position*. Trade, investment, and technology flows are instruments of national security — and vulnerability. The goal is not wealth maximization but *strategic autonomy and competitive advantage*.

The difference matters: a mercantilist would maximize Boeing jet sales to China. A geoeconomist would ask whether those sales strengthen a rival's aerospace capacity and whether the revenue is worth the strategic cost. The Trump-Xi Boeing deal resolves this tension in favor of tactical mercantilism — but the underlying geoeconomic logic reasserts itself as soon as the summit photo op fades.

## 8. Reflection Questions

1. **The Blowback Problem.** The US CHIPS Act was designed to rebuild domestic semiconductor manufacturing — but major fabs (TSMC Arizona, Samsung Austin) are behind schedule and over budget, while US chip companies lobbied against the export controls that were supposed to protect them. What does this tell you about the gap between geoeconomic *intent* and geoeconomic *execution*? Under what conditions does competitive geoeconomics actually deliver strategic advantage versus simply creating expensive market distortions?

2. **The Timing Problem.** Geoeconomic structural themes play out over 5–15 years. But equity markets price industrial policy tailwinds immediately — defense stocks and semiconductor equipment companies surged the day the CHIPS Act passed. How do you distinguish between "I'm buying the structural theme early" and "I'm buying the hype and will be bored for five years while the market catches up to reality"? What metrics would tell you the structural thesis is actually advancing?

3. **The Middle Power Question.** The US and EU can weaponize the dollar and financial infrastructure because they *own* the infrastructure. China can use rare earths and manufacturing capacity because it *dominates* those sectors. What geoeconomic tools are available to a mid-size power — say India, Brazil, or Turkey — that lacks dollar leverage *or* manufacturing dominance? What does this asymmetry mean for EM investment risk premiums?
