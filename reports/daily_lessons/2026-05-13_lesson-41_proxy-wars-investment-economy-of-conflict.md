## 1. What Is a Proxy War — and Why It Persists

A **proxy war** is armed conflict in which a sponsoring state supports a third-party actor (state or non-state) to fight on its behalf, advancing the sponsor's strategic interests while avoiding the full costs of direct engagement.

### Why Great Powers Use Proxies

Three structural reasons, all durable:

**A. Nuclear deterrence creates a permission structure.** When two nuclear-armed rivals go head-to-head, escalation risk is existential. Proxy warfare allows states to contest territory, resources, and influence while keeping the conflict beneath the threshold that triggers direct retaliation. The Cold War institutionalized this: Korea, Vietnam, Angola, Nicaragua, Afghanistan — all proxy theatres.

**B. Proxy wars are cost-shifted.** The sponsoring state provides weapons, funding, intelligence, and advisors — but the dying is done by someone else. Russia has run this playbook in Ukraine, Syria, and across the Sahel. Iran runs it through Hezbollah, the Houthis, and Iraqi Shia militias. The U.S. has deployed it for a century.

**C. Deniability preserves diplomatic room.** Proxy conflict allows sponsors to maintain official relations, keep trade channels open, and avoid the full economic consequences of declared war — even while actively fighting each other.

## 2. The Economics of Proxy War: Who Pays, Who Profits, Who Loses

### The Sponsor Side: Weapons and Money Flow Out

As of 2026:
- **$30+ billion in remaining U.S. appropriations** will be used to replenish weapons donated to Ukraine, flowing directly to American defense contractors
- Ukraine's defense production capacity is projected at **$55 billion in 2026**
- The EU's **€840 billion defense spending increase** (announced 2025) signals a structural shift

**Defense contractors are not cyclical plays in a proxy war environment — they are structural beneficiaries.** Proxy war in 2026 is not tank warfare. It is: drone production and counter-drone systems, electronic warfare, long-range precision munitions, ISR, and cybersecurity.

### The Reconstruction Side: Money Flows Back In

Ukraine is the current case study:
- Reconstruction cost estimated at **$524 billion over the next decade**
- **$136.5 billion financing gap** through 2029
- The **U.S.-Ukraine Reconstruction Investment Fund** (the "minerals deal") gives American companies preferential access to Ukraine's critical minerals and reconstruction contracts

This is not aid. This is structured geopolitical deal-making with explicit economic terms.

### The Battlefield Country: The Full Cost

1. Capital flight and currency collapse
2. Infrastructure destruction (Ukraine's GDP contracted by ~35% in the first year of full-scale invasion)
3. Brain drain
4. Commodity supply disruption

## 3. The Commodity Channel: How Proxy Wars Move Global Prices

When a proxy conflict disrupts production or transit:
1. **Supply shock** → immediate price spike
2. **Route disruption** → shipping costs rise, freight rates spike
3. **Substitution pressure** → demand shifts to non-disrupted sources, repricing upward
4. **Duration premium** → futures curves steepen

**Current signals (2026):**
- **Ukraine/Russia:** Black Sea grain corridor uncertainty has repriced global wheat and corn
- **Houthis/Red Sea:** ~12% of global trade transits Bab el-Mandeb; attacks force rerouting around the Cape of Good Hope
- **Sahel/Africa Corps:** Russia's proxy control is targeting French uranium supply chains; Niger was France's largest uranium supplier before the 2023 coup

## 4. The Market Mispricing Pattern

**Markets underprice proxy war duration and overprice resolution speed.**

Consistent pattern:
1. Market initially panics (sharp but temporary spike)
2. Markets "normalize" when resolution appears possible
3. Conflict either resumes or freezes — "normalized" prices prove too optimistic
4. Assets reprice lower again, often repeatedly

This reflects a structural bias: **financial markets are built around quarterly earnings cycles; proxy wars unfold over years or decades.**

The historical base rate for proxy wars is 8–12 years. If markets are pricing a 2-year resolution, there is a systematic mispricing — and it cuts in multiple directions simultaneously.

## 5. Investment Implications — Directional Framework

| Asset Class | Proxy War Effect | Direction | Timeframe |
|---|---|---|---|
| Defense equities (drone, EW, precision munitions) | Structural demand uplift | Long | 2–5 years |
| European defense contractors | EU rearmament + Ukraine support | Long | 3–5 years |
| Uranium | Sahel supply disruption + energy security | Long | 2–4 years |
| Agricultural commodities | Black Sea route uncertainty | Long (with vol) | 1–3 years |
| Shipping/tanker rates | Red Sea disruption, rerouting premium | Long (cyclical) | 6–18 months |
| Ukraine reconstruction plays | Long-horizon reconstruction finance | Long (illiquid) | 5–10 years |
| EM sovereign bonds (conflict-adjacent) | Risk premium elevation | Underweight | Duration-dependent |
| Safe havens (gold, CHF, UST) | Conflict escalation hedges | Tactical overweight | Episodic |

**One CEO Note:** The defense trade is broadly understood and widely owned — mean reversion risk is real if proxy wars de-escalate faster than expected. The highest asymmetry may be in *reconstruction finance and resource access* plays, which are underowned because they require accepting illiquidity and a long horizon most institutional investors can't hold.

## Key Concepts Recap

- **Proxy war** = armed conflict fought by surrogates, sponsored by great powers below the direct-war threshold
- **Nuclear deterrence** creates the structural permission structure for proxy warfare
- **Defense contractors** are structural (not cyclical) beneficiaries — but the capabilities that matter have shifted to drones, EW, and precision munitions
- **Commodity channels** are the highest-conviction investment expression of proxy war
- **Reconstruction finance** is the long-horizon play — illiquid, underowned
- **Markets systematically underprice proxy war duration** — the base rate is years to decades
