## Opening Question

*You hired your first researcher six months ago. They were the best candidate you interviewed. You've been friends since university. They work hard, they care deeply, and they are clearly not performing at the level the role requires now that the company has moved.*

**How do you have that conversation — and how do you know whether you're making the right call before you have it?**

This is the question that derails more early-stage CEOs than almost any other. The failure mode is not cruelty. It is avoidance. Most founders who mismanage underperformance don't do it by having the conversation badly. They do it by not having the conversation at all — or having it so softened, so hedged, so wrapped in diplomatic language that the message never lands.

The result is a person who does not know they are underperforming, cannot make the changes required, and eventually gets fired without ever having been given the honest chance to succeed or course-correct.

This lesson is about building the systems and the will to avoid that outcome.

## Part I — Why Early-Stage Performance Management Fails

### The Ambiguity Problem

At an early-stage company, roles are poorly defined by necessity. You hired a "researcher" and they ended up doing half the analyst work, some client communication, a bit of data engineering, and helped pitch investors twice. In an environment that fluid, how do you evaluate performance?

The answer most founders give (implicitly, not explicitly) is: *I'll know it when I see it.* This is the root cause of most performance management failures. "I'll know it when I see it" means:

1. The employee doesn't know what success looks like for their role
2. Feedback is delivered against a standard that was never communicated
3. When the feedback finally arrives, it feels unfair — because the standard was invisible

The fix is almost trivially simple in concept and genuinely hard in practice: **define what good looks like before you need to evaluate whether someone is achieving it.**

### The Friendship Problem

Many early-stage companies hire friends, former colleagues, or people the founder has a deep personal relationship with. This is not inherently wrong — trust is a genuine asset in a startup context. The problem is that personal relationships create a conflict between two roles the founder has to play simultaneously:

- **The friend:** wants to protect the relationship, avoid discomfort, give the benefit of the doubt
- **The CEO:** accountable for the company's performance, has obligations to other team members and customers

These roles are in tension. Most founders resolve the tension by staying in the friend role and failing to perform the CEO role. The consequence is not just individual underperformance — it is a systemic signal to every other person in the company that accountability is not real.

When high performers on your team see that a close relationship with the CEO protects someone from accountability, they draw the correct conclusion: results don't matter as much as proximity to the CEO. That belief, once established, is extraordinarily difficult to reverse.

### The "Not Yet" Problem

A variant of the avoidance failure: the performance concern is real, clearly visible, and has been visible for months — but the CEO has been waiting for it to self-correct. The inner monologue sounds like: *They're going through something difficult personally. We're in a busy period. Once this project finishes. Once we close this customer. Once we hire someone to take some of the load off.*

The "not yet" impulse is entirely human. The problem is that underperformance compounds. A person who is 70% effective at month 3 is usually still 70% effective at month 8 — unless something actually changes. The "not yet" CEO has, functionally, accepted 70% performance as the baseline. Every other person on the team has noticed.

## Part II — The Performance Conversation Architecture

### Before the Conversation: Are You Solving the Right Problem?

Before concluding someone is underperforming, answer three questions honestly:

1. **Have you clearly defined what success in this role looks like, and have you communicated that definition explicitly?** If the answer is no, the first performance conversation should be "I have not been clear enough about what I expect — let me fix that now."

2. **Have you given specific feedback previously, and has there been time to improve?** A person cannot course-correct against feedback they haven't received. If you're having a performance concern without having delivered specific feedback first, the first conversation is feedback, not a performance warning.

3. **Is this a will problem or a skill problem?** The two have different implications:
   - **Skill gap:** The person wants to succeed but lacks the capability for the role as it exists now. This is sometimes fixable (training, scoping changes, a different role) and sometimes not (the role requires competencies the person will never develop).
   - **Will gap:** The person has the capability but is not applying it — motivation, engagement, or personal fit has broken down. This is harder to fix than most CEOs believe.

If the answer to questions 1 and 2 is "no," you are not ready for a performance conversation. Fix those first.

### The Conversation Itself: Structure and Tone

The most effective feedback conversations share a common structure. This is not a script — it is a framework to ensure the right information gets communicated and the right outcome is possible.

**1. Specificity of observation**

Start with observable behavior, not character judgment. 

Wrong: *"I'm concerned about your output quality."*  
Right: *"The last three research memos had factual errors that required correction before client delivery — [specific memo], [specific memo], [specific memo]. That's the pattern I want to address."*

The specific example:
- Removes ambiguity about what the issue is
- Prevents the conversation from becoming a debate about whether a problem exists
- Makes it easier for the person to understand what they need to change

**2. Impact articulation**

Connect the behavior to consequence. Not as a threat — as reality.

*"When research has to be corrected after it's been sent, it creates two problems: it damages our credibility with the client who received the incorrect version, and it takes me time to fix that could go toward building the product."*

Most people underperforming are not fully aware of the impact. Making the impact visible is not cruel — it is the information they need to understand why the conversation is happening.

**3. Explicit expectation**

State what success looks like, in concrete terms, going forward.

*"What I need over the next 90 days is research memos that clear QA on first review at least 90% of the time. That's the standard I'm going to hold this role to, and it's the standard I should have communicated more clearly from the start."*

This gives the person something to aim at. It also creates a clear basis for evaluation at the 90-day mark.

**4. Support and resources**

What will you do to help? This is not about softening the message — it is about being a genuine manager, not just an evaluator.

*"I want to invest in making this work. Let's add a peer review step before memos go out for the next 30 days — not as a punishment, but so you get real-time feedback while we're resetting the standard."*

**5. Clarity about stakes**

If this is a genuine performance threshold conversation (not a coaching conversation), say so directly.

*"I want to be honest with you: this is a conversation I've been avoiding for a few months, and that wasn't fair to you. This is a serious concern. If the pattern doesn't change over the next 90 days, I'll have to make a harder call about the role. I'm telling you that clearly because I want you to have the real picture."*

This is the part most founders omit. And its omission is the single most common reason that people are genuinely surprised when they are eventually let go — they had never been told, in plain language, that the stakes were real.

## Part III — Expectations Without Bureaucracy

### The Problem With Informal Cultures

Early-stage companies often pride themselves on being non-bureaucratic. No performance review cycles, no structured 1:1 templates, no HR processes. And in the first 5 people, this is often right — the formality creates more overhead than value.

The danger is when the team grows past that informal threshold and the culture of informality persists. A 12-person team operating without structured expectations and feedback is not non-bureaucratic — it is operating without a nervous system. The signals that would allow the organization to self-correct (what's working, what isn't, who is on track, who isn't) are simply not being transmitted.

The solution is not bureaucracy. It is lightweight, high-signal infrastructure:

**Weekly 1:1s (30 minutes):** One recurring meeting between manager and report where the agenda is driven by the report, not the manager. What are you working on? Where are you stuck? What do you need from me? This is not a status update — it is a diagnostic conversation.

**Quarterly role scorecards:** Every quarter, take 30 minutes to update the role scorecard (from Lesson 366) for each member of your team. What are the outcomes expected for this role over the next 90 days? Are those outcomes still the right ones given how the company has evolved? This is not a formal review. It is a forcing function to ensure the definition of "good" stays current.

**The "Start/Stop/Continue" conversation:** Once a quarter, ask each person to tell you: one thing they want to start doing, one thing they want to stop doing, and one thing they want to continue. Then share your own Start/Stop/Continue for them. This creates a bilateral feedback channel at very low transaction cost.

None of this requires HR. None of it requires a performance management system. It requires only 45–60 minutes per person per quarter and a commitment to having the real conversations rather than the comfortable ones.

## Part IV — The Hard Call: When Someone Is No Longer Right for the Role

### Recognizing the Threshold

There is a point in performance management beyond which the conversation shifts from "how do we get this person to where the role requires" to "this person and this role are fundamentally mismatched." Recognizing that threshold is one of the hardest managerial judgments at an early-stage company.

The signals that suggest you've crossed it:

1. **The gap between the role's requirements and the person's capability is structural, not situational.** The role now requires skills the person does not have and is unlikely to develop in the time available. This is different from a person who is underperforming due to a discrete issue they can fix.

2. **Feedback has been delivered clearly and consistently, time has been given, and the pattern has not changed.** If you have had 2–3 explicit performance conversations (not hints, not softened feedback — explicit conversations) and the output has not substantively changed, you have a data point about capability or will that should inform your decision.

3. **The company is paying an opportunity cost.** Every month a role is filled by someone who is not performing at the level required is a month you are not hiring the person who could. That is not a small cost at a company with limited headcount.

4. **Other team members are carrying the weight.** This is often the clearest signal: when the rest of the team has quietly reorganized their own work to compensate for someone's underperformance, and they know you know, the credibility cost of inaction is compounding.

### The Separation Conversation

When you have made the decision, the separation conversation should be:

**Definitive, not ambiguous.** You are not "thinking about making a change." You have made the decision. Do not create false hope by framing a decision as a possibility.

**Brief, not exhaustive.** This is not the moment for a comprehensive performance review. The person knows the concerns — if you've been managing correctly, you've told them. The conversation is: "I've made the decision to make a change. Here's what that means practically. I want to be as helpful as I can in your transition."

**Respectful of dignity.** How you handle the end of an employment relationship is observed by every other person in the company. A separation handled with fairness, clear process, and genuine care for the person's transition is a cultural signal as powerful as any all-hands speech. The reverse is equally true.

**Legally appropriate.** Depending on jurisdiction and tenure, there are notice periods, severance considerations, and documentation requirements. At the point you believe you are approaching a termination decision, get a quick legal review of the requirements. In California and several other jurisdictions, the defaults are not in the employer's favor, and learning this after the conversation is worse than before.

### The Prospectra-Specific Context

Prospectra is likely to face its first material performance call within the next 12–18 months if hiring proceeds. The particular risk is in research roles: the quality bar for institutional-grade geopolitical analysis is high and unforgiving, and it can take 3–6 months to fully assess whether a researcher meets it.

**Mitigation strategy:** Build a 90-day trial period into all research hires with explicit output expectations written in advance. The output expectations are not generic ("good research") — they are specific: three fully developed investment memos at institutional quality, with peer review, before the end of the trial. This is not punitive. It is fair to both sides: the hire knows exactly what they're working toward, and you have a clean evaluation framework.

## Key Concepts

- **Ambiguity is the root cause of most performance management failures** — not cruelty, but unclear expectations
- **The will/skill distinction:** Skill gaps are sometimes fixable; will gaps rarely are at the pace a startup requires
- **Performance conversation structure:** Specific observation → impact → explicit expectation → support → clarity about stakes
- **Lightweight infrastructure over bureaucracy:** Weekly 1:1s, quarterly scorecards, Start/Stop/Continue — low overhead, high signal
- **The separation conversation:** Definitive, brief, respectful of dignity, legally appropriate
- **Observation by the team:** How you handle underperformance and separation is a cultural signal louder than any all-hands speech

## Investment Implications

Performance management quality is one of the strongest early predictors of organizational scalability — and it is systematically under-observed by investors. A few signals that surface in due diligence:

- **Team tenure distribution:** If a Series A company has had 40% turnover in its first 3 years, there is a performance management or culture problem. If that turnover is concentrated in specific functions (all in sales, all in research), the function is almost certainly misaligned with the company's current stage requirements.

- **Founder reference checks:** Sophisticated investors now conduct reference checks on founders, not just executives. The question they ask former employees is almost always a performance management question in disguise: "How did [founder] handle it when someone on the team wasn't working out?"

- **Engineering/research velocity as a proxy:** In data and research businesses like Prospectra, the lag between hiring a researcher and that researcher producing institutional-quality output is measurable. Teams with strong performance management have shorter lags. Teams without it have researchers who are still "ramping" two years in.

**Macro context (2026):** In a tighter labor market for specialized talent (geopolitical analysts, data engineers with financial services domain knowledge), the cost of a wrong hire has increased — both the financial cost and the opportunity cost of the time it takes to re-recruit. Building performance management infrastructure early, when it feels premature, is the highest-ROI insurance policy an early-stage CEO can buy.

## Databricks Angle

**Building an Internal Performance Analytics Layer:**

The same analytical discipline applied to market signals can be applied to team performance:

1. **Dataset:** Internal production metrics — research memo completion rate, QA pass rate on first review, signal-to-delivery latency, client feedback scores per analyst. These are measurable if you define them.

2. **Pipeline idea:** A simple Databricks notebook that pulls from your production tracking system (even a spreadsheet, initially) and computes per-researcher quality metrics weekly. The goal is not surveillance — it is to make the performance signal visible before it becomes a crisis.

3. **Feature engineering:** Construct a "quality trajectory" feature for each researcher: is their QA pass rate improving, flat, or declining over their first 90 days? Early trajectory is highly predictive of 6-month performance — this is a hiring calibration tool as much as a management tool.

4. **GDELT relevance:** There is an interesting cross-signal here: does research quality (as measured by QA scores) correlate with geopolitical event intensity? During high-intensity periods (elevated GDELT tone scores in a region a researcher covers), does output quality hold or drop? Researchers who maintain quality under event-load are producing a different product than those who don't — and that distinction matters to institutional clients.

## Reflection Questions

1. **The clarity test:** Think about every person on the Prospectra team today. Can you write, in two sentences, what success looks like for their role over the next 90 days? If you can't — not because the role is new, but because you've never defined it clearly — that is an action item, not a reflection question.

2. **The avoidance inventory:** Is there a performance concern you've been aware of for more than 60 days without having an explicit conversation about it? What has the "not yet" reasoning been? What is the actual cost — to the company, to the other team members, and to the underperforming person — of 60 more days without the conversation?

3. **Will vs. skill:** Take one performance concern you're aware of. Walk through the will/skill diagnostic honestly. Does the person lack the capability for this role at this stage? Or do they have the capability but something has broken in motivation or fit? The answer determines the intervention — and the two interventions are completely different.
