## Opening Question

The wire hits your account. $5–12 million, depending on what you raised. Your board seat is filled. Your cap table is clean. Your term sheet is signed.

**Here is the question almost every founder fails to answer well before spending the first dollar:**

*If you had to explain your capital allocation decisions to your Series B investor in 24 months — not just what you built, but why you chose to build it in that sequence, with that team composition, on that timeline — could you reconstruct the logic from first principles?*

Most founders treat the post-close period as permission to execute instincts accumulated during the fundraise. They hire the people they've been wanting to hire. They build the product features they deprioritized to hit the close. They fill the whiteboard with roadmap.

That is execution, but it is not strategy. Strategy requires that every dollar deployed can be traced back to a specific hypothesis: *this hire unlocks this capability, which enables this metric, which is required for this milestone, which is the thing a Series B investor will pay for.*

The founders who raise clean Series Bs — at higher valuations, on their terms, without a down round — are the ones who built that logic before spending dollar one.

## Part I — The Operating Plan as Investment Thesis

The operating plan for the 18-24 months after a Series A is not a financial projection. Financial projections are largely fiction at this stage and sophisticated investors know it.

The operating plan is a **sequenced hypothesis map**: a document that makes explicit the causal chain between capital inputs and the outcomes that define Series B eligibility.

### The Three Layers of an Operating Plan

**Layer 1: The Milestone Layer**
Define the 3–5 outcomes that must be true at the time of your Series B raise. These are not aspirational. They are the minimum evidence required for a Series B investor to build a credible growth model.

At Series A for a B2B SaaS company, typical Series B milestones look like:
- ARR: $3–8M (with meaningful growth rate — the "Rule of 40" matters here)
- Net Revenue Retention: 110%+ (demonstrates land-and-expand motion)
- 2–3 proof-of-market customers in target ICP that can be referenced
- Sales efficiency: CAC payback under 18 months
- Team: VP of Sales hired and ramping, VP of Engineering in seat

These are not your goals. They are the exit conditions for the round you're building toward. Everything else is in service of these.

**Layer 2: The Hypothesis Layer**
For each milestone, identify the 2–3 critical hypotheses that must prove true for you to achieve it. Be explicit and falsifiable.

*Example:*
- **Milestone:** $5M ARR by Month 22
- **Hypothesis 1:** Average contract value can scale to $80K+ with enterprise motion (currently $30K with SMB)
- **Hypothesis 2:** Sales cycle compresses below 90 days with a dedicated AE + SDR pair
- **Hypothesis 3:** Expansion revenue from existing customers reaches 30% of new ARR by Month 18

If you cannot articulate these hypotheses before spending, you are flying blind. The post-Series A period is not the time to discover what your business model is — it is the time to execute against a known thesis with capital behind it.

**Layer 3: The Resource Layer**
For each hypothesis, identify the minimum viable resource investment required to test it within 6 months. Not the full build — the minimum required to know whether the hypothesis is true or false.

This is where most founders over-spend. They hire for scale before proving the hypothesis. The discipline is: prove the hypothesis with minimum resources, then invest to scale the proven thesis.

## Part II — The Hiring Plan: The Highest-Leverage and Highest-Risk Deployment

Talent is typically 60–75% of Series A capital deployment. It is also the decision set with the longest lead time and the highest switching cost. A bad executive hire can absorb 6–18 months before you recognize and resolve it.

### The Sequencing Logic

The hiring plan should be derived from the milestone and hypothesis maps, not from intuition. The question is not "who do we need?" but "which capabilities must exist by what date to prove which hypothesis?"

**The Three Categories of Hires**

| Category | Description | Priority |
|----------|-------------|----------|
| Thesis-critical | Capabilities without which a key hypothesis cannot be tested | Immediate |
| Leverage hires | Multipliers on founder capacity in areas where founders are the bottleneck | Month 3–9 |
| Scale hires | Infrastructure for when the thesis is proven | Month 12+ |

**The most common mistake:** Hiring Scale hires in Month 1.

Founders close a Series A and immediately hire a CFO, a VP of Marketing, a Head of HR, and a Chief of Staff. These are scale hires — they are infrastructure for a company operating at 5x current size. At a 15-person Series A company, they consume capital, add management overhead, and provide negative leverage until the business reaches a threshold they were hired to manage at.

The thesis-critical hire for most early-stage B2B companies post-Series A is the first enterprise sales leader — typically a VP of Sales who can personally close deals while building the playbook. That hire directly tests the enterprise thesis. The CFO can wait until Month 12.

### Hiring for Series B Optionality, Not Just Series A Execution

Every senior hire you make in the 18 months post-Series A will still be on your team when you raise Series B. The Series B investor will evaluate those people. Mediocre executives who "get the job done" will cost you valuation or the round itself.

The discipline: hire people whose ceiling is 3x your current scale, not 1.2x. The marginal cost of hiring better is lower than the structural cost of managing through a Series B with an executive team that doesn't pass diligence.

## Part III — Capital Allocation: The Budget as Strategy

The Series A budget is not a spreadsheet — it is a public commitment to a theory of value creation. How you allocate capital signals what you believe about where leverage is.

### The Allocation Framework

There is no universal allocation — it varies by business model. But the logic is universal:

**Step 1:** Identify the primary constraint on growth. In most early-stage B2B companies, the constraint is one of three things: (a) product-market fit verification, (b) go-to-market machine, or (c) technical scalability. Your largest allocation should address the primary constraint.

**Step 2:** Allocate to constraint-resolution, not to parallel build. A common mistake is distributing capital evenly across product, sales, and marketing because "all three matter." All three do matter — but one is bottlenecking the others. Put the capital there first.

**Step 3:** Build a cash runway buffer. Run the model with a hard rule: you will begin the Series B process no later than Month 15 and will have 9 months of runway when you start. That means your effective spending window is Month 1–Month 15. Do not let burn creep push you to start a Series B with 6 months of runway. Investors can smell desperation through a spreadsheet.

### The 18-Month Budget Architecture

| Period | Priority | Guardrails |
|--------|----------|------------|
| Month 1–6 | Prove hypotheses with minimum viable resources | No infrastructure hires |
| Month 7–12 | Scale proven thesis; begin Series B benchmark tracking | Begin reporting board-level metrics monthly |
| Month 13–18 | Prepare for Series B process; optimize narrative | Re-confirm milestone achievement; identify gaps |

## Part IV — Metrics Architecture: Building the Series B Data Room Now

The Series B investor will ask for 24 months of cohort-level data. If you don't build the metrics infrastructure in Month 1, you will be reconstructing it from imperfect sources under time pressure during the raise. That is a negotiating disadvantage.

### The Metrics Stack to Instrument Immediately

**Revenue & Growth**
- MRR/ARR (total, new, expansion, contraction, churned)
- MoM and YoY growth rate
- Bookings vs. revenue (for ACV/TCV distinction)

**Customer**
- Net Revenue Retention (by cohort)
- Gross Revenue Retention (by cohort)
- Logo churn rate
- Expansion ARR as % of new ARR

**Sales**
- Pipeline by stage
- Conversion rates by stage
- Average sales cycle by segment
- CAC by channel
- CAC payback period

**Unit Economics**
- Gross margin (at contract level, not just aggregate)
- LTV/CAC ratio
- Payback period by cohort

The rule: if you cannot explain the movement of every one of these metrics in your monthly board report, you do not yet understand your business well enough to raise a Series B. Build the systems to track them starting day one.

## Part V — The Investor Relations Cadence Post-Close

Your Series A investor is now a board member and a financial stakeholder. The quality of that relationship over the next 18 months directly affects your Series B — investors talk to each other.

### The Reporting Rhythm

**Monthly Investor Update** (sent within 5 business days of month close)
- 1 page maximum
- Key metrics vs. plan (with brief narrative on variances)
- Top 3 accomplishments, Top 3 challenges
- One explicit ask (warm intro, customer reference, hiring lead)
- 1 sentence on Series B progress / milestone tracking

The investor update is not a status report. It is trust infrastructure. Investors who receive consistent, candid monthly updates give the benefit of the doubt when things go wrong. Investors who only hear from founders when they need something assume the worst.

**Quarterly Board Meeting** (structured)
- 2-hour format: 30 min financials, 45 min strategic discussion, 30 min exec updates, 15 min executive session (board only)
- No surprises in the board meeting — surface problems in monthly updates before they become board-level issues
- Build an explicit Series B milestone tracker into every board deck starting at Month 6

**The Proactive Bad News Protocol**
When something goes wrong — a key customer churns, a critical hire resigns, a product bet fails — communicate within 48 hours, with your assessment of root cause and the plan to address it.

Founders who surface bad news proactively demonstrate management competence. Investors who discover bad news independently lose trust in founders permanently. That loss of trust rarely recovers fully.
