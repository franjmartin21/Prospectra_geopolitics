## I. Nickel's Role in Batteries — and Why It Got More Important as Cobalt Got Less Important

### The Chemistry Linkage

The transition away from cobalt (Lesson 173) did not reduce critical mineral complexity — it transferred it to nickel. The dominant high-performance battery cathode chemistry, NMC, has evolved as follows:

| Chemistry | Ni% | Mn% | Co% | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| NMC 111 | 33% | 33% | 33% | Legacy, being phased out |
| NMC 532 | 50% | 30% | 20% | Still deployed, transitional |
| NMC 622 | 60% | 20% | 20% | Broadly deployed |
| NMC 811 | 80% | 10% | 10% | Current high-performance standard |
| Ultra-high-Ni NMC | 90%+ | ~5% | ~5% | Research frontier, 2028+ |

**The directional trend is unambiguous:** as cobalt content falls, nickel content rises. NMC 811 requires approximately 5–6× more nickel per kWh than NMC 111 did. The industry's solution to the cobalt problem created a structural demand surge for nickel — specifically for **high-purity battery-grade nickel sulfate**, a material that requires processing infrastructure the world barely had before 2018.

**NCA (Nickel Cobalt Aluminum):** Tesla's original cell chemistry (with Panasonic) and still used in certain premium applications. NCA is typically ~80% nickel, 15% cobalt, 5% aluminum. Similar nickel intensity to NMC 811, slightly higher cobalt content.

### Beyond Batteries: The Industrial Nickel Economy

Nickel has a demand base far larger than batteries, which matters for understanding price dynamics:

- **Stainless steel:** ~70% of all nickel demand. Stainless requires nickel alloying for corrosion resistance. This demand is relatively price-inelastic and linked to industrial production, construction, and infrastructure cycles.
- **Alloys and superalloys:** Nickel superalloys for jet engines, gas turbines, and industrial equipment. Defense-critical; not battery-grade.
- **Electroplating:** Decorative and functional applications; small and shrinking.
- **Batteries:** ~12–15% of demand in 2024, projected to reach 30–40% by 2030 as EV penetration accelerates.

**The investment complication:** Because stainless steel dominates demand, nickel price is primarily a function of industrial production cycles, not battery demand — at current battery penetration. As battery demand grows, it increasingly competes with stainless for Class 1 nickel, creating a structural premium for high-purity material. **The market for battery-grade nickel and the market for stainless-grade nickel are diverging — and most commodity nickel analysis conflates them.**

## II. The Two Nickel Markets: Class 1 and Class 2

This is the most important structural distinction in nickel, and the one most frequently missed by investors looking at the LME nickel price:

### Class 1 Nickel (>99.8% purity)
- **Sources:** Sulfide deposits (Canada, Russia, Australia, Zimbabwe)
- **Processing:** Conventional smelting and refining — well-established Western technology (Vale's Sudbury, Norilsk, BHP's Nickel West)
- **Uses:** Battery-grade nickel sulfate, electroplating, specialty alloys, defense
- **Price benchmark:** LME nickel (official contract, but see the 2022 crisis below)
- **Key characteristic:** Finite known reserves; major deposits in Canada (Sudbury), Russia (Norilsk), and a small number of other jurisdictions. **Geopolitically awkward: Russia's Norilsk Nickel is the world's largest producer of Class 1 nickel and it is sanctioned.**

### Class 2 Nickel (lower purity — nickel pig iron, ferronickel)
- **Sources:** Laterite deposits (Indonesia, Philippines, New Caledonia)
- **Processing:** Rotary Kiln Electric Furnace (RKEF) → nickel pig iron (NPI) for stainless; OR HPAL → mixed hydroxide precipitate (MHP) → battery-grade
- **Uses:** Stainless steel (NPI), and increasingly battery cathode precursors after HPAL upgrading
- **Price benchmark:** Typically a discount to LME nickel; NPI has its own spot pricing

**Why this matters for investment:** When Indonesia banned nickel ore exports in 2020, it forced laterite ore to be processed locally. This meant building RKEF (for stainless) and HPAL (for battery) plants in Indonesia — which Chinese companies did. The Class 2 Indonesian supply surge since 2021 has depressed global nickel prices and made high-cost sulfide deposits in Canada and Australia economically marginal. **The Indonesian supply flood is Class 2 material displacing Class 1 economics.**

## III. Indonesia: The Dominant Geology — and the Chinese Build

### The Numbers

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Indonesia nickel reserves | ~21 million tonnes (est. 40–45% of world total) |
| Indonesia 2024 production | ~1.8–2.0 million tonnes (est.) |
| Indonesia share of global production | ~50%+ |
| Philippines | ~2nd largest producer, ~10% |
| Russia (Norilsk) | ~3rd, ~9%, but sanctioned |
| Canada | ~4th, ~8%, high-cost Class 1 |

Indonesia's Halmahera, North Sulawesi, and Central Sulawesi provinces sit atop some of the world's richest nickel laterite deposits. Laterite ore is near-surface (easier to mine) but low-grade and requires significant processing to extract battery-grade nickel. The HPAL process — High Pressure Acid Leach — dissolves laterite ore in sulfuric acid under high temperature and pressure to selectively extract nickel and cobalt into a MHP that can then be refined into nickel sulfate.

### The Chinese Industrial Parks in Indonesia

**Indonesia Morowali Industrial Park (IMIP):**
- Built by Tsingshan (China's largest stainless steel producer) starting 2013
- Located in Central Sulawesi
- Has grown to the world's largest nickel processing complex
- Contains RKEF smelters (NPI for stainless) and increasingly HPAL units
- ~70,000+ workers; built entire infrastructure (roads, port, power plant)
- Tsingshan's role: not extracting ore itself (Indonesian miners supply ore) but processing it — controlling the value-added midstream layer

**QMB New Energy Materials (Zhejiang Huayou Cobalt + CATL joint venture):**
- HPAL plant in North Maluku
- Designed specifically for battery-grade MHP production
- Zhejiang Huayou is also China's largest cobalt refiner — the same company building nickel HPAL capacity
- CATL is the world's largest battery maker: integration of supply (Huayou) with demand (CATL) through a joint venture in Indonesian territory

**PT Vale Indonesia (now majority Vale, partly Bahodopi project with Huayou):**
- Vale Indonesia's Sorowako mine is a legacy Class 1 nickel operation
- But Vale entered a joint venture with Zhejiang Huayou Cobalt for new HPAL capacity
- **Even Western mining majors are partnering with Chinese processors to build Indonesian HPAL — because no Western company has cost-competitive HPAL technology at scale**

### Why China Built It and the West Didn't

Chinese companies built HPAL at scale in Indonesia for three structural reasons that Western mining companies cannot easily replicate:

1. **State-backed capital at below-market returns:** Tsingshan, Huayou, CMOC, and similar firms access Chinese policy bank financing and equity at costs that no Western mining company's balance sheet can match. They can accept low initial returns on projects that build market position.

2. **Engineering and EPC capacity at scale:** Chinese engineering firms (ENFI, NERIN) have built dozens of nickel HPAL circuits in Indonesia. They have the operational knowledge base. Western HPAL projects (BHP Ravensthorpe, First Quantum) have historically suffered from cost overruns, commissioning delays, and early operational failures that Chinese projects have progressively avoided.

3. **Speed and permitting:** Building in Indonesia's industrial park zones with government support compresses timelines that Western environmental and permitting processes extend to 10+ years. The Chinese built Morowali's first facilities faster than a Canadian mine can get a federal environmental permit.

**The result:** Indonesia has developed as a nickel processing hub — but the industrial value-add flows through Chinese corporate hands. Indonesia captures some wage income and royalties; China captures the industrial margin and strategic material control.

## IV. The LME Nickel Crisis of March 2022: A Warning About Market Structure

In March 2022, nickel's price increased 250% in 48 hours — one of the most extreme short squeezes in commodity market history:

**What happened:**
- Tsingshan Holding had built a massive short position in LME nickel (reportedly 150,000+ tonnes short at a time when global annual production is ~2.5 million tonnes)
- Russia's invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 created fears about Norilsk Nickel supply disruptions (Russia = ~9% of global supply, predominantly Class 1)
- Speculators and commodity funds began squeezing the position
- As nickel prices surged toward $100,000/tonne (from ~$25,000), the LME suspended trading for the first time since 1988 and canceled $4 billion in trades made in the chaotic trading hours

**What this reveals:**
1. **Market structure fragility:** LME nickel's price discovery mechanism is vulnerable to concentrated positions — particularly from Chinese industrial players whose physical production gives them enormous hedging rationale and information advantages
2. **Class 1/Class 2 divergence hidden in one price:** The LME nickel contract is technically for Class 1 nickel, but the flood of Indonesian NPI has changed the physical market reality. The LME price increasingly disconnects from actual battery-grade material supply and demand
3. **Geopolitical trigger sensitivity:** Norilsk Nickel alone being at risk from Russia sanctions moved the entire global nickel market — demonstrating how concentrated Western Class 1 supply is in one Russian company

**Investment implication:** LME nickel price is a **poor indicator of battery supply chain economics** — it reflects stainless steel industrial demand and financial positioning, not battery-grade nickel sulfate supply/demand. A new market structure is emerging (nickel sulfate spot pricing, separate from LME) that investors need to track independently.

## V. Indonesia's Resource Nationalism — What It Won and Lost

### The Ore Export Ban

Indonesia banned raw nickel ore exports in January 2020 — a year earlier than initially announced. The rationale was clear: why export ore at $40/tonne when you could export nickel pig iron at $2,500/tonne or nickel sulfate at $5,000+/tonne?

The EU filed a WTO complaint, arguing the ban violated trade rules. The WTO Dispute Settlement Body ruled against Indonesia in 2022. Indonesia appealed — and kept the ban. This is resource nationalism enforced against a WTO ruling, backed by the logic that a country with 45% of world nickel reserves can make this calculation.

**What Indonesia won:**
- Forced construction of domestic processing infrastructure (RKEF plants, HPAL plants)
- Increased royalty and tax revenues from value-added processing activity
- Employment (Morowali employs tens of thousands)
- Higher export revenues per unit of nickel exported

**What Indonesia lost (or gave away):**
- Control over who built the processing infrastructure — Chinese companies moved fastest
- Environmental costs: RKEF and HPAL plants have significant waste stream, water, and energy footprints; Morowali has faced deforestation and pollution controversies
- Strategic dependency on Chinese processing expertise and capital

**The bottom line:** Indonesia's export ban was strategically rational from a resource perspective but was captured by Chinese industrial capital because no Western mining major was willing to commit capital fast enough. **Indonesia got the tax revenues; China got the strategic value.**

## VI. The Norilsk Problem: Sanctioned Class 1 at Scale

Russia's Norilsk Nickel (Nornickel) produces approximately 190,000–200,000 tonnes of refined Class 1 nickel annually — roughly 7–9% of global production, but a much larger share of high-purity Class 1 available to Western battery makers.

Norilsk Nickel is not formally sanctioned at the entity level by the US (as of mid-2026), but:
- Western companies avoid Russian supply chains due to secondary sanctions risk
- LME delisted Russian nickel warehouse receipts as an acceptable delivery instrument in 2022 (later walked back, then contested)
- Western battery supply chain commitments (EU Battery Regulation, US IRA battery sourcing requirements) are explicitly designed to exclude Russian-origin materials

**The consequence:** ~190,000+ tonnes of high-quality Class 1 nickel is functionally excluded from the Western supply chain — tightening the available pool of battery-grade material from non-Chinese-controlled sources. This makes Canadian, Australian, and Finnish nickel operations (all high-cost Class 1) strategically more important even as they are economically marginal compared to Indonesian Class 2.

**Nornickel's response:** Nornickel has actively developed relationships with Chinese offtake customers, pricing contracts in renminbi, and routing supply through Chinese processors. The sanctions have pushed Russia's critical mineral output further into the Chinese supply chain.

## VII. The Sodium-Ion Wildcard — The Disruption That Makes Nickel Optional

The deepest risk to nickel's strategic premium is a battery chemistry that doesn't require nickel, cobalt, lithium, or graphite in meaningful quantities: **sodium-ion (Na-ion).**

### The Chemistry

Sodium-ion batteries replace lithium with sodium as the charge carrier. Sodium is:
- 1,000× more abundant in the earth's crust than lithium
- Geographically distributed (sodium is everywhere; no DRC, no Atacama, no rare earth monopoly)
- Cheaper to source

Sodium-ion cathodes use **Prussian blue analogs** or **layered oxide materials** — which can be made without nickel, cobalt, or manganese in meaningful quantities. Anodes can use hard carbon (made from abundant organic precursors) rather than graphite.

**What Na-ion lacks:** Energy density. Current Na-ion cells are approximately 100–160 Wh/kg vs. 200–300 Wh/kg for NMC/LFP. This means heavier batteries for equivalent range — acceptable for certain applications, disqualifying for others.

### Commercial Deployment (2024–2026)

**CATL** — the world's largest battery maker — announced Na-ion commercial deployment in 2023 and is integrating Na-ion cells into hybrid packs (first generation EV models) where energy density matters less and cost/safety advantages dominate. Chery's iCAR 03 launched in 2023 with CATL Na-ion cells. CATL's second-generation Na-ion has announced 200 Wh/kg — closing the gap with LFP.

**BYD** announced its own Na-ion battery development program (sodium-ion "seagull" models targeting under-$10,000 EVs in emerging markets).

**HiNa Battery, Farasis, SVOLT:** Chinese battery makers building Na-ion production lines, targeting short-range urban EVs and energy storage systems.

**The timeline:** Na-ion is a real commercial product today, not a research story. It will not displace NMC 811 in premium long-range EVs by 2030 — the energy density gap is too large. But it will:
- Capture urban/short-range EV market share in Asia, particularly for price-sensitive buyers
- Dominate stationary energy storage (where weight doesn't matter) — eliminating lithium/nickel/cobalt from the fastest-growing battery application
- Put a structural floor under raw material cost savings, ensuring LFP and NMC cathode makers price competitively

**The long-run implication for nickel:** If Na-ion reaches 200 Wh/kg at scale, and if stationary storage goes Na-ion, **the battery demand growth story for nickel weakens substantially.** The bull case for battery-grade nickel sulfate depends on premium EVs staying NMC 811+ for 10+ years — a scenario that is less certain than it looked in 2022.

## VIII. Western Responses — Reshoring Nickel

### US Inflation Reduction Act: Battery Sourcing Requirements
The IRA requires battery components and critical minerals to meet sourcing thresholds for EV tax credits to apply. By 2025, 50% of critical mineral value must come from the US or a free trade agreement (FTA) partner. By 2028, this rises to 80%.

Indonesia is **not an FTA partner** with the United States (the US-Indonesia FTA has been negotiated but not ratified as of mid-2026). This means Indonesian nickel processed by Chinese companies does not qualify for IRA credits — even if the nickel ore itself is Indonesian. **IRA is a de facto ban on Chinese-processed Indonesian nickel in US EV supply chains.**

### US Critical Mineral Partnerships
- US-Australia Critical Minerals Investment Partnership: Australia has significant Class 1 nickel deposits (BHP's Nickel West in Western Australia) and qualifies as FTA partner
- US FTA negotiations with Indonesia: ongoing but politically contentious
- Pentagon/DoD critical mineral stockpiling: nickel is now on the National Defense Stockpile list

### European Union: Critical Raw Materials Act (CRMA)
The EU's CRMA (2024) sets strategic autonomy targets — no single third country should supply >65% of any critical mineral. This is directly aimed at Indonesian/Chinese nickel dominance. CRMA prioritizes:
- Permitting acceleration for EU domestic projects
- Supply chain diversification requirements on battery makers
- Strategic partnerships (EU-Canada critical minerals deal; EU-Australia)

**The CRMA problem:** Europe has limited domestic nickel geology. The policy intent is right but the geological reality is constraining. The most viable European response is battery recycling (nickel is 100% recoverable from spent NMC batteries) and Class 1 supply partnerships with Canada, Australia, and Scandinavia (Finland has Terrafame, a major nickel sulfate producer).

### The Reshoring Pipeline
Key projects attempting to build non-Chinese battery-grade nickel capacity:

| Project | Country | Type | Status | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| BHP Nickel West | Australia | Class 1 sulfide | Operating | High-quality but high-cost; BHP paused nickel operations in 2024 due to low prices — a warning signal |
| Talon Metals (Tamarack) | USA (Minnesota) | Class 1 sulfide | Permitting | IRA-eligible; faces environmental permitting challenges |
| Giga Metals (Turnagain) | Canada | Class 1 sulfide | Development | High-grade; permitting timeline 5–7 years |
| Vale Sudbury | Canada | Class 1 sulfide | Operating | Highest quality; highest cost; losing money at current prices |
| Terrafame | Finland | Bioleach laterite | Operating | EU domestic Class 1 production; small scale |

**The competitive problem:** These projects cannot compete on cost with Indonesian HPAL material while nickel prices are depressed by the Indonesian supply surge. BHP's 2024 nickel operations pause — the world's 3rd-largest nickel miner temporarily stopping production — illustrates how price signals are working against Western supply chain investment at precisely the moment geopolitical signals argue for it.

## IX. Investment Framework

### The Structural Setup

Nickel presents a more complex investment thesis than the pure Chinese-chokepoint story of rare earths or cobalt, because it has three simultaneous forces operating:

1. **Short-term bearish (oversupply):** Indonesian HPAL supply surge + Chinese inventory release = structural nickel price depression through 2026-2027. Western Class 1 miners are being priced out.
2. **Medium-term transition (bifurcation):** Battery-grade nickel sulfate develops its own premium pricing vs. LME nickel. Class 1 Western miners with IRA/CRMA-qualifying production become strategically more valuable even as commodity prices stay depressed.
3. **Long-term uncertainty (Na-ion):** If CATL's Na-ion reaches 200 Wh/kg at scale before 2030, the battery nickel demand growth story gets truncated. The 10-year demand curve is much more uncertain than commodity consensus suggests.

### Portfolio Implications

**Scenario 1: Status Quo — Indonesian Glut Continues (Base Case 12–24 months)**
*Bearish:* 
- Vale SA (VALE3.SA) — nickel margin compression; base metals mix mostly iron ore, but nickel writedowns
- BHP (BHP.AX/BHP.L) — nickel operations in loss; forced closures possible
- Panoramic Resources, Nickel Industries (NIC.ASX) — junior Australian miners

*Monitor:*
- Nickel sulfate spot price premium over LME — leading indicator of battery market tightening
- CATL/Samsung SDI capacity additions requiring Class 1 nickel sulfate with IRA sourcing

**Scenario 2: IRA Sourcing Tightens Demand for FTA-Eligible Nickel (18–36 months)**
*Bullish:*
- Vale Canada operations (Sudbury) — premium IRA-eligible Class 1; loses money at spot but commands strategic premium
- Talon Metals (when/if permitted) — domestic US production; unique IRA positioning
- Terrafame (private, EU-listed bonds) — Finnish Class 1; CRMA-eligible
- Battery recyclers: Li-Cycle, Umicore (recovering nickel from spent NMC batteries is IRA/CRMA compliant by definition)

**Scenario 3: Na-ion Inflects (2027–2030 structural)**
*Bearish structural:* Long-cycle nickel development projects (anything with 5+ year timeline to first production — when production starts, the demand curve may have shifted)
*Bullish:* Hard carbon (Na-ion anode material) producers; copper (Na-ion current collector = copper vs. aluminum in lithium batteries); sodium carbonate producers

### The Battery Recycling Hedge (Applies to Entire Series)
This lesson completes the battery supply chain series: lithium, graphite, cobalt, nickel. In every case, battery recycling is the structural long-horizon hedge:
- Recovers all critical minerals with geography independent of mines
- Qualifies for IRA and CRMA compliance (domestic material content)
- Improves economics as EV battery installed base grows
- Requires Western recycling infrastructure (Li-Cycle, Umicore, Redwood Materials) not Chinese

**The recycling thesis:** By 2030, the global EV battery installed base will be large enough that recycled nickel, cobalt, lithium, and graphite could supply 15–25% of battery material demand — from a supply source that China does not control. **Battery recycling is the only supply chain solution that simultaneously addresses all four critical mineral dependencies mapped in this lesson series.**

## XI. Market Connection

The nickel lessons completes the battery supply chain architecture — and the lesson is sobering:

| Supply Chain Step | Western Control | Chinese Control |
|---|---|---|
| Lithium mining | ~30% (Albemarle, SQM, Pilbara) | ~40% via Tianqi, Ganfeng, DRC indirect |
| Nickel mining | ~25% (Vale, BHP, Norilsk-excluded) | ~70% Indonesian HPAL |
| Cobalt mining | ~15% (Glencore) | ~70% DRC |
| Graphite mining | ~5% | ~80%+ |
| Rare earth mining | ~5% (Lynas, MP Materials) | ~90% |
| **Cathode precursor** | **~10%** | **~85%+** |
| **Battery cell manufacturing** | **~8%** | **~78%** (CATL, BYD, CALB, SVOLT) |

The Western battery supply chain exists on paper — through policy mandates, IRA credits, CRMA targets, and strategic partnership agreements. It does not yet exist in commercial-scale physical reality. The gap between policy ambition and industrial reality is the fundamental investment tension for the next 5–10 years.

**The two portfolio positions that sit at this tension:**
1. **Long the reshoring play:** Western critical mineral miners and processors that will capture IRA/CRMA premium pricing even if they are uncompetitive on spot cost — Vale Canada, Terrafame, battery recyclers. Patience required; the premium hasn't fully materialized yet.
2. **Long the substitution play:** Na-ion, LFP, and synthetic graphite companies that make the entire mineral dependency problem less severe by engineering it away. CATL's equity (accessible via Shenzhen Exchange or Hong Kong-listed equivalents) is the blunt instrument; supply chain partners of Na-ion makers are more targeted.

The risk to both positions: the China-West technology decoupling is slower, messier, and more porous than policy mandates suggest — and Chinese processing dominance proves more durable than the policy window implies.
