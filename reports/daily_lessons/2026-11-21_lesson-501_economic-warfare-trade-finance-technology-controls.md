## 1. The Strategic Logic: Why Economic Warfare?

Military deterrence — the threat of physical destruction — is expensive, escalatory, and increasingly ambiguous. Economic coercion offers a different toolkit: persistent, deniable, and capable of degrading adversary capability over years without firing a shot.

The modern great power competition has three economic warfare tracks running simultaneously:

| Track | US Instrument | China Instrument |
|---|---|---|
| **Technology denial** | Export controls, Entity List, Foreign Direct Product Rule | Import substitution, domestic subsidies, counter-controls |
| **Financial isolation** | SWIFT disconnection threat, secondary sanctions, correspondent banking denial | CIPS (Cross-border Interbank Payment System), CNY settlement, digital yuan |
| **Investment screening** | CFIUS (inbound), outbound investment controls | FDI screening, tech transfer restrictions, "unreliable entities" list |

These three tracks are not new. What is new is the **intensity, breadth, and explicit weaponization** that began with the first Trump administration (2018 tariffs, Huawei Entity List designation), accelerated under Biden (October 2022 semiconductor controls), and has entered a confused but volatile phase under Trump II.

## 2. The Export Control Architecture — How the US Restricts Technology

### The Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS)

The US Commerce Department's BIS administers the **Export Administration Regulations (EAR)** — a system of licenses and restrictions governing what technology can be exported to whom. The key instruments:

**The Entity List:** Companies designated as acting contrary to US national security or foreign policy interests. Exports to listed entities require a license, which is presumed denied for Chinese military-civil fusion companies. As of late 2024, the December 2024 rule added 140 entities. Then: near-total pause.

**The Foreign Direct Product Rule (FDPR):** The most powerful and controversial extension of US jurisdiction. If a foreign-made product uses US-origin technology, software, or equipment at any point in its production, the US claims jurisdiction over its export. This allowed Washington to cut Huawei off from TSMC (Taiwan) even though TSMC is not a US company. This is the tool that most enrages Beijing — it asserts extraterritorial reach over global supply chains.

**The October 2022 Controls (Biden's inflection point):** The single most aggressive export control action since COCOM (the Cold War-era Coordinating Committee for Multilateral Export Controls). The rule:
- Banned export of advanced AI chips (above certain compute thresholds) to China without a license
- Banned export of semiconductor manufacturing equipment capable of producing leading-edge chips
- Extended the FDPR to cover any chip made using US equipment, software, or IP
- Added a novel "US persons" rule prohibiting American nationals from supporting Chinese chip fabrication above certain nodes — effectively making it illegal for American engineers to work in Chinese fabs

The October 2022 controls represented a doctrine shift: from restricting specific bad actors to restricting an entire country's capability to develop strategically critical technology.

### The 2026 Inflection: Weaponized Bargaining

As of November 2026, the architecture is under political stress from an unexpected direction:

The Trump II administration suspended export control enforcement related to China for one year following the **Trump-Xi Busan Summit** in late 2025, in exchange for China suspending its newly established counter-controls. BIS moved advanced AI chip licensing from "presumption of denial" to **case-by-case review** in January 2026.

That suspension expires approximately **this month**.

The result: a year-long pause has allowed Chinese companies to accelerate domestic alternatives, US chipmakers to lobby aggressively for access, and both governments to test whether economic warfare can be turned off and on like a faucet. The answer, as we will see, is: not cleanly.

## 3. The Technology Chokepoints — Why Semiconductors Are the Center of Gravity

The semiconductor supply chain has four critical chokepoints where the US (and close allies) hold near-monopoly positions:

**1. EUV Lithography Machines (ASML)**
Extreme Ultraviolet lithography is the only technology that can manufacture chips at or below 7nm. ASML (Netherlands) makes 100% of EUV machines globally. Without EUV, you cannot make a leading-edge logic chip. ASML has been prevented from exporting EUV to China under US pressure since 2019 — without a formal US regulation, through quiet bilateral diplomacy. China has never received a single EUV machine. This is the single most consequential chokepoint in the entire competition.

**2. Electronic Design Automation (EDA) Software**
Chips are designed using software from Synopsys, Cadence, and Mentor Graphics — all US companies. Without EDA tools, you cannot design an advanced chip. This chokepoint is less visible than ASML but equally decisive.

**3. Advanced Semiconductor Equipment**
Applied Materials, Lam Research, KLA (all US), along with Tokyo Electron (Japan) and ASM International (Netherlands), supply the deposition, etch, and inspection equipment that every advanced fab requires. The October 2022 controls restricted exports of the most advanced versions of these tools to China.

**4. High-Bandwidth Memory (HBM)**
AI accelerators (Nvidia H100, etc.) require HBM chips — produced primarily by SK Hynix (South Korea) and Samsung. The December 2024 controls extended restrictions to HBM for China.

**The strategic reality:** China can build a 28nm chip (comparable to what TSMC was making in 2015) entirely with domestic tools. It cannot build a 7nm or below chip without foreign equipment. Its AI chip capability is therefore structurally constrained at the leading edge — the edge that matters for AI training.

**The race:** Huawei's Kirin 9000S chip (reverse-engineered to 7nm using non-EUV techniques) demonstrated in 2023 that China can reach 7nm without EUV — with enormous cost and yield penalties. It is not commercially competitive. But it demonstrated the thesis: given enough time and money, China will develop workarounds to every chokepoint except one. EUV is probably the only durable moat.

## 4. Financial Warfare — The Dollar as a Weapon

### The SWIFT Lever

In February 2022, the US, EU, and UK disconnected Russia's major banks from SWIFT (the Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunication), the messaging system underpinning global payment flows. This was the most aggressive financial warfare action since the Iran sanctions — and it had immediate, devastating effects on the Russian economy.

China watched. And concluded two things simultaneously:
1. The dollar system is an existential vulnerability — if the US can do this to Russia, it can do it to China
2. China is too large to sanction the same way — cutting China from SWIFT would devastate the global economy and trigger the dollar's demise

Both conclusions shaped Beijing's subsequent financial architecture decisions: accelerating CIPS (Chinese cross-border payment system), expanding CNY settlement in bilateral trade, pushing the digital yuan for international use, and stockpiling gold.

### The Sanctions Architecture

The US operates the world's most powerful sanctions system through the **Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC)**. Key instruments:
- **SDN List (Specially Designated Nationals):** Any entity that does business with an SDN is itself at risk of secondary sanctions — a form of financial FDPR
- **Sectoral sanctions:** Target specific industries (Russian energy, Iranian oil)
- **Secondary sanctions:** Punish third-country entities that do business with sanctioned parties — the most extraterritorial and most resented instrument

For China specifically: no major Chinese bank is on the SDN list. Putting ICBC or Bank of China on the SDN list would trigger a global financial crisis. This creates a structural paradox: the US holds a weapon against China that it cannot deploy without destroying the global financial system it benefits from most.

### The Dollar Paradox

Every time the US uses the dollar system as a weapon, it erodes the structural reason others hold dollars. This is not a theoretical risk — it is a measurable trend. Central bank reserve diversification away from the dollar has accelerated since 2022. Gold purchases by central banks hit multi-decade highs in 2023 and 2024. The dollar's share of global reserves has fallen from 71% (2000) to approximately 57% (2026 estimate).

The more aggressively the US weaponizes dollar access, the stronger the incentive for every other country to develop alternatives. This is the core tension in the financial warfare track: the weapon is powerful but self-consuming.

## 5. The Chinese Counter-Architecture

Beijing has not been passive. Its economic counter-measures:

**Export controls as retaliation:** In April 2026, China formally adopted a regulation allowing export controls as countermeasures against foreign entities. It had already restricted gallium, germanium, and graphite exports (critical for semiconductor and defense manufacturing) in 2023-2024. In October 2026, China proposed new controls on 40 strategic technology systems, 23 linked to AI.

**Rare earth leverage:** China controls approximately 85% of global rare earth processing capacity. Rare earths (neodymium, dysprosium, terbium) are essential for EV motors, wind turbines, guided missiles, and advanced sensors. This is China's FDPR equivalent — a chokepoint it can activate against the US defense-industrial base.

**The domestic substitution race:** CATL (batteries), SMIC (chips at 28nm), Huawei (5G, OS), DJI (drones), and dozens of state-backed "national champions" are racing to substitute for US technology across critical sectors. The results are mixed — competitive in some sectors (EVs, solar, drones), years behind in others (leading-edge chips). But the trajectory is compression, not capitulation.

## 6. What the Bargaining Moment Tells Us

The November 2026 expiration of the US-China export control suspension reveals the fundamental structure of economic warfare between great powers:

**It is not a strategy; it is a negotiating lever.** The Trump II administration suspended enforcement not because it changed its view of China's strategic threat, but because US semiconductor companies were losing market share to Samsung and TSMC-alternative suppliers, and the farm belt needed soybean exports resumed. Economic warfare creates costs for the country wielding it as well as the target.

**The escalation ladder is fragile.** Each new round of controls triggers a counter-move that narrows the future policy space. By 2026, the US has exhausted many of its easy options (Huawei, Hikvision, the leading-edge chip ban) and faces diminishing marginal returns on new designations, while China's counter-leverage has grown.

**Technology denial has a time dimension.** The October 2022 controls were designed to set China's AI chip development back by 5-10 years. Whether they achieved that is genuinely contested. China's DeepSeek demonstrated in early 2025 that training efficiency improvements can partially substitute for raw compute. The moat is real but narrowing faster than expected.

## 7. Investment Implications

### Direct — Semiconductor Equipment (Structural Compression)
The companies most exposed to the US-China trade in this space are in an impossible position: they depend on China revenue (20-30% for AMAT, Lam Research, KLA) but face systematic restriction from their most important growth market.

**Directional views:**
- **ASML:** Structural long, but China revenue compression is real. EUV cannot go to China; DUV (older) lithography is next in line for restriction. Watch for the MATCH Act (congressional push for stricter controls) — if passed, DUV exports to China end, ASML loses ~15% of revenue. The stock price at current levels does not fully reflect this downside scenario
- **AMAT, Lam Research, KLA:** Under-appreciated China exposure. Each generates 20-35% of revenue in China. Any expansion of controls to 28nm equipment — likely under MATCH Act pressure — compresses this severely. Structural underweight relative to sector peers with less China exposure
- **US defense electronics (Raytheon, L3Harris, Northrop):** Direct beneficiaries of the export control regime — the domestic defense-industrial base that export controls are designed to protect gains as the walls go up around Chinese competitors

### Indirect — The Substitution Wave
The companies building China's domestic alternatives are not easily investable by Western portfolios. But the proxies are:
- **Capital equipment demand from Chinese fabs:** NAURA Technology, Piotech, ACM Research (US-listed, China operations) — beneficiaries of Beijing's forced substitution spending
- **Japan semiconductor exposure:** Tokyo Electron, Shin-Etsu Chemical — caught between US pressure and China access, but less constrained than US peers on some categories

### Critical Minerals — China's Counter-Chokepoint
- **Lynas Rare Earths (Australia), MP Materials (US):** Structural long — the entire Western strategy for competing with China's rare earth processing chokepoint runs through these two companies. CHIPS Act and Pentagon contracts provide demand floor
- **Germanium and gallium alternatives:** Watch US/EU investment in domestic production of these two materials, where China's export restrictions are most acute for semiconductor manufacturing

### Currency & Sovereign Implications
- **CNH/CNY:** Each escalation round creates short-term RMB pressure (capital flight risk), but China's current account surplus provides a structural floor. Economic warfare escalation = short-term CNH weakness, medium-term range-bound
- **Taiwan Semiconductor (TSMC) exposure:** TSMC is the irreplaceable node in every US export control regime. Any Taiwan contingency scenario makes TSMC simultaneously the most valuable and most at-risk asset in the global economy. The market persistently underprices this duality

## 8. Databricks Angle

**Signal: Economic Coercion Intensity Tracker**

The BIS Entity List is a public dataset updated regularly — a machine-readable record of US economic warfare escalation tempo.

**Pipeline design:**
1. **Scrape BIS Entity List additions** (Federal Register, public CSV) → track monthly addition counts by country, flagging China/Russia/Iran
2. **Tag entity types** (military end-use, AI, semiconductor, telecom, biotech) using NLP on entity descriptions
3. **Correlate with asset price responses:** 30/60/90-day lag analysis of Entity List China additions → AMAT/Lam/KLA stock returns, USD/CNH, Philadelphia Semiconductor Index
4. **Build an "economic warfare escalation index"** combining: Entity List additions + OFAC SDN designations + tariff rate changes + BIS license denial rates

**Expected signal:** Entity List China additions should lead semiconductor equipment underperformance on 30-60 day lag. SDN additions of Chinese entities should lead USD/CNH strengthening on 10-20 day lag (capital flight premium).

**Data sources:**
- BIS Entity List: publicly available at bis.gov (CSV download)
- OFAC SDN List: publicly available at treasury.gov
- Federal Register (regulations.gov) for rule publication dates
- Yahoo Finance / FRED for asset price responses

This is a genuinely novel dataset that most quant shops don't use systematically. The signal frequency is event-driven (not daily), making it suitable for 30-90 day positioning rather than daily trading.

## 9. CEO Portfolio Note

The economic warfare track is the most complex — and least well-priced — dimension of US-China competition. The military balance is debated by defense analysts; the financial sanctions risk is discussed by EM investors; but the technology control architecture and its second-order effects on supply chains, corporate revenue, and national industrial capacity are systematically underweighted in portfolio construction.

**The November 2026 moment is a decision point.** The Trump-Xi suspension expires. Three scenarios:

1. **Extension:** Controls stay suspended, semiconductor equipment stocks recover, US-China trade volumes normalize. Near-term relief, but structural decoupling merely pauses.
2. **Partial escalation:** Controls restored selectively (AI chips, HBM), but equipment makers retain some China access. Sector-specific pressure, not systemic.
3. **Full restoration + MATCH Act:** Comprehensive restrictions expand to 28nm equipment, rare earth counter-controls escalate. AMAT/Lam/KLA face structural revenue loss; rare earth miners surge; risk-off EM move.

**Base case:** Partial escalation. The Trump II pattern is to escalate enough to demonstrate toughness, then bargain down for transactional concessions (soybean access, TikTok deal, etc.). But the structural trajectory — both sides building domestic alternatives, reducing interdependence — continues regardless of the bargaining rhythm.

**Position implication:** Long defense-industrial base and Western rare earth alternatives; underweight China-exposed semiconductor equipment on a 12-month horizon; watch the November 2026 BIS decision as a near-term binary event for the sector.

## 10. Reflection Questions

1. **The FDPR extraterritoriality problem:** The Foreign Direct Product Rule allows the US to control exports from Taiwan, South Korea, and the Netherlands if US technology was used in their production. This is simultaneously the most powerful and most resented US tool. How should a portfolio manager think about the geopolitical risk premium on TSMC stock, given that it is both the enforcement mechanism for US controls and the most targeted company in any Taiwan contingency?

2. **The dollar paradox:** Every time the US uses dollar system access as a weapon, it increases the incentive for adversaries — and neutral countries — to develop alternatives. At what level of dollar reserve-share decline does the financial warfare capability become self-defeating? What would you watch as an early indicator that the threshold is approaching?

3. **Economic warfare has a time dimension:** Export controls designed to set China back 5-10 years on AI chips appear to have been partially circumvented faster than expected (DeepSeek, Kirin 9000S). Does this mean the controls failed? Or did they succeed in buying time for US domestic capability (CHIPS Act fabs, domestic HBM production) to catch up? How do you structure a thesis around a race whose outcome you can't observe in real time?
