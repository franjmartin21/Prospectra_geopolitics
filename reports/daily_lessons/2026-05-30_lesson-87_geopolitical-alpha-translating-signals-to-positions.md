## 1. The Marko Papic Constraint — Why Most Geopolitical Analysis Is Useless For Investing

Marko Papic, author of *Geopolitical Alpha: An Investment Framework for Predicting the Future*, opens with a claim that is jarring but correct: **most geopolitical analysis is useless for investment purposes.**

Not because it's wrong. Because it ignores constraints.

**The core Papic insight:** Policymakers are constrained by political, economic, and institutional realities far more than they are driven by preferences or ideology. Analysis that ignores constraints and focuses on stated preferences generates scenarios that sound important but have near-zero investment utility. The analyst who predicts what leaders *want* to do is not predicting what they *will* do.

**The investor's job:** Identify the constraints binding the most important actors and derive from those constraints what they must do — regardless of what they say. Then determine what that *forced* behavior implies for asset prices.

**Applied to the 2026 Iran crisis:**
- Iran's constraint: Economic devastation from war + oil revenue disruption makes prolonged closure economically catastrophic for the Iranian population and regime
- U.S. constraint: Election-year inflation sensitivity + ally pressure makes permanent Hormuz closure untenable; the ceasefire was structurally predictable
- Israel's constraint: Can escalate militarily but cannot afford full regional war without U.S. cover, which Trump has rationed
- Saudi Arabia's constraint: Wants Hormuz functional; existential interest in oil revenue; mediation role is structurally forced

**Constraint analysis conclusion:** Hormuz full normalization was always more likely than permanent closure. The ceasefire was the structurally forced outcome. What is *not* constrained to normalize: the geopolitical risk premium, the oil insurance premium, the accelerated de-dollarization in Asian energy settlement, and the permanent re-rating of non-Gulf energy supply. These structural consequences survive the ceasefire.

**Rule 1 of geopolitical alpha:** Only invest in what is constrained to happen — not what you fear or hope will happen. Constraint analysis, not scenario analysis, generates the investable thesis.

## 2. The Five-Filter Framework — From Observation to Position

Between observing a geopolitical event and taking a position, there are five mandatory filters. Skip any one and the trade is speculation, not investment.

### Filter 1: Significance Test
*Is this event structurally significant or noise?*

The GPR index at the 99th+ percentile tells you the market is paying attention. But not all events that generate GPR spikes create investable structural change. Ask:
- Does this event change the *rules* governing trade, finance, or energy, or does it change one outcome within existing rules?
- Does it alter alliance relationships — not just strain them, but restructure them?
- Does it create a new equilibrium that is sticky, or does it resolve back to the prior equilibrium?

**Hormuz 2026 Significance Test:** Passes. First successful disruption of the Strait since its establishment as global energy chokepoint. Created new insurance pricing regime. Accelerated Asian energy settlement in yuan. Established that the "Hormuz option" is now a credible, exercised threat — meaning the risk premium survives physical reopening.

### Filter 2: Constraint Analysis
*What are the binding constraints on the most powerful actors, and what does that force them to do?*

Per Papic: ignore stated preferences. Map constraints. Derive forced behavior. Identify which forced behaviors are market-moving and asset-class-specific.

### Filter 3: Asset Class Mapping
*Which specific instruments will be materially re-priced by the forced behavior, and in what direction?*

"Oil goes up" is not a position. "Long 12-month WTI call options because the Hormuz risk premium will compress more slowly than the spot market is pricing, and non-Gulf supply expansion takes 18–24 months to come online" is a position.

This filter forces you to be specific: instrument, direction, and the mechanism connecting event → price.

### Filter 4: Horizon Alignment
*What is the time frame over which the forced behavior unfolds, and can you hold for that long?*

A structural thesis that resolves over 3–5 years requires a position you can hold through multiple quarters of underperformance. A tactical thesis that resolves over 3–6 months requires different sizing and tighter stop conditions.

**Mismatch is fatal:** Taking a structural position with tactical patience (cutting after 2 quarters of drawdown) is the most common way geopolitical alpha is destroyed before it's realized.

### Filter 5: Falsifiability Test
*What specific observable event or data point would prove this thesis wrong?*

Every position requires a falsification condition stated *before* entry. Not a stop-loss price (that's risk management, separate issue) — a falsification condition in the underlying thesis.

**Example:**
- **Thesis:** Non-Gulf energy equities (U.S. shale, Canadian oil sands, Brazilian offshore) are structurally undervalued because the Hormuz risk premium will persist for 3+ years, keeping Gulf supply reliability discounted and non-Gulf reliability premium elevated.
- **Falsification condition:** Hormuz fully normalizes AND Iran-U.S. diplomatic framework is agreed that structurally prevents future closure AND global oil demand growth exceeds non-OPEC supply growth by less than 1mb/d for two consecutive quarters. All three conditions must be met simultaneously.

If you can't write a falsification condition, you don't have a thesis. You have a view.

## 3. Position Sizing Under Geopolitical Regime Uncertainty

Geopolitical positions are different from equity positions in one crucial way: **the uncertainty about timing is irreducible.** You can be right about the direction and still lose if you are wrong about timing. Position sizing must account for this explicitly.

### The Three-Tranche Structure

For every geopolitical thesis, size positions in three tranches:

**Tranche 1 — Core (50% of intended full size):** Entered when Filters 1–5 are all passed. This is the position that survives the full investment horizon regardless of short-term volatility. It is sized to be held for the full thesis duration without forced selling. Rule: If you would sell this tranche in a 20% drawdown, it is too large.

**Tranche 2 — Confirmation (30% of intended full size):** Entered when the first confirming event occurs (a specific observable trigger defined at position initiation). This tranche adds to the core when the thesis begins to be validated by evidence. It is not added speculatively — it requires the trigger to fire.

**Tranche 3 — Resolution (20% of intended full size):** Entered only when the market begins to price the structural change you identified. This is the momentum leg — you are adding as the thesis becomes consensus, capturing the final re-rating.

**The discipline:** Most investors do the reverse. They size small when conviction is high (because uncertainty is uncomfortable), add when the thesis starts working (the crowd piles in), and hold too long when the resolution tranche should be exited. The three-tranche structure inverts this: maximum commitment when conviction is highest (Tranche 1), reduced additions as the trade becomes crowded (Tranche 3 is only 20%).

### Maximum Sector Exposure Guidelines

Based on a diversified long-horizon portfolio:

| Geopolitical Theme | Maximum Single-Theme Exposure | Maximum Single Instrument |
|---|---|---|
| Energy geopolitics (total) | 20% of portfolio | 5% per instrument |
| Defense/security (total) | 15% of portfolio | 5% per instrument |
| Commodity/materials | 15% of portfolio | 5% per instrument |
| EM political risk (FX/bonds) | 10% of portfolio | 3% per instrument |
| Gold/safe haven | 10% of portfolio | Full in single instrument (GLD/physical) |
| Total geopolitical tilts | 40% maximum | — |

**The remaining 60%** is the non-geopolitical portfolio — broad market exposure, domestic equities, investment-grade bonds — which provides the financial stability to hold the 40% geopolitical book through multi-year drawdown periods without forced selling.

### The Kelly Criterion — Adapted for Geopolitics

The Kelly Criterion gives optimal bet sizing as:
`f* = (bp - q) / b`
Where:
- `b` = odds received on the wager (reward/risk ratio)
- `p` = probability of winning
- `q` = probability of losing (1 - p)

For geopolitical positions, practitioners typically use **half-Kelly** because:
1. The probability estimate is genuinely uncertain (we're estimating, not computing)
2. Geopolitical timing is irreducible — even high-conviction theses can take longer than expected
3. Half-Kelly reduces drawdown volatility by ~50% while capturing ~75% of expected return

**Practical application:**
- High conviction Type B thesis (P(correct direction) ~70%, reward/risk ~2:1): Full position = 30-35% of intended maximum → use half-Kelly → 15-17% of max = Tranche 1 size
- Medium conviction thesis (P ~55%, reward/risk ~1.5:1): Full position = 10-12% → half-Kelly → 5-6% = modest Tranche 1
- Low conviction thesis: Do not open a position. Write a monitoring alert instead.

### Why Documentation Matters More Than the Trade

The log serves three functions that transcend any individual position:

1. **Pre-mortem discipline:** The act of writing the falsification condition forces you to confront the ways you're wrong before you're emotionally committed to being right. Most investment mistakes are made before entry, not at exit.

2. **Learning compounding:** A portfolio without a documented track record doesn't compound learning — it just compounds capital (if you're lucky) or loses both capital and learning (if you're not). The log is how the framework improves over time.

3. **Accountability:** The CEO doesn't execute trades. Bolo executes. The log creates explicit accountability — both for the CEO's recommendations and for Bolo's execution. When the thesis is right but the position wasn't taken, that's a different failure than when the position was taken and the thesis was wrong.

## 5. Stop-Loss Architecture — The Structural vs. Tactical Distinction

The worst discipline error in geopolitical investing: applying tactical stop-losses to structural positions.

A **tactical stop-loss** (exit if price falls 10% from entry) appropriate for a 3-month thesis will force you out of a 3-year structural thesis during a temporary counter-move — exactly the period when the patient investor should be adding (Tranche 2).

A **structural stop-loss** is not price-based. It is thesis-based.

### The Two-Level Stop-Loss System

**Level 1 — Thesis Deterioration Warning (review but don't exit):**
Triggered when an observable development materially undermines (but doesn't disprove) the thesis. Action: review the full constraint analysis, reassess the falsification condition, and determine if the thesis remains intact.

**Level 2 — Thesis Invalidation (exit):**
Triggered when the falsification condition is met. This is the exit signal, regardless of whether you're in profit or loss. The exit is driven by logic, not by price.

**Examples for the current Hormuz thesis:**

*Level 1 Warning (review):*
- Iran and U.S. reach a formal diplomatic engagement framework (not yet a deal — but establishes negotiating channel that could lead to structural normalization)
- OPEC+ announces a 1mb/d supply increase specifically targeted at offsetting Hormuz-related disruption
- Oil forward curve shifts from backwardation to contango, signaling market assessment of supply normalization

*Level 2 Invalidation (exit):*
- Formal Iran-U.S. nuclear framework agreement signed, including Hormuz non-disruption guarantee backed by IAEA monitoring
- AND: Non-Gulf oil supply (U.S., Canada, Brazil) increases by 2mb/d+ within 12 months
- AND: Asian LNG re-routing through Cape of Good Hope normalizes within existing shipping economics (insurance premiums return to pre-2026 levels)

**Notice:** The Level 2 condition is hard to meet. That is intentional. A structural Type B thesis should only be exited when there is structural Type B evidence that the regime has reversed — not when the position is down 15% and the quarterly report looks bad.

## 7. The Track Record Discipline — How to Audit the Framework

The investment log is not a journal. It is an auditable track record. The discipline of maintaining it creates the feedback loop that makes the framework improve over time.

### Quarterly Review Protocol

Every 90 days, the CEO conducts a portfolio review against the log with the following questions:

1. **Which theses are being validated by evidence?** (Not by price — evidence of the structural change predicted in the constraint analysis)
2. **Which theses are showing deterioration signals (Level 1 warnings)?**
3. **Which falsification conditions have been triggered that require exit?**
4. **What new structural development in the past 90 days changes the geopolitical regime assessment?**
5. **Are any new constraint-based thesis opportunities now visible that were not previously?**

### The Honest Accounting Rule

Every closed position is assessed on two dimensions, separately:
1. **Was the direction correct?** (Yes/No)
2. **Was the reasoning correct?** (Yes/No)

Four possible outcomes:
- **Right for the right reasons:** Framework validation. Compound the approach.
- **Right for the wrong reasons:** Lucky. Do not compound the approach that generated the call. Diagnose what actually drove the return.
- **Wrong for the right reasons:** Bad luck or timing failure. The framework may be intact. Assess whether the falsification condition was genuinely met or whether you bailed early.
- **Wrong for the wrong reasons:** Framework failure. Root-cause analysis mandatory. Update the framework.

**The most dangerous outcome is "right for the wrong reasons."** A lucky call that gets credited to the framework inflates confidence in a flawed analytical process. This is how hedge funds blow up — not from bad calls, but from over-confidence in calls that happened to work.
