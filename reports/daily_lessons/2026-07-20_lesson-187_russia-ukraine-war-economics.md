## Opening Framing Question

Russia's economy has not collapsed.

Three years of the most comprehensive sanctions regime in history — excluding Russia from SWIFT, capping its oil price, seizing €300 billion in frozen central bank assets, cutting off Western technology exports, and expelling Russia from Western capital markets — has produced a country with an economy that grew above 4% in 2023 and 2024, maintained military spending at approximately 7% of GDP, funded a war consuming an estimated 1,500 artillery shells per day on the Russian side, and continued to earn approximately **€464 million per day** from fossil fuel exports.

At the same time, Ukraine's nominal GDP has recovered to $225.3 billion in 2026 — above its pre-war 2021 level of $200 billion — despite losing approximately 20% of its pre-war territory, suffering physical damage exceeding $195 billion, and becoming the largest recipient of Western financial aid since the post-WWII Marshall Plan.

**Here is the question that every investor with exposure to European assets, energy markets, or emerging market sovereigns must answer:**

*If Russia's war economy is more resilient than the West expected, and Ukraine's war economy has survived against longer odds than anyone predicted — what does the actual financial architecture of this conflict tell us about the limits of economic warfare as a geopolitical tool? And what happens to asset prices when this war ends — in whichever form that ending takes?*

This is not a question about who is "winning." It is a question about understanding what a multi-year conventional war actually does to national economies, to commodity markets, and to the geopolitical investment framework — because those mechanics will recur. The Russia-Ukraine war is not an outlier. It is a template.

## Part I: The Financial Anatomy of Russia's War Economy

### The Wartime Fiscal Architecture

Russia has restructured its federal budget around war in a way that is historically unusual among major powers. In 2026:

| Budget Category | 2026 Allocation | Share of Budget |
|---|---|---|
| National Defense (official) | 12.93 trillion rubles (~$162B) | ~29% |
| Defense + National Security (combined) | ~15.86 trillion rubles (~$198B) | ~38% |
| Social spending (pensions, health, education) | ~13.2 trillion rubles | ~30% |
| All other | ~8.5 trillion rubles | ~20% |
| **Total federal expenditure** | ~**41.5 trillion rubles** | 100% |

**Defense is now the single largest line item in the Russian federal budget** — ahead of social spending for the first time in the post-Soviet era. This is not just a wartime anomaly; it represents a structural reorientation of the Russian fiscal state toward military power.

For comparison: in 2021, Russia's defense spending was approximately 4% of GDP. In 2026, it is estimated at **6.8–7.2% of GDP**, with some analysts suggesting classified spending pushes the true figure higher. The United States, by comparison, spends approximately 3.5% of GDP on defense.

### Why the Economy Didn't Collapse — The Four Mechanisms

The resilience of Russia's war economy rests on four structural mechanisms that Western policymakers systematically underestimated:

**Mechanism 1: The Oil Revenue Floor**
Despite the G7 oil price cap ($60/barrel when introduced; EU lowered to $44.1/barrel in February 2026), Russia has continued to earn substantial fossil fuel revenues. The key reason: **the shadow fleet**. By July 2026, approximately 1,337 tankers operate outside Western insurance and regulatory frameworks, facilitating 65% of Russian seaborne oil exports and transporting an estimated 3.7 million barrels per day. The spread between Russian Urals crude and the global Brent benchmark has widened to over $20/barrel under tightened enforcement — a real cost — but Russia still earns approximately **€156 million per day from seaborne crude alone**, and approximately **€464 million per day from all fossil fuels**.

Even at a $20/barrel discount and substantially below the 2022 energy price spike, Russian fossil fuel revenue is sufficient to fund the war.

**Mechanism 2: The Defense Industrial Mobilization**
Russia has rebuilt its Cold War-era defense industrial capacity at a pace that surprised Western intelligence agencies. Rheinmetall's German artillery factory runs three shifts to produce 155mm shells; Russia's defense industrial complex, by some estimates, now produces more artillery shells per month than all NATO countries combined. This is not efficiency — it is mobilization, achieved through:
- Forced labor in defense plants
- Technology transfer from Iran and North Korea (shells, drones, ballistic missiles)
- Chinese dual-use component imports (the single most consequential sanctions evasion channel)
- Dismantling civilian manufacturing capacity to feed military production

**Mechanism 3: The Wartime Fiscal Stimulus Effect**
Defense spending is Keynesian stimulus, applied at scale. A government spending 7% of GDP on weapons, munitions, and military logistics generates employment, income, and demand — even if the output is economically destructive. Russia's unemployment rate has fallen to a post-Soviet record low (~2.4%) not because the economy is healthy, but because workers are either deployed in the military or absorbed into defense manufacturing. The "sugar rush" of wartime spending created 2023–2024 growth; the hangover is now emerging.

**Mechanism 4: The Pivot to Non-Western Markets**
Russia has successfully redirected energy exports from Europe (which imported roughly 40% of its gas from Russia pre-2022) to India, China, and Turkey, which collectively now absorb approximately 80% of Russian oil exports. This market redirection came at a price discount (the $20/barrel Urals-Brent spread), but it prevented the supply collapse that Western sanctions were designed to trigger.

### The Crack Lines: What Is Actually Breaking in Russia's Economy

The resilience is real, but it is being purchased at an accelerating cost:

**Revenue shortfall**: For the first time since the pandemic, Russia collected less budget revenue in 2025 than planned — actual receipts came in at 36.6 trillion rubles versus the projected 40.3 trillion rubles, a shortfall of 3.7 trillion rubles ($46 billion). This is the beginning of fiscal pressure, not a crisis — but it is a structural trend.

**Inflation and monetary distortion**: Russia's central bank has maintained policy rates at extraordinary highs (16–18%) to contain wartime inflation. The result: civilian borrowing is effectively frozen. Mortgage markets have collapsed for non-military buyers. Consumer credit is contracting. The economy is bifurcated — booming in defense-adjacent sectors, contracting in civilian consumption.

**Demographic cost**: Russia is losing approximately 800–1,200 military personnel per day (killed or seriously wounded), by Ukrainian and Western intelligence estimates. The cumulative human cost after four years represents a demographic shock that will suppress Russian economic potential for a decade. The mobilization has also triggered one of the largest brain drains in modern history — an estimated 500,000–1,000,000 educated Russians (disproportionately young, male, technology-capable) have emigrated since February 2022.

**Technology denial effects, delayed**: The export control architecture — cutting Russia off from ASML lithography machines, advanced semiconductors, Western aviation components — has not collapsed the Russian economy in 2022–2026, but it is quietly degrading Russian manufacturing capability in ways that compound. Russian civilian aircraft cannot be maintained with replacement parts. Russian oil fields cannot be modernized without Western drilling technology. The next generation of Russian oil reserves, particularly Arctic offshore, is essentially inaccessible without Western capital and engineering.

**CEO Assessment: Russia's war economy is not collapsing — it is slowly compressing**. The 2023–2024 growth surge is over; GDP growth is projected at ~1% for 2025–2026 (IMF). The fiscal position is worsening. The technology denial is a slow-motion constraint, not an immediate rupture. Russia can sustain the war for years — but at an accelerating cost to future economic potential that is not visible in current GDP statistics.

## Part II: The Sanctions Architecture — What Worked, What Didn't

### The Design Logic

The Western sanctions response to Russia's February 2022 invasion was the most ambitious economic warfare program since World War II. It combined:
- **Financial exclusion**: SWIFT disconnection for major Russian banks; asset freeze on the Central Bank of Russia (CBR) — approximately €300 billion in frozen reserves
- **Technology denial**: Export controls on semiconductors, aerospace components, manufacturing equipment
- **Energy price cap**: Coordinated G7 cap on Russian oil exports at $60/barrel (insurance and shipping services conditions)
- **Trade prohibition**: EU ban on imports of Russian coal, oil, refined products, steel, and timber
- **Capital market exclusion**: Russian sovereign bonds delisted from Western indices; Russian companies banned from Western exchanges

### The Scorecard

| Sanction | Intended Effect | Actual Effect | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|
| SWIFT disconnection | Cut off Russian banks from dollar/euro settlement | Partial — excluded major banks; workarounds via non-SWIFT channels (SPFS, China's CIPS) | C |
| CBR asset freeze (€300B) | Deny Russia access to foreign reserves | Succeeded in freezing assets; failed to deny use — Russia pivoted to yuan and gold reserves | B |
| Oil price cap ($60→$44.1/bbl) | Reduce Russian oil revenue | Partially successful — created a discount vs Brent; failed to prevent shadow fleet from transporting volume | C+ |
| Technology export controls | Deny military-critical components | Partially successful — battlefield systems degraded; Chinese/Iranian/North Korean supply chains partially compensating | B- |
| European energy decoupling | Reduce Russian leverage; deny gas revenue | Highly successful — EU gas dependence on Russia fell from ~40% to <10% | A |
| Capital market exclusion | Raise Russian borrowing costs | Succeeded — Russia cannot access Western capital markets; forced to rely on domestic market and Chinese financing | A- |

**The meta-lesson from this scorecard: sanctions are most effective when there is no alternative supplier and no substitute technology.** European gas decoupling worked because Russia could not easily re-pipe gas to Asia (pipelines take years to build; LNG liquefaction capacity is finite). Technology export controls work for the highest-end chips (which only ASML/TSMC can produce) but fail for lower-grade components that China, India, or Turkey can supply. Oil sanctions failed because India and China chose to buy.

### The Shadow Fleet: The Decisive Evasion Architecture

The shadow fleet deserves focused analysis because it is the single most consequential sanctions evasion mechanism and the one most likely to recur in future sanction regimes.

A "shadow fleet" tanker is typically:
- 15–25 years old (beyond the useful life of Western-insurance vessels)
- Registered under a flag of convenience (Panama, Gabon, Cameroon)
- Insured by non-Western underwriters (Russian mutual insurers, P&I clubs in Turkey or UAE)
- Operated by a shell company with opaque ownership chains

The EU's 21st sanctions package (June 2026) added over 100 vessels to the blacklist and targeted LNG for the first time — the first time LNG has been explicitly included in the sanctions architecture. France seized a Russian shadow fleet vessel off Brittany in June 2026 in the first physical enforcement action against a designated tanker in European waters. The US Sanctioning Russia Act of 2026 proposes 100% tariffs on buyers of Russian oil transported outside the price cap.

**The enforcement paradox**: designating a tanker prevents it from accessing EU ports and Western insurance — but it can still trade with India, China, and Turkey, which collectively represent the buyers. The sanction only works if the buyers of Russian oil comply with G7 enforcement — and they have explicitly chosen not to. This is the fundamental architecture of a multipolar world applied to economic warfare.

## Part III: Ukraine's War Economy — Resilience, Aid Dependency, and the Reconstruction Horizon

### The Paradox of Ukrainian Economic Survival

Ukraine's economy has survived on terms that defy conventional analysis. Standard economic doctrine would predict severe economic collapse for a country that:
- Lost control of approximately 20% of its pre-war territory
- Sustained over $195 billion in direct physical infrastructure damage
- Lost significant portions of its industrial base (eastern Ukraine — Donbas — contained its heavy industry heartland)
- Suffered a 40%+ GDP contraction in 2022 alone (revised to ~29% in final accounts)

Yet by 2026:
- Nominal GDP reached **$225.3 billion** — above the pre-war 2021 level of $200 billion
- Real GDP (inflation-adjusted) remains approximately **21% below 2021** — the nominal recovery is partly driven by wartime inflation and international aid flows
- IMF projects **2.0% real GDP growth** for 2026
- Unemployment has partially recovered — but remains structurally elevated by pre-war standards

### The Aid Architecture: What is Financing Ukraine's Survival

Ukraine's economic survival is not primarily a function of Ukrainian economic policy. It is a function of Western aid at historically unprecedented peacetime (or quasi-peacetime) scale:

**Cumulative Western aid since 2022 (to July 2026): approximately €267 billion**

| Category | Amount |
|---|---|
| Military assistance (weapons, equipment, training) | ~€134 billion |
| Financial support (budget grants and loans) | ~€118 billion |
| Humanitarian aid | ~€19 billion |

**2026 commitment: international financing exceeding €110 billion committed for 2026–2027**

This aid volume — approximately €33–35 billion per year in financial support — has essentially substituted for Ukraine's fiscal capacity destroyed by the war. Without it, Ukraine's government would have been unable to pay military salaries, pension obligations, civil service, or maintain basic infrastructure.

### The Reconstruction Arithmetic

The reconstruction price tag is now formally estimated at **$588 billion over the next decade** (World Bank / UNDP, February 2026), up from $524 billion in the prior assessment as new damage accumulates. The largest reconstruction needs:

| Sector | Estimated Need |
|---|---|
| Housing | $84 billion |
| Transport infrastructure | $78 billion |
| Energy and extractives | $68 billion |
| Commerce and industry | $64 billion |
| Agriculture | $55 billion |
| Other | $239 billion |

**The investment implication of $588 billion**: this is approximately 2.6 times Ukraine's 2026 nominal GDP. For comparison, the Marshall Plan (1948–1952) provided approximately $173 billion in today's dollars to 17 countries — roughly $10 billion per country. Ukraine's reconstruction need is 3–4 Marshall Plans directed at a single country.

Financing this will require: G7 government grants, EU accession funds, multilateral development bank lending (EBRD, World Bank, EIB), private sector investment (conditional on security guarantees and rule of law), and potentially the mobilization of frozen Russian assets — an estimated €300 billion in CBR reserves still immobilized under G7 sanctions.

## Part IV: The Negotiation Landscape — Where Things Stand in July 2026

### The Timeline of Failed Peace Attempts

The Russia-Ukraine war has now accumulated four years of unsuccessful peace initiatives — a record that is itself analytically significant:

| Date | Initiative | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| March 2022 | Istanbul negotiations (mediated by Turkey and Israel) | Collapsed when Ukraine claimed Russian atrocities at Bucha were revealed; Russia withdrew negotiating team |
| 2022–2024 | Multiple informal backchannel contacts | No formal process materialized |
| January 2026 | Abu Dhabi talks (US-Russia-Ukraine, first formal multilateral) | Prisoner exchange (314 individuals); no territorial progress |
| February 2026 | Geneva talks (Geneva, Switzerland) | Territorial issue formally on agenda for first time; no breakthrough |
| May 9–11, 2026 | Victory Day ceasefire | Both sides declared competing ceasefires; both sides reported violations; no lasting effect |
| July 2026 | Kremlin status | "No immediate prospects for resuming talks" |

### The Structural Sticking Points

Two issues have prevented any ceasefire from converting into a durable peace process, and they are not resolvable through negotiating technique — they reflect incompatible strategic interests:

**Issue 1: Territory**
Russia insists that the entire Donbas region — which Russia has formally annexed (Donetsk, Luhansk, Zaporizhzhia, Kherson oblasts) — be ceded as a condition of any agreement. Ukraine insists it will not unilaterally withdraw troops from any portion of Donbas, and Ukrainian constitutional law (amended in 2019) prohibits any government from ceding sovereign territory. This is not a bargaining position on either side — it is a constitutional constraint on Ukraine and a defined war aim on Russia.

**Issue 2: Security Guarantees**
Ukraine's condition for any ceasefire: legally binding Western security guarantees, ideally NATO membership or a functional equivalent. Ukraine's demand emerges from the Budapest Memorandum precedent — in 1994, Ukraine surrendered its nuclear weapons (the third-largest arsenal in the world at the time) in exchange for Russian, US, and UK security "assurances." Russia violated those assurances in 2014 and again in 2022. A piece of paper is no longer sufficient. Russia cannot accept formal NATO security guarantees for Ukraine, because it defines NATO expansion as an existential threat.

## Part V: Investment Implications — What the War's Economics Mean for Your Portfolio

### The Russia Premium in Current Asset Prices

The Russia-Ukraine war is currently priced into financial markets through several persistent channels:

**1. The European Energy Infrastructure Premium**
The war forced Europe's complete energy restructuring — from Russian pipeline gas to LNG from Qatar, Norway, Algeria, and the US; from Russian coal to Australian and Colombian supply. This restructuring is structurally complete: Europe now has floating LNG terminals, long-term alternative supply contracts, and domestic renewable acceleration. **The energy restructuring premium is now permanent — but it has converted from crisis premium to structural cost.** European energy prices are structurally higher than they were in 2021, and that is now priced into European industrial competitiveness, not into a geopolitical risk premium.

**2. The Defense Spending Structural Baseline**
The war has structurally moved Europe's defense spending floor from 2% to 3.5%+ of GDP. This is a durable investment thesis independent of war outcome (discussed in Lesson 186).

**3. The Russia Discount in Global Commodity Markets**
Russian fossil fuels trade at a >$20/barrel discount to Brent because of sanctions enforcement costs and shadow fleet premiums. Russian fertilizers and agricultural commodities trade at similar discounts. A ceasefire or peace settlement that even partially removed sanctions would release this discount — creating commodity price downside risk in a post-conflict scenario.

**4. The Ukraine Reconstruction Opportunity**
A ceasefire (Scenario A) or settlement (Scenario B) would trigger the most significant infrastructure reconstruction program since post-WWII Europe. $588 billion over 10 years in infrastructure investment creates opportunities in: construction materials, engineering services, telecommunications, energy infrastructure, and financial intermediation (lending into Ukraine's reconstruction).

### The Reconstruction Trade: Building the Ukraine Position

The most asymmetric opportunity in the Russia-Ukraine investment framework is not a current active position — it is a **pre-positioned option on Ukraine's reconstruction**, which activates at any of Scenarios A, B, or C.

The reconstruction investment opportunity has three layers:

**Layer 1 — European engineering and construction primes**: STRABAG (Austria), Vinci (France), Skanska (Sweden), and their Eastern European subsidiaries. These companies have the engineering capacity and are already present in Ukrainian pre-war infrastructure projects. Under a ceasefire or settlement, they receive the largest contracts soonest.

**Layer 2 — Building materials manufacturers**: HeidelbergMaterials (cement), Saint-Gobain (glass and insulation), Wienerberger (bricks). Ukraine's housing sector needs $84 billion in reconstruction — the bulk of which is cement, brick, and structural steel. These are European companies already in Eastern European markets.

**Layer 3 — Financial intermediaries**: The EBRD (European Bank for Reconstruction and Development), EIB, and — importantly — private lenders who underwrite reconstruction financing. OTP Bank (Hungary) and Raiffeisen Bank International are already the largest foreign banks active in Ukraine; they are best positioned to intermediate reconstruction lending.

**CEO Note: No active Ukraine reconstruction position currently.** The thesis activates on ceasefire. The pre-positioning move is to build watchlists and monitor peace process indicators — the ceasefire announcement is the trigger, not the warm-up.

### The Russia Sanctions Unwinding: A Multi-Year Optionality Question

A frequently asked question from investors: should we be positioning for Russian asset recovery as sanctions eventually unwind?

**CEO answer: not yet, and probably not for years.**

The €300 billion in frozen CBR assets is the fulcrum. Western governments cannot return these assets to Russia without a formal peace settlement that includes accountability for war crimes (under current EU and G7 legal frameworks). Even Scenario B (negotiated settlement) is unlikely to trigger rapid asset unfreezing — the political conditions for returning sovereign assets to Russia are, in current Western political environments, nearly impossible to meet without a change of government in Russia.

Moreover, the Russia investment universe has been restructured: Western companies have exited Russia (BP, Shell, Total exited; Western banks have wound down). Re-entry would require not just sanctions removal but legal clearing of asset disputes from 2022–2026 expropriations.

**This is a 2028–2030 optionality question, not a 2026 position.**

## Part VI: The Meta-Lesson — What This War Teaches About Economic Warfare

Russia-Ukraine is the first major test of comprehensive Western economic warfare against a major commodity-exporting power in the multipolar era. The results are instructive for every future conflict scenario:

**1. Sanctions are more effective at imposing costs than at changing behavior.** Russia has paid a real economic price — lower growth, higher inflation, technological degradation, fiscal strain. But the costs have not been sufficient to alter Putin's strategic calculus on the war. Economic coercion works best when the target's leadership values economic welfare over strategic objectives. Autocratic governments can absorb costs that would produce electoral change in democracies.

**2. Commodity exporter resilience is higher than analysts assumed.** A country that produces what the world needs — oil, gas, wheat, fertilizer — can find alternative buyers. The G7 oil price cap was always premised on the assumption that India and China would comply; they did not, and there was no effective enforcement mechanism to make them.

**3. The shadow economy scales faster than enforcement.** The shadow fleet grew from approximately 200 vessels in 2022 to 1,337 by February 2026 in four years. The vessels are not being built — they are old Western-insured tankers that are sold when they age out of Western insurance requirements and recycled into the shadow network. The economics (high freight rates, no insurance overhead, cash transactions) incentivize participation faster than blacklisting can deter it.

**4. Technology denial is the most durable sanction — but works on a 5–10 year horizon.** Russia's inability to obtain EUV lithography machines, advanced avionics, and Western oilfield technology is creating a slow compounding constraint on its modernization. But the effects are visible over a decade, not a fiscal quarter.

**5. The war created a new economic bloc.** Russia-China-Iran-North Korea now constitute a sanctioned goods-and-services network: North Korea provides shells; Iran provides drones; China provides dual-use components; Russia provides oil and gas. This is not an alliance of convenience — it is the early architecture of a sanctions-evasion economy that will outlast this war.

## Key Concepts

- **Wartime fiscal mobilization**: The restructuring of a national budget around military spending, with defense absorbing resources at the expense of civilian investment and consumption. Russia's 38% defense-and-security budget share is a modern example. Historical precedents: US in WWII (40% of GDP), Germany in WWII (50%+).
- **Shadow fleet**: A network of old, non-Western-insured tankers operating outside G7 regulatory frameworks to transport sanctioned goods (oil, commodities). Scale: ~1,337 vessels transporting 65% of Russian seaborne oil exports. Key enabler of Russian sanctions evasion.
- **Urals-Brent spread**: The price differential between Russian Urals crude and North Sea Brent benchmark crude — the observable measure of how much Russia's oil earns per barrel less than global market price due to sanctions. As of mid-2026: >$20/barrel discount.
- **The Budapest Memorandum (1994)**: The security "assurance" memorandum under which Ukraine surrendered its Soviet-era nuclear arsenal in exchange for Russian, US, and UK commitments to respect Ukraine's sovereignty and territorial integrity. Russia violated it in 2014 and 2022; the failure of this mechanism shapes Ukraine's insistence on hard security guarantees in any peace settlement.
- **Frozen conflict**: A state of conflict resolution in which active hostilities cease but no formal peace treaty is signed, territorial control is de facto fixed but not internationally recognized, and the underlying dispute remains unresolved. Examples: Korea (since 1953), Cyprus (since 1974), Nagorno-Karabakh (various phases), Georgia (Abkhazia/South Ossetia since 2008).
- **Reconstruction financing architecture**: The multi-layer structure for funding post-conflict reconstruction — grants (EU, G7), multilateral loans (EBRD, World Bank, EIB), private sector lending, and potentially mobilized frozen assets (Russian CBR reserves). For Ukraine, the $588 billion need over 10 years requires all four layers simultaneously.
- **War premium / peace dividend**: In commodity and equity markets, the price components that reflect active conflict risk (war premium) and would reverse upon conflict resolution (peace dividend). For European energy, the war premium has partially converted to structural cost; a genuine peace settlement would generate a peace dividend in oil prices and a reconstruction opportunity in equities.

## Reflection Questions

1. **The Budapest Memorandum's failure is now the central obstacle to any Ukraine peace settlement.** Ukraine will not accept security assurances (again); it demands binding guarantees. But formal NATO membership is unacceptable to Russia. What alternative security architecture — between "assurances" (worthless) and "NATO membership" (red line) — could conceivably provide Ukraine with sufficient security to agree to a ceasefire? Map the historical precedents: South Korea's US security treaty (1953), West Germany's integration into NATO (1955), Israel's de facto US security guarantee (informal). Which model, if any, is transferable to Ukraine?

2. **Russia earns €464 million per day from fossil fuels despite the most comprehensive energy sanctions ever applied.** The EU has just imposed its 21st sanctions package. At what point does incremental sanctions escalation produce diminishing returns — or even perverse effects (driving more buyers into shadow fleet infrastructure, increasing China-Russia economic integration)? Construct a framework for evaluating when sanctions should be tightened versus when a different pressure mechanism (military aid, diplomatic, economic inducement) becomes more effective per dollar of Western political capital expended.

3. **The Databricks War Resolution Probability Index (WRPI) above combines four data streams (GDELT, ACLED, World Bank, commodity prices).** One significant limitation: all four are lagging or coincident indicators — they measure what has happened, not what is being decided in closed-door negotiations that precede any public announcement. What information sources could serve as *leading* indicators of a peace breakthrough — signals that would be observable in data 1–6 months before a ceasefire announcement? Consider: financial flows (who is buying ruble-denominated assets or Ukrainian debt), diplomatic travel patterns (senior officials visiting mediating capitals), energy market behavior (Russian energy prices narrowing toward global), and military logistics patterns (supply chain changes observable via satellite imagery or shipping data).
