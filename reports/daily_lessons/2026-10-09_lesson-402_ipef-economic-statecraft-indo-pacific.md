## Opening Question

*In the four years after the US withdrew from the Trans-Pacific Partnership in January 2017, eleven remaining members — Japan, Australia, Canada, Mexico, Vietnam, Malaysia, Singapore, and four others — signed the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP) without Washington. Then in 2022, the Biden administration launched the Indo-Pacific Economic Framework (IPEF) as its answer to the vacuum. Five years on, IPEF has no tariff reductions, no market access commitments, and no mechanism to compel compliance. Meanwhile, the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP) — China's regional trade bloc — encompasses 15 countries covering 30% of global GDP, with enforceable rules and actual market access.*

**The question that makes this lesson matter: When the world's largest economy builds a security architecture of genuine strategic depth (AUKUS, Quad, bilateral alliances) but repeatedly fails to match it with a credible economic framework — what does the resulting gap mean for regional alignment, for the companies competing in the Indo-Pacific, and for the portfolios that hold them?**

## 1. The Architecture That Wasn't: From TPP to CPTPP to IPEF

Understanding IPEF requires understanding what it replaced — and why that replacement happened.

**The TPP Logic (2008–2016)**
The Trans-Pacific Partnership was conceived under the Bush administration, negotiated under Obama, and represented the most ambitious trade liberalisation since the WTO's founding. Its strategic logic was explicit in Obama's 2015 statement: "We can't let countries like China write the rules of the global economy." TPP would set high standards for labour, environment, intellectual property, and state-owned enterprise competition — standards designed to be incompatible with Chinese economic practices, effectively creating a rules-based trade club that excluded Beijing.

TPP covered 12 nations and 40% of global GDP. It was the economic pillar of the "pivot to Asia."

**The Collapse (January 2017)**
Donald Trump signed an executive order withdrawing the US from TPP on his third day in office. The stated rationale: TPP would cost American manufacturing jobs. The actual strategic cost: the US surrendered the dominant position it had spent eight years building in the architecture of Indo-Pacific trade.

The eleven remaining members, led by Japan, negotiated the CPTPP — a modified version of TPP with 22 US-specific provisions suspended. The CPTPP took effect in December 2018. The US has never rejoined. In 2026, with Trump having served a second term (2025–present), re-entry remains politically impossible in both parties.

**The RCEP Reality**
While the US was absent, China was building its own architecture. The Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership — ASEAN plus China, Japan, South Korea, Australia, New Zealand — entered into force in January 2022. RCEP is not a deep trade agreement by Western standards: it does not cover services, investment, or intellectual property comprehensively. But it is *enforceable*, it has *tariff reductions*, and it *includes China*.

RCEP covers 15 countries, 2.3 billion people, and roughly 30% of global GDP. For Southeast Asian manufacturers, RCEP's rules of origin provisions created a framework for supply chain integration that explicitly includes Chinese inputs. This is the opposite of what US decoupling policy intends.

**IPEF (May 2022)**
The Biden administration launched the Indo-Pacific Economic Framework in May 2022 with 14 member countries: the US, Australia, Brunei, Fiji, India, Indonesia, Japan, South Korea, Malaysia, New Zealand, Philippines, Singapore, Thailand, Vietnam. Notably absent: China.

IPEF has four pillars:
1. **Trade** — "fair and resilient trade" (no tariff reductions; Congress never authorised them)
2. **Supply chains** — early warning systems, buffer stockpiles, investment in critical sectors
3. **Clean economy** — infrastructure, clean energy standards
4. **Fair economy** — anti-corruption, tax standards

The critical structural weakness: IPEF is an executive agreement, not a treaty. It requires no Congressional approval. This means it also confers no binding commitments — on the US *or* its partners. Countries joined because the US asked them to. They can leave when the US changes its mind, and the US can change its mind without Congressional consequences.

**In November 2023, the Biden administration failed to conclude the IPEF Trade pillar** — effectively the only pillar that involved actual market access — because domestic political constraints made any tariff concessions impossible before the 2024 election.

The Trump administration that took office in January 2025 has shown no interest in IPEF's trade or clean economy pillars, though the supply chain and anti-corruption pillars have been selectively continued under different branding.

## 2. The Strategic Logic of "Security-First, Economics-Second"

The failure of US economic engagement in the Indo-Pacific is not accidental. It reflects a structural tension in American politics.

**The Tension:**
- US foreign policy establishment wants deep economic engagement with Indo-Pacific partners to cement alliances and build an alternative to China-dependent supply chains.
- US domestic politics — left (labour) and right (economic nationalism) — makes any trade agreement that involves tariff reductions on manufactured goods politically toxic.
- Result: The US can offer security commitments (troops, treaties, technology transfer) but cannot offer the economic carrots (market access) that would give partners a genuine stake in the US-led order.

**The Regional Calculus:**
Southeast Asian countries — Vietnam, Indonesia, Malaysia, Thailand — are running the following logic:
- Security: "We want the US Navy present; China's territorial claims in the South China Sea are a genuine threat."
- Economics: "We want to sell to the American consumer market; we also need to maintain supply chain access to Chinese manufacturing and the Chinese consumer market."
- Strategy: "We will not choose. Strategic autonomy — hedging between Washington and Beijing — maximises our leverage."

This is not fence-sitting. It is a rational response to a situation where the US offers security without economics and China offers economics without security.

**The Investment Consequence:**
Companies operating across the Indo-Pacific cannot rely on a clean US/China binary supply chain logic. The "friend-shoring" thesis (Lesson 59) assumed that US policy could effectively redirect supply chains to US-aligned countries. The reality in 2026: ASEAN supply chains are deeply integrated with both Chinese inputs and US-market destination. The friend-shoring thesis has partially played out in specific sectors (semiconductors, EVs, critical minerals) but has not produced the broad manufacturing relocation the policy intended.

## 3. Historical Grounding: America's Track Record in Regional Economic Architecture

The current moment is not novel. The US has repeatedly led on security while lagging on economics in the Pacific.

**1944–1971 (Bretton Woods Success):** US dominance of the post-war economic order created the IMF, World Bank, and GATT (predecessor to WTO). American market access was the glue of the alliance system — Germany, Japan, South Korea all rebuilt their economies by exporting to the US. The economic pillar was real.

**1985 (Plaza Accord):** Japan accepted yen appreciation under US pressure, triggering the asset bubble that burst in 1990. The lesson Asian policymakers drew: deep US economic engagement can be turned into a weapon. Maintain reserves. Maintain capital controls. Do not be entirely dependent on US financial architecture.

**1997–1998 (Asian Financial Crisis):** The IMF — dominated by the US Treasury — imposed pro-cyclical austerity on Indonesia, Thailand, South Korea in exchange for bailouts. The conditions were widely viewed as a US-driven power play, not a neutral technical response. China provided bilateral financial support without conditionality, positioning itself as the regional alternative. This is the moment when Chinese soft power in Southeast Asia began its modern rise.

**2001 (China WTO accession):** The Clinton and Bush administrations actively supported China's entry into the WTO, expecting that economic integration would moderate Chinese political behaviour. Twenty-five years later, that thesis has been officially abandoned. But the supply chain integration built during those 25 years cannot be undone by executive order.

The pattern: US economic engagement in Asia is powerful when it is binding and continuous; when it is contingent on US domestic politics, regional partners hedge accordingly.

## 4. The IPEF Investment Matrix

The failure of IPEF's trade pillar does not mean there are no investable themes. The supply chain pillar has produced real money flows.

### A. Supply Chain Resilience Spending (Real Signal)

IPEF's supply chain pillar includes the "Supply Chain Council" and "Critical Minerals Dialogue." These are soft mechanisms, but they have catalysed actual government investment in critical sector supply chains:

- The CHIPS Act ($52 billion in US semiconductor manufacturing subsidies) is structurally aligned with IPEF supply chain logic even though it predates IPEF.
- The Inflation Reduction Act's domestic content requirements for EVs and clean energy effectively incentivise supply chain relocation away from China.
- IPEF member countries have received US EXIM Bank and DFC (Development Finance Corporation) financing for critical mineral projects, port infrastructure, and clean energy.

**Investable:** DFC-backed infrastructure funds in IPEF member countries; critical mineral developers in Philippines, Indonesia (nickel), Vietnam (rare earths), Australia (lithium, cobalt).

### B. The Vietnam Trade Thesis (Strongest Near-Term Signal)

Vietnam is the clearest IPEF beneficiary — not because of IPEF specifically, but because Vietnam has strategically positioned itself as the preferred alternative to China in electronics manufacturing.

**Why Vietnam:**
- Young, cost-competitive manufacturing workforce (average age 30)
- Geographic proximity to Chinese supply chains (northern Vietnam shares a 1,300km border with China)
- Aggressive FDI attraction: Samsung, Intel, Apple supplier Foxconn, LG Electronics all have major Vietnamese manufacturing operations
- Member of both CPTPP (real tariff reductions) and RCEP — simultaneously positioned in the US-aligned and China-aligned trade blocs
- Bilateral Trade Agreement with the US (2001) predates the current framework; Vietnamese exports to the US have grown 40x since 2001

**The Vietnam investment thesis is not primarily a China-decoupling play; it is a Vietnam-as-manufacturing-hub-for-the-world play.**

**Investable:** Vietnam Manufacturing ETF (VNM — Vietnam-listed stocks); industrial park developers in Vietnam (KBC, Vinh Hoan, Becamex — local listings); upstream: port infrastructure beneficiaries.

### C. Indonesia — The Largest Underinvested Bet in the Indo-Pacific

Indonesia is the fourth most populous country on earth (280 million), the world's largest nickel producer, the largest emerging market economy in Southeast Asia, and has consistently punched below its weight in global portfolio allocations.

**The IPEF angle:** Indonesia joined IPEF but has been the most demanding partner — seeking tariff-free access to the US market for nickel products (the battery supply chain is central to Indonesia's industrialisation strategy) in exchange for regulatory cooperation. The US has been unwilling to provide this. The result: Indonesia's Prabowo government (elected 2024) has pursued a more assertive hedging strategy, deepening economic ties with both China and India while maintaining the US security relationship.

**The investment thesis on Indonesia does not depend on IPEF resolution.** It depends on:
1. Nickel supply: Indonesia has 24% of global nickel reserves; EVs need nickel for battery cathodes; Indonesia's export processing ban (requiring domestic refining) is squeezing global nickel supply
2. Demographic dividend: 40% of population under 30; middle class expanding; consumer market growing at 5%+/year
3. Digital economy: Indonesia's e-commerce (GoTo, Sea Group's Shopee), payments (GoPay), and ride-hailing are the fastest growing in the region

**Directional view:** Long Indonesia (EIDO ETF or direct exposure to IDX Composite via market access), long Indonesian nickel plays (Vale Indonesia, Aneka Tambang), long regional digital economy (Sea Limited/SE — Singapore-listed but Indonesia exposure dominant).

### D. The India Gap

India is the critical IPEF member that has not signed the trade pillar, clean economy pillar, or fair economy pillar. India signed only the supply chain pillar.

This is not surprising. India has never joined a trade agreement that requires liberalising its agricultural sector or reducing industrial tariffs — domestic political constraints prevent it. India's approach to IPEF mirrors its approach to the WTO: participate in governance discussions, resist binding liberalisation.

**Investment implication:** India-US economic integration will advance through investment and technology channels (semiconductors, defence), not trade liberalisation channels. The India thesis (Lesson 34) remains intact and is not materially dependent on IPEF.

### E. The China Counterfactual

The most important IPEF dynamic for investment is: **what is China doing while the US fails to deliver economic engagement?**

China has moved systematically:
1. **RCEP** — the regional trade bloc that includes most IPEF members
2. **Belt and Road Initiative** — infrastructure financing that creates Chinese-anchored supply chains
3. **RMB internationalisation in ASEAN** — bilateral currency swap arrangements reducing dollar dependency for intra-ASEAN trade
4. **Cross-border e-commerce** — Temu, Shein, AliExpress penetrating ASEAN consumer markets more aggressively than any US digital platform
5. **EV exports** — BYD, SAIC, GAC entering ASEAN markets with EVs priced 30–40% below US and Japanese competitors

**The China counterfactual creates investable tension:** The US builds security architecture that protects against Chinese coercion, but China builds economic architecture that deepens interdependence. The gap between these two dynamics is where emerging market volatility lives.

## 6. Databricks Angle

**Dataset:** OECD Trade in Value Added (TiVA) database — tracks where value is actually created in global supply chains, not just where goods are assembled. ASEAN supply chain integration data shows the real China-dependency picture.

**Pipeline idea — "IPEF Gap Tracker":**
1. Ingest DFC and EXIM Bank commitment data (public, USASpending.gov equivalent for development finance)
2. Map commitment geographies to IPEF member countries and critical sectors (critical minerals, clean energy, semiconductors)
3. Cross-reference with UNCTAD FDI inflows data and World Bank procurement data
4. Build a "US economic engagement score" by country: how much actual US money is flowing vs. stated IPEF commitments?
5. Compare to Chinese BRI disbursements in the same countries (AidData at William & Mary tracks this)

**Hypothesis:** Countries with high US security engagement but low US economic engagement scores are the highest-risk ASEAN hedgers — they are most likely to expand Chinese economic ties as a counterweight. These countries also tend to show the highest FX volatility during US-China tension episodes.

**Feature engineering:** "US economic engagement score" / "China BRI disbursement score" → the ratio is a leading indicator for ASEAN country alignment shifts, which in turn predicts sovereign spread movements and currency volatility.

## 7. Reflection Questions

1. **The tariff dilemma:** Domestic political constraints prevent the US from offering ASEAN partners meaningful market access. Yet market access is precisely what ASEAN manufacturing economies need to justify aligning with Washington. Is there a third-order mechanism — outside traditional trade agreements — through which the US can provide economic incentives that are politically viable domestically? (Hint: consider DFC investment guarantees, preferential treatment in US federal procurement, and technology licensing as potential substitutes for tariff reductions.)

2. **The Vietnam hedge:** Vietnam is the most clearly positioned "winner" of US-China decoupling. But Vietnam is also deeply integrated with Chinese supply chains and relies on Chinese inputs for the electronics it assembles for US customers. If the US escalated semiconductor export controls to include *components used in Vietnam-assembled electronics*, how would the Vietnam manufacturing thesis change — and what indicators would you track to get early warning?

3. **The Indonesia choice:** Indonesia has the most leverage of any IPEF member — it has the nickel the world's EV industry needs, a massive consumer market, and strategic position across critical sea lanes. If Indonesia's Prabowo government concluded that China would offer more economically (nickel market access, BRI infrastructure) than the US (only security guarantees), how does that alter the Pacific security architecture the last three lessons have been building? Is there an IPEF deal that prevents that outcome — or is the gap structural?

## 8. CEO Portfolio Note

Four consecutive lessons (399–402) have built the complete Indo-Pacific investment architecture:
- **Security layer** (Lessons 399–401): Japan rearmament, Korea nuclear hedge, AUKUS → defence industrial long thesis
- **Economic layer** (Lesson 402): IPEF failure, RCEP/China economic advance, ASEAN hedging → regional EM complexity, critical minerals, Vietnam manufacturing

The portfolio implication of combining these two layers is important: **the Indo-Pacific is not a simple US-wins-China-loses binary.** The optimal portfolio positioning captures the US security spending (defence primes — already held), the critical mineral supply chain disruption (nickel, rare earths — Indonesia, Philippines, Vietnam exposure), and the ASEAN consumer/digital economy growth (Sea Limited, Grab, GoTo) — *without* making a directional bet on which great power "wins" the region.

Watch DFC Indonesia commitments and US-Indonesia BIT (Bilateral Investment Treaty) negotiations as the leading indicators.
