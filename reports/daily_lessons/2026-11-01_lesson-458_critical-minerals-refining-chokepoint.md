## Opening Question

*Imagine you have all the lithium ore in the world sitting in the ground in Chile, but you cannot build a single EV battery without first sending that ore to a country that has imposed export controls on you. You own the deposit, but they own the process. Who actually controls the supply chain?*

This is not a hypothetical. It is the current state of the global critical minerals market — and it is the upstream constraint on every reshoring thesis discussed in the last four lessons.

---

## 1. The Distinction That Most Analysts Miss: Mining vs. Refining

The popular narrative around critical minerals focuses on *mining* — who owns the deposits. But ownership of ore in the ground is far less strategically valuable than ownership of the **processing and refining infrastructure** that turns ore into battery-grade material, semiconductor-grade chemicals, or rare earth magnets.

**The strategic bottleneck is refining, not mining.**

Here is why: mining is geologically determined (the ore is where it is), but **refining is a learned industrial capability** built over decades. It requires:
- Proprietary hydrometallurgical and pyrometallurgical processes
- Highly trained chemical engineers and process scientists
- Massive capital investment in plant and equipment
- Regulatory approvals for hazardous chemical handling
- Established supply chains for reagents and energy

China spent 30+ years building this capability, deliberately and at scale, while the West deindustrialized. The result is a structural chokepoint.

## 2. The Numbers: How Concentrated Is Processing?

| Mineral | China's Share of Global Refining/Processing |
|---------|-------------------------------------------|
| Lithium (battery-grade) | ~65–70% |
| Cobalt (refined) | ~70–75% |
| Rare Earth Elements (separated) | ~85–90% |
| Natural graphite (battery anode) | ~90–95% |
| Nickel (Class I, battery-grade) | ~35–40% (rising via Indonesia JVs) |
| Gallium & Germanium (semiconductor-grade) | >80% |
| Manganese (battery-grade) | ~90%+ |

Source context: USGS, IEA Critical Minerals Report, Benchmark Mineral Intelligence estimates through 2025.

**The key insight:** Even where Western countries mine the ore — Australian lithium spodumene, Chilean copper concentrate, Congolese cobalt ore — the vast majority flows to China for refining before it enters the global supply chain. Ownership of the mine does not confer control of the supply chain.

## 3. The Historical Precedent: Japan's Oil Shock, 1973

The closest historical parallel is instructive.

In 1973, Arab members of OPEC embargoed oil exports to the US and Netherlands. The US imported only ~17% of its oil from OPEC at the time — but the price shock was global and devastating because **oil is priced in a unified global market**. The embargo demonstrated that dependency on a single export route, even a partial one, creates system-wide vulnerability.

China's refining dominance is structurally similar — but with an important difference: **refining capacity cannot be redirected overnight**. Oil could theoretically be rerouted through other exporters in months. Building a new lithium hydroxide refinery takes 5–7 years and $500M–$1B in capital.

China used this leverage explicitly in 2023, imposing export controls on gallium and germanium (semiconductor inputs), and then in 2024 on graphite exports to the US. These were not full embargoes — they were licensing requirements that created uncertainty and demonstrated the mechanism. The signal was clear: **the chokepoint exists and China is willing to use it.**

## 4. Why the West Didn't Build This Capacity

A common question: if this dependency was this dangerous, why did the West allow it to develop?

The answer is a combination of economic logic, regulatory failure, and strategic blindness:

1. **Cost**: Chinese processing capacity was significantly cheaper due to lower labor costs, lower regulatory compliance costs, lax environmental standards, and government subsidies. Western refiners couldn't compete on price.

2. **"The market will provide"**: Western policymakers assumed that commodity markets would self-correct. If geopolitical risk materialized, prices would rise and capital would flow to new capacity. This ignored the 5–7 year build time and the capital intensity.

3. **Environmental externalities**: Processing rare earths and battery materials generates toxic waste streams. Western communities and regulators rejected facilities that China built without comparable constraints.

4. **Strategic illiteracy**: Defense departments focused on military hardware; economic ministries focused on trade flows. Nobody was responsible for the intersection: the industrial base inputs that made both possible. This is the "nobody watched the supply chain" failure.

**The consequence:** The US has no domestic lithium hydroxide refinery at scale (as of 2026). The EU has one. Australia, the world's largest lithium miner, ships almost all spodumene to China for conversion.

## 5. The Race to Build Western Processing Capacity — Where Do Things Stand?

The policy response has been substantial but slow:

**United States**
- Inflation Reduction Act (2022) included domestic content requirements for battery minerals — creating a direct financial incentive to build US/allied processing
- DoE Loan Programs Office (LPO) committed billions to mineral processing projects
- **Status**: 3–4 lithium hydroxide refineries in various stages of development (Albemarle Bessemer City NC expansion, Livent/Allkem merger projects, Piedmont Lithium). Expected to reach meaningful scale 2027–2030.
- **Gallium/Germanium**: New Mountain Pass rare earth facility (California) expanded; MP Materials ramping downstream processing. Still far from self-sufficient.

**European Union**
- Critical Raw Materials Act (2024): mandated that by 2030, 40% of EU annual consumption of strategic raw materials must come from EU processing
- Lithium Valley projects in Portugal, Czech Republic, Finland
- **Status**: Well behind the 40% target on current timelines

**Australia**
- Strategic geography: close to Japan, South Korea, Taiwan — natural partner for friend-shoring
- Government funding for "midstream" processing on Australian soil to capture more value
- **Status**: Most ambitious of any major mining country on domestic processing; 2–3 lithium hydroxide projects moving toward FID

**Japan/South Korea**
- Largest vulnerability given semiconductor and EV manufacturing dependence
- Japanese government stockpiling programs; JOGMEC (state mining company) investing in overseas processing
- South Korea's battery manufacturers (POSCO, LG Chem) investing directly in Chilean and Australian processing JVs

**The timeline gap:** The most optimistic credible scenario has Western processing capacity reaching 30–40% self-sufficiency in key battery minerals by 2030. For rare earths used in defense applications, 2035 is the more realistic horizon. This is a multi-decade structural dependency.

## 6. China's Strategic Response: Deeper Integration Through Indonesia

While the West builds alternative capacity, China has moved to lock in the next generation of supply.

The key move: **Chinese battery manufacturers (CATL, GEM, Zhejiang Huayou Cobalt) have invested aggressively in Indonesian nickel processing**, through HPAL (High-Pressure Acid Leach) technology that converts low-grade laterite nickel ore into battery-grade nickel sulfate.

Indonesia now accounts for ~40% of global nickel supply, and the majority of Indonesian processing capacity is Chinese-owned or Chinese-JV. This is a deliberate strategic play: as the world transitions from cobalt-heavy to nickel-heavy battery chemistries (NMC 8-1-1 and beyond), China has positioned itself to control the next generation of refining just as it controlled the previous one.

**The investment implication:** Pure-play Indonesian nickel equities (INCO, ANTM) are partially offset by Chinese ownership of downstream processing. The real value capture is concentrated at the processing stage, which is Chinese-controlled.

## 7. Investment Implications — The CEO's Directional View

**The refining chokepoint creates several distinct investment opportunities:**

| Position | Thesis | Timeframe | Key Risk |
|----------|---------|-----------|----------|
| **Long Western midstream processors** (Albemarle, MP Materials, Piedmont, SQM Chile processing expansion) | First movers building Western refining capacity will receive subsidies, long-term supply contracts, and scarcity premiums | 5–10 years | Execution risk; Chinese cost competition if trade tensions ease |
| **Long royalty/streaming companies with diversified mineral exposure** (Wheaton, Royal Gold if they expand into minerals) | Exposure to processing ramp without direct operational risk | 3–7 years | Limited pure-play options currently; most royalty cos are gold/silver focused |
| **Long Australian mining + processing integrators** (Pilbara Minerals, Lynas Rare Earths) | Australia is the most credible Western ally building integrated mine-to-refinery chains | 5–8 years | Chinese competition; capital intensity; permitting delays |
| **Long defense-adjacent rare earth processors** (MP Materials, NioCorp, Energy Fuels) | Defense procurement mandates for domestic sourcing create floor demand regardless of market economics | 3–5 years | Government program risk; subsidy dependency |
| **Cautious on pure mining plays without processing** | Ore without refining capacity faces continued dependency; price risk to the downside if China floods refined markets | — | — |

**The single most important signal to watch:** The IRA domestic content compliance rate for US-sold EVs. If automakers are successfully sourcing battery minerals from IRA-compliant (non-Chinese-processed) supply chains, Western refining capacity is working. If compliance rates stall, the chokepoint remains and the structural thesis is intact.

## 8. The Geopolitical Dimension: Export Controls as a Weapon

Export controls on processed minerals are now an established tool of great-power competition. The sequence to understand:

1. **2019–2021**: US imposes export controls on Huawei, then broad semiconductor restrictions on China
2. **2023**: China retaliates with export licensing requirements on gallium and germanium (semiconductor inputs)
3. **2024**: China expands controls to graphite exports to the US (battery anode material)
4. **2025–2026**: Further restrictions on rare earth magnet precursors

This is a **tit-for-tat escalation** in industrial supply chain weaponization. It is not over. The next likely escalation points: battery-grade lithium hydroxide, cobalt refined chemicals, HPAL nickel.

**The strategic logic for China:** Unlike semiconductors (where the US has genuine technological superiority in design), China's refining dominance is structural and cannot be quickly replicated. Export controls on processed minerals are more durable leverage than equivalent US semiconductor controls, because building new capacity takes 5–7 years and requires capabilities that cannot be simply purchased.

**Portfolio implication:** Allocate to Western refining capacity not as a cyclical trade but as a **structural hedge against escalating supply chain weaponization**. The thesis does not require knowing *when* the next export control hits — only that the trajectory of great-power competition makes it more likely over time than not.
