## Section 2: Thesis Falsification vs. Thesis Modification — The Core Concept

Here is the framework concept that this lesson is designed to build:

**A structural thesis is composed of multiple independent sub-components. When new information arrives, your job is to determine which sub-components are falsified, which are strengthened, and which are unchanged — not to declare the entire thesis valid or invalid.**

This is not intuitive under pressure. Under pressure, the cognitive shortcuts are:
- "Oil fell 20% — sell everything energy-related"
- "The deal might not hold — don't do anything"

Both are wrong. Both abandon the framework in favor of emotional shorthand.

The disciplined version is a component-by-component audit, conducted in real time, against the kill switch rules already specified.

## Section 3: Historical Grounding — When "Done Deals" Don't Hold

The reason you do not immediately sell on a deal announcement is the historical record of Middle Eastern diplomatic agreements.

### Case 1: The 1991 Gulf War Resolution

In August 1990, Iraq invaded Kuwait. Brent crude rose from $18 to $46 in approximately 60 days on fears of supply disruption and regional escalation. The invasion was "resolved" in 100 hours of ground combat in February 1991 — faster than virtually any analyst forecast.

What did the market do before the resolution? It priced resolution risk correctly — Brent had already fallen to approximately $20 by January 1991 as diplomatic and military options crystallized. By the time ground combat began on February 24, the market had largely priced the outcome.

**Investment lesson**: Markets reprice faster than events resolve. The deal announcement is the market repricing event. The actual operational resolution (ships transiting, insurance reinstated, mines cleared) is a weeks-to-months process. These are not the same thing, and confusing them is how investors sell positions the day before they should hold them.

### Case 2: The 2015 JCPOA

In July 2015, the Iran nuclear deal (JCPOA) was signed after years of negotiations. Oil prices, which had already been falling since mid-2014 (OPEC flooding the market), fell another 20% in the weeks following the deal as Iranian supply was anticipated to return.

What happened next? JCPOA implementation was slow, contested, and ultimately short-lived — the US unilaterally withdrew in 2018 under Trump. The "certain resolution" priced in 2015 reversed three years later.

**Investment lesson**: An MOU with conflicting reports between the parties is not the 2015 JCPOA. It is not even close to a signed, verified, implemented agreement. The market is pricing the *probability-weighted expected value* of resolution — not certainty. Your job is to assess whether the market is correctly pricing that probability.

The CEO's read: The market is pricing approximately 65-70% probability of successful implementation — i.e., Brent at $83-86 vs. a fully-open Hormuz (pre-crisis price ~$65-70) and a fully-closed Hormuz ($112+). This seems approximately correct given the deal structure and outstanding uncertainties. It is not obviously mispriced in either direction.

### Case 3: The Ceasefire-to-Peace Gradient

Between 2020 and 2023, the Yemen conflict produced multiple UN-brokered ceasefires. Each one caused brief commodity market reactions. None of the ceasefires became permanent peace.

**Investment lesson**: There is a gradient from ceasefire → framework agreement → MOU → signed treaty → implemented agreement → operational stability. Jumping from "MOU announced" to "structural thesis resolved" is a five-step error. The market often makes this error in the first 24-48 hours. Your framework does not.

## Databricks Angle — Dynamic Conviction Scoring

This lesson reveals a gap in the Phase 2 Position Monitor architecture. The five-layer system (Position Register → Market Data → Thesis Scoring → Kill Switches → Audit Report) tracks whether kill switch rules *trigger*. It does not model *conviction decay* as thesis components are partially falsified.

**New component to add: Layer 3.5 — Conviction Score Engine**

```python
# Conviction scoring model
# Each thesis is decomposed into named components, each scored 0-1

THESIS_COMPONENTS = {
    "P01_ENERGY": {
        "component_A_hormuz_closure": {
            "weight": 0.45,  # % of thesis dependent on Hormuz
            "current_score": 0.55,  # Partially challenged by MOU announcement
            "kill_switch_trigger": False
        },
        "component_B_opec_fracture": {
            "weight": 0.30,
            "current_score": 1.0,  # Unchanged; UAE still out
            "kill_switch_trigger": False
        },
        "component_C_geo_risk_premium": {
            "weight": 0.25,
            "current_score": 0.9,  # Slightly reduced by deal; structurally intact
            "kill_switch_trigger": False
        }
    },
    "P02_GOLD": {
        "fiscal_dominance": {
            "weight": 0.40,
            "current_score": 1.0,  # Warsh neutral → fiscal dominance intact
            "kill_switch_trigger": False
        },
        "dollar_structural_weakness": {
            "weight": 0.30,
            "current_score": 0.95,
            "kill_switch_trigger": False
        },
        "geopolitical_multipolar_premium": {
            "weight": 0.30,
            "current_score": 1.0,  # Reinforced by gold's behavior on deal day
            "kill_switch_trigger": False
        }

def compute_weighted_conviction(position_id):
    """Returns blended conviction score 0-100"""
    components = THESIS_COMPONENTS[position_id]
    weighted_score = sum(
        c["weight"] * c["current_score"] 
        for c in components.values()
```
