## Opening Question

*In February 2026, the United States and Israel launched coordinated strikes on Iran. Oil hit $126/barrel. The Strait of Hormuz partially closed — for the first time in history. China and Iran seized the moment to accelerate settlement of oil trades in RMB. The ceasefire came April 8th, but Hormuz traffic is still 30% below pre-war levels.*

**Here is the question: If the United States is still the most powerful military force on earth — and just proved it by obliterating Iran's nuclear program in six weeks — why are so many countries accelerating their efforts to reduce dependence on the dollar? Why does military dominance no longer automatically translate into monetary dominance?**

Hold that question. The answer is one of the most important frameworks in international political economy.

## I. The Core Theory: Hegemonic Stability

In 1973, economist Charles Kindleberger published *The World in Depression*. His thesis was startling: the Great Depression was not caused primarily by bad monetary policy (though that played a role). It was caused by the **absence of a hegemon willing to maintain the international economic system**.

Britain — the previous hegemon — was exhausted after WWI and couldn't sustain the open global economy it had built. The United States — the rising power — was unwilling to take on systemic responsibilities. No one provided the public goods the system needed: a lender of last resort, open markets during a crisis, a stable reserve currency. The result: cascading bank failures, trade wars, and eventually shooting wars.

**Kindleberger's Law:** Open, stable international economic orders require a single dominant power willing to bear the disproportionate costs of providing global public goods.

This became **Hegemonic Stability Theory (HST)**: the argument that international economic order is stable only when one nation:
1. Provides a reserve currency trusted globally
2. Acts as lender of last resort during crises
3. Maintains open markets even when it would be economically rational to close them
4. Enforces the rules of the system — even against its own allies

The hegemon is, in effect, subsidizing the global order for everyone else. It gets paid back in structural power: seigniorage income, first-mover advantage in institutions, and the ability to shape the rules.

---

## II. The British to American Transition — The Historical Case

The only clean example of a complete hegemonic transition in the modern era is the shift from British to American dominance, approximately 1890–1944.

**What it looked like:**
- 1890s: US GDP overtakes Britain's. Britain remains the financial hegemon; the pound is still the reserve currency; City of London dominates global finance.
- 1914–1918: WWI destroys European wealth. Britain goes from creditor to debtor nation (primarily to the US).
- 1919–1929: The interregnum. Britain can't sustain hegemony. US won't accept it. Neither country provides adequate global public goods. Instability simmers.
- 1929–1933: The collapse. No lender of last resort. No open markets. Smoot-Hawley tariffs. Bank failures cascade globally.
- 1944: Bretton Woods. US formally accepts hegemonic responsibility. Dollar becomes the anchor. World Bank and IMF established. Rules of the game codified.

**The key lesson:** The transition took **50+ years** and ran through **two world wars and a global depression**. It was not smooth. The interregnum — the period when the old hegemon was declining but the new one hadn't stepped up — was the most dangerous period.

**We are living in the interregnum of the 21st century.**

---

## III. Power Transition Theory — The War Risk Variant

HST's cousin is **Power Transition Theory**, developed by A.F.K. Organski in 1958 and later elaborated by Jacek Kugler.

The core claim: **Wars between great powers are most likely not when power is maximally unequal, but when the rising power approaches parity with the dominant power and is dissatisfied with the existing order.**

The logic:
- When the gap is large, the rising power can't win a war — so it doesn't try.
- When the gap closes to near-parity, the rising power calculates it might win — especially if it acts before the gap widens again.
- The dominant power, sensing the closing gap, may consider **preventive war** to lock in its advantage.
- Both sides have incentives for aggression in the "danger zone" of 80–120% parity.

**The Thucydides Trap** (Graham Allison's framing): Of 16 historical cases where a rising power challenged an established hegemon, 12 ended in war. The four exceptions succeeded in avoiding war through deliberate diplomatic management.

**The current picture:**
- China's GDP (PPP) has already surpassed the US.
- China's military spending has grown 10-fold since 2000.
- The US maintains a significant lead in naval power, technology, and alliance network.
- But the gap is closing — and China is explicitly dissatisfied with the existing order.

The US-Iran war (2026) is directly relevant here: the US demonstrated it retains overwhelming military capability in the Middle East theater. But it also consumed political capital, diverted military resources, and — critically — gave China a window to accelerate de-dollarization while the US was distracted. Every American military action that weakens US economic credibility accelerates the transition, even when it demonstrates military strength.

## IV. The Succession Problem — Why This Transition Is Different

The British-American transition had one major advantage: the successor (the US) wanted an open, liberal economic order not radically different from Britain's. American hegemony was costly for America but broadly beneficial for the world system — including most developing countries.

**The US-China transition has no such luck.**

China does not want to sustain an American-designed order. It wants to revise it. Specifically:
- Replace dollar-centric finance with a multipolar system (or a yuan-centric system)
- Replace US-led multilateral institutions with Chinese-influenced alternatives (BRI, AIIB, SCO)
- Replace norms of sovereignty-as-conditional (liberal human rights, democracy promotion) with sovereignty-as-absolute
- Replace US military primacy in the Pacific with a Chinese sphere of influence

The problem: **No one wants Chinese hegemony.** Not Europe, not Japan, not India, not most of ASEAN, not even most of Africa. China lacks the "soft power" infrastructure that made American hegemony tolerable — the cultural appeal, the legal system credibility, the open capital markets.

The result is the most dangerous possible outcome: **an interregnum with no clear successor.** Not a smooth handoff from one order to another, but a prolonged period of contested leadership, fragmented institutions, and competitive disorder.

Kindleberger would recognize it immediately. He'd say: "No one is providing the public goods. Get ready."

## V. What the Interregnum Looks Like — An Investor's Map

History and theory give us a clear picture of what an unmanaged hegemonic interregnum produces. Map this against what we're already seeing:

| Interregnum Characteristic | Historical Pattern | Current Signal |
|---|---|---|
| Reserve currency fragmentation | Multiple competing currencies; no single anchor | Dollar share of reserves: 71% (1999) → ~47% (2026); RMB, Euro, gold rising |
| Trade fragmentation | Competing blocs, rival standards, tariff wars | US-China decoupling; friend-shoring; techno-blocs |
| Institution gridlock | Veto dynamics; parallel institutions | WTO paralysis; US vs. China in UN Security Council; BRICS+ as alternative |
| Military competition | Great power rivalry, proxy wars, arms races | US-China Taiwan risk; Ukraine; Iran war; global defense spending surge |
| Financial instability | No lender of last resort; more frequent crises | IMF constrained; EM debt crises; crypto as exit valve |
| Commodity volatility | Supply disruptions; resource nationalism | Iran-Hormuz crisis; critical mineral nationalism; energy price spikes |

**The interregnum is not a cliff edge — it's a sustained elevation of structural volatility.** For investors, this means the regime of low, stable volatility (2010–2020) is gone. The new baseline has more frequent supply shocks, more frequent financial crises in the periphery, and more persistent geopolitical risk premia.

## VI. Investment Implications — Positioning for the Interregnum

### 1. Defense and Security — Secular Tailwind
Every major power — including historically pacifist ones like Germany and Japan — is rearming. NATO members are finally hitting 2% GDP on defense; many are pushing toward 3%. This is not a cyclical uptick. It's a structural reorientation that will run for a decade.
- **Directional view:** Long defense and aerospace. European defense stocks have been particularly underowned and are now rerated. US primes (Northrop, Raytheon/RTX, L3Harris) benefit from procurement surge. Long-horizon conviction: strong.

### 2. Energy — Volatility as the Investment Thesis
The Iran war proved that supply disruptions are not just risk scenarios; they're recurring facts. Oil at $126/barrel in March 2026. Gas markets dislocated. The Hormuz crisis temporarily took 20% of global oil off the market.
- **Directional view:** Long energy infrastructure (LNG terminals, strategic reserve capacity, midstream). Long uranium (as energy security drives nuclear expansion). Long oil & gas producers with low-cost, politically stable reserves (Canada, Norway, UAE). The structural case for high energy prices is the interregnum itself — no hegemon willing to police supply routes cheaply forever.

### 3. Gold — From Relic to Reserve Asset
Central bank gold buying has been running at multi-decade highs since 2022. China, Russia, Turkey, India, Poland, Czech Republic — all accumulating gold outside the dollar system. This is not speculation; it is sovereign reserve diversification happening in real time.
- **Directional view:** Structurally long gold. Not as an inflation hedge (though it serves that function) but as a hedge against dollar reserve system fragmentation. The thesis: if the dollar's share of reserves continues to fall from 47% toward 35-40%, gold absorbs a significant share of the rebalancing flow. Falsification condition: dollar share stabilizes above 50% for 3+ consecutive years.

### 4. EM Differentiation — Geography Determines Fate
In a fragmented world, "emerging markets" as a monolith becomes meaningless. Some EMs benefit from multipolar competition (commodity exporters courted by multiple blocs); others get caught in the crossfire.
- **Winners in the interregnum:** Countries with strategic resources (copper: Chile, Peru, DRC), countries with geographic leverage (Vietnam, Mexico, India as friend-shoring destinations), countries that can play both sides (Gulf states, UAE, Saudi Arabia).
- **Losers:** Countries heavily indebted in dollars with no commodity buffer, countries forced to pick a side without strategic leverage, countries in active military/political zones.
- **Directional view:** Long commodity-rich EM with strong institutional frameworks. Short EM sovereign debt of countries with dollar refinancing cliffs and no strategic leverage.

### 5. Dollar — Wounded But Not Dead
The dollar's share of reserves has fallen sharply. But there is still no viable single alternative. The euro has a fragmentation problem (no EU treasury, no political union). The RMB has a capital control problem. Gold has a liquidity problem. SDRs have a governance problem.
- **Directional view:** The dollar is not collapsing — it is slowly losing market share in a world of competing alternatives. This means: not betting against the dollar on a 12-month horizon, but positioning for a world where dollar seigniorage is lower, dollar-denominated assets command less of a scarcity premium, and EM FX volatility is higher. A basket view: mildly short USD over a 3-5 year horizon, expressed through commodity currency longs (CAD, AUD, NOK) and selective EM currency positions.

## VII. The Second-Order Question — Does Anyone Provide Stability?

Kindleberger's concern wasn't just who the hegemon was, but whether anyone was providing the public goods. There is a scenario — which we should take seriously — where the transition produces not Chinese hegemony but **collective provision** of stability through reformed multilateral institutions.

The conditions for this scenario:
- G20 or similar body gains real enforcement power
- IMF is recapitalized and given broader EM lender-of-last-resort mandate
- WTO reform successfully resolves the appellate body crisis
- US and China negotiate managed competition rather than unmanaged rivalry

The conditions for the dark scenario:
- No institution fills the vacuum
- Dollar fragmentation accelerates without a replacement
- Military competition produces miscalculation
- Trade fragmentation deepens into permanent bloc division

**Our base case:** The dark scenario is more probable over a 10-year horizon. Not because the actors are irrational, but because the incentive structures favor defection over cooperation. Every rational actor hedges against the dark scenario, and those hedging behaviors (rearming, reserve diversification, supply chain reshoring) collectively accelerate the fragmentation they're designed to protect against. Classic prisoner's dilemma at civilizational scale.

## VIII. Databricks Angle

**Hegemonic Stability Index — A Composite Signal**

This lesson suggests a specific Databricks pipeline:

**Inputs:**
- GDELT: Conflict event counts between major powers (US-China, US-Russia)
- BIS: Dollar share of international reserves (quarterly)
- SIPRI: Defense spending as % of GDP by country
- FRED: Gold price, dollar index (DXY), oil price
- IMF: SDR allocation data, EM reserve composition

**Pipeline logic:**
1. Build a composite "Hegemonic Stress Index" — a weighted score of: (a) reserve currency fragmentation, (b) great power conflict event frequency, (c) defense spending trend, (d) institutional dysfunction proxy (WTO dispute filings)
2. Correlate index movements with asset class returns (gold, defense equities, oil, EM FX)
3. Track whether the stress index predicts regime shifts in volatility (VIX, EM volatility)

**Research question this pipeline answers:** Does rising hegemonic stress (as measured by our index) predict elevated commodity volatility and gold outperformance? If yes, we have a systematic signal for the structural trade positioning described in Section VI.

**Dataset priority:** BIS international reserve composition (quarterly) + SIPRI military expenditure database (annual) + GDELT (daily) — these three together give you most of what you need.

## Market Connection

The Iran war's immediate market impact (oil to $126, gold to multi-year highs, defense stocks +40%) was noise. The signal is the underlying structural shift: the hegemon is fighting wars that weaken its economic credibility, reserve diversification is accelerating at the sovereign level, and no institution exists to prevent the next disruption from being worse.

**The interregnum is not a one-time event. It's the new operating environment.**

Position accordingly: long the stores of value and the security producers, skeptical of the assumed stability that priced assets for the 2010–2020 world, and humble about the timeline — these transitions take decades, not quarters.
