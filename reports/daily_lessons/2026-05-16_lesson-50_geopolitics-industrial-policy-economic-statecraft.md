## Opening Question

> *You are the CEO of a German solar panel manufacturer in 2023. The US just passed the Inflation Reduction Act, offering $369 billion in clean energy subsidies — but with domestic content requirements that effectively demand you build in America to access them. China is selling panels at prices below your manufacturing cost, subsidized by a state that has no requirement to show a profit. The EU announces a response package within weeks. You have one decision: where do you build your next factory, and why?*

Hold that question. Because the same decision is being made simultaneously by hundreds of manufacturers across solar, EVs, semiconductors, steel, batteries, and pharmaceuticals — and the aggregate answer to it is physically rewiring global supply chains in real time.

This is not trade policy. This is economic statecraft: the deliberate use of industrial subsidies, tariffs, and domestic content requirements as instruments of geopolitical competition. And it represents the single most significant structural shift in the global economic order since the founding of the WTO in 1995.

## 1. What Economic Statecraft Is — and Why It Returned

**Economic statecraft** is the use of a state's economic instruments — trade policy, investment screening, subsidies, sanctions, export controls — to achieve geopolitical objectives rather than purely economic efficiency gains.

This is distinct from ordinary trade policy in a crucial way: **the goal is not to maximize national welfare in the economic sense. The goal is to accumulate strategic capabilities, deny adversaries strategic capabilities, and build the domestic political coalitions that sustain the government doing it.**

For approximately thirty years (1989–2019), this logic was suppressed. The Washington Consensus, the WTO framework, and the post-Cold War peace dividend created a genuine — if incomplete — epoch of trade liberalization. States still used economic tools politically, but the dominant ideology said this was inefficient and should be minimized.

**Three shocks killed that consensus:**

1. **China's WTO entry (2001) produced unexpected strategic outcomes.** China used market access not to converge toward liberal economics but to climb the industrial value chain at speed — while maintaining state capitalism, data controls, and technology transfer extraction. The US and EU interpreted this as a systematic violation of the WTO's implicit bargain. The political backlash arrived in 2016 but had been building for a decade.

2. **COVID-19 exposed supply chain fragility as a national security issue (2020).** When PPE, then vaccines, then semiconductors couldn't be obtained at speed because they were manufactured in concentrated geographies (mostly China), governments across the world reached the same conclusion independently: single-point supply chain dependencies are a security risk, not just a business risk. Industrial policy was re-legitimized overnight.

3. **Russia's invasion of Ukraine (2022) weaponized energy interdependence.** European dependence on Russian gas — built over thirty years on the assumption that economic integration would prevent conflict — became an acute vulnerability the day tanks crossed the border. The lesson every government drew: deep trade relationships with potential adversaries are not insurance against conflict. They are leverage *for* conflict.

These three shocks in seven years buried the Washington Consensus. By 2023, the United States, European Union, China, India, Japan, South Korea, and dozens of other states were all running explicit industrial policies. The question was no longer *whether* governments should pick winners — it was *which* instruments to use and *which* sectors to target.

## 2. Historical Grounding: Three Eras of Industrial Policy

Understanding the current wave requires knowing what came before.

**Era 1 — Hamiltonian Protection (1790s–1940s)**

Alexander Hamilton's 1791 *Report on Manufactures* made the foundational case for infant industry protection: free trade benefits established industrial powers; new industrial nations must protect their nascent industries until they can compete globally. This was the dominant US economic doctrine for over a century. The US built the world's largest economy behind protective tariff walls while preaching free trade to others.

Germany under Bismarck, Japan in the Meiji Restoration, and later South Korea and Taiwan all followed the same playbook: protect domestic industry from import competition, subsidize strategic sectors, and climb the value chain before liberalizing.

*The lesson: every major industrial power used industrial policy to become a major industrial power. Free trade as a universal doctrine arrived only after the US had already achieved dominance.*

**Era 2 — The Liberal Order (1945–2019)**

The US, having won World War II as the world's dominant manufacturer and creditor, had no need for protectionism. It built the GATT/WTO system to open global markets — which, as the most productive economy, served its interests. The Washington Consensus exported this framework as the universal model.

Industrial policy persisted in the shadows — US defense spending is the world's largest industrial policy — but the explicit use of subsidies and tariffs for non-defense strategic sectors was ideologically delegitimized in mainstream economic thinking.

*The lesson: the ideological dominance of free trade was always partly the revealed preference of the world's most competitive economy. When that economy felt its competitive position threatened, the ideology shifted accordingly.*

**Era 3 — The New Economic Nationalism (2016–present)**

Trump 1.0's steel tariffs (2018) were the opening shot. But the more consequential development was the Biden administration's IRA (2022) — which married massive subsidies to domestic content requirements in a way that was explicitly protectionist while being legally structured to minimize WTO challenge exposure. Every major economy followed suit.

The current era is characterized by:
- Subsidies targeting sectors deemed "strategic" (semiconductors, clean energy, EVs, pharmaceuticals, AI)
- Tariffs used as both economic protection and political negotiating leverage
- Domestic content requirements that force investment location decisions
- Export controls weaponizing technology as strategic denial
- Investment screening blocking adversary capital from sensitive sectors

## 3. The Three Main Instruments

### Instrument 1 — Subsidies

Direct government support to domestic producers: production tax credits, grants, loans, R&D funding, and procurement preferences.

**The IRA model (2022):** $369 billion over ten years for clean energy — structured as production tax credits (not grants) designed to be WTO-compatible. The domestic content provisions meant foreign manufacturers had to build in America or forgo the credits.

**The strategic logic:** Subsidies don't just lower production costs. They shift the *location* of production. If America subsidizes battery manufacturing at sufficient scale, the factory that would have been built in Germany or South Korea gets built in Tennessee instead. The subsidy is a supply chain relocation tool as much as a competitiveness tool.

**The reversal:** Trump's One Big Beautiful Bill Act (July 2025) repealed most IRA clean energy subsidies including the EV tax credit. This was not an economic decision — EVs became a culture war symbol. The policy reversal created stranded assets for companies that had invested in IRA-eligible facilities, and created an investment uncertainty premium that will take years to clear from US clean energy economics.

*Investment implication: Government subsidy programs are now a source of project finance risk, not just upside. IRA-dependent projects lost their cash flow models when the credits were repealed. Political durability of subsidies must be assessed before committing capital.*

### Instrument 2 — Tariffs

Taxes on imports, raising domestic prices and protecting domestic producers.

**The strategic logic:** Tariffs serve three distinct political objectives that are often conflated but should be separated:

| Objective | Mechanism | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Protect domestic industry | Raise import cost above domestic cost | Steel/aluminum tariffs (2018, 2025) |
| Generate government revenue | Tariff as fiscal instrument | Trump's "reciprocal tariff" framing |
| Coerce trading partner | Use market access as leverage | US-China Phase 1 deal negotiations |

By 2025, the US average effective tariff rate had risen to 7.7% — the highest level since 1947, up from 2.4% in 2024. The household cost was estimated at $1,500–$3,800 per year. GDP impact: approximately -1% from baseline.

**The paradox:** High tariffs protect industries that might otherwise not be competitive. But they also raise input costs for industries downstream. A 25% auto tariff protects US car assembly but raises costs for every manufacturer that uses imported steel, aluminum, and components. The net effect is typically negative for economy-wide efficiency — which is why tariffs as economic policy are poorly regarded by economists — but potentially positive for specific strategic industries, which is why politicians keep using them.

### Instrument 3 — Domestic Content Requirements (DCRs)

Mandates that a percentage of a product's value must be produced domestically to qualify for subsidies, government procurement, or market access.

**The strategic logic:** DCRs are more surgical than tariffs. They don't raise prices for consumers; they shape *where* production investment flows. The IRA's battery component DCRs effectively told Hyundai, Toyota, and Volkswagen: if you want access to the US EV subsidy market, bring your supply chain with you.

The EU's Industrial Alliance Act (IAA, 2025) extended this logic further: mandating that state funding for energy and infrastructure projects use European-made goods or goods from partner countries. This is Fortress Europe logic applied to the green transition.

**DCRs as supply chain architecture:** When every major market (US, EU, China) has domestic content requirements for strategic goods, global supply chains must be *duplicated* to serve each market. The era of the single globally-optimized supply chain for strategic sectors is over. Companies must build parallel supply chains — one for each geopolitical bloc — at significant capital cost.

## 4. Five Live Examples from 2020–2026

### Case 1 — US Semiconductors: The CHIPS Act
The 2022 CHIPS and Science Act committed $52 billion in subsidies to domestic semiconductor manufacturing. Taiwan's TSMC, South Korea's Samsung, and Intel all committed to major US fab investments.

**The strategic logic was explicit:** the US cannot be dependent on Taiwan for 90%+ of advanced logic chip manufacturing when Taiwan is under credible military threat from China. This is industrial policy as insurance against geopolitical catastrophe, not as economic optimization.

*Investment implication: CHIPS Act investments create a structural floor under US semiconductor manufacturing capex regardless of commercial economics. The beneficiaries are equipment makers (ASML, Applied Materials, Lam Research) and materials providers, not just the foundries themselves.*

### Case 2 — China: Made in China 2025 and Beyond
China's 2015 strategic plan identified ten sectors for state-directed dominance: EVs, AI, robotics, aerospace, semiconductors, biopharmaceuticals, rail equipment, power equipment, agricultural machinery, and new materials.

By 2025, China had "won" several of these races. It produces ~60% of global EVs, dominates solar panel manufacturing (80%+ of supply chain), and controls the rare earth processing infrastructure described in Lesson 49. The vehicle: a combination of state-owned enterprise direction, protected domestic market, massive R&D subsidies, and patient capital that does not require near-term returns.

China's 2025 trade-in subsidy program allocated approximately RMB 342 billion in auto-sector subsidies alone — roughly $47 billion. This is not stimulus; it is demand manufacturing for the domestic industries China wants to dominate globally.

*Investment implication: Western companies competing with Chinese state-subsidized producers in EVs, solar, and batteries are not competing on economics. They are competing against a state balance sheet. The only sustainable response is either equivalent state support (the IRA model) or differentiation into segments China is not targeting (premium EVs, specialty solar applications).*

### Case 3 — EU: From Single Market to Strategic Autonomy
The EU's evolution from free trade champion to industrial policy actor is the most ideologically significant shift of the decade. The phrase "strategic autonomy" — meaning EU independence from third-party (especially US and Chinese) dependencies in critical sectors — became the organizing principle of EU trade and industrial policy after 2022.

Key instruments: the Net-Zero Industry Act, Critical Raw Materials Act, EU Chips Act, and the Industrial Alliance Act. Total committed industrial policy spending across these instruments: over €400 billion by 2026.

The political driver: European voters and governments concluded after the gas crisis that deep interdependence with unreliable partners is a security vulnerability. The economic driver: watching the US IRA redirect EU clean energy investment to America made doing nothing politically untenable.

*Investment implication: EU industrial policy is creating a second major subsidy pool for strategic sectors. Companies building in the EU for EU markets now have access to capital structures that substantially de-risk projects. But the EU's procurement-linked domestic content requirements mean non-EU manufacturers must localize to access this market.*

### Case 4 — India: Production-Linked Incentive Schemes
India's Production-Linked Incentive (PLI) schemes, launched 2020–2022, offer cash incentives to manufacturers based on incremental sales from domestic manufacturing across 14 sectors: mobile phones, pharma, medical devices, cars, advanced battery chemistry, specialty steel, textiles, food processing, and others.

Total committed: ₹1.97 trillion (~$24 billion). Results by 2025 included Apple shifting ~18% of iPhone production to India, Samsung major investment, and substantial pharmaceutical manufacturing capacity additions.

**The strategic logic for India is distinct:** unlike the US or EU, India is not trying to protect incumbents. It's trying to attract the supply chain relocation driven by the US-China decoupling. India positioned itself as the neutral manufacturing alternative — neither the US ally required to take sides nor the China risk that companies are diversifying away from.

*Investment implication: India's PLI schemes represent a credible claim on the supply chain relocation trade. The risk: India's infrastructure, regulatory complexity, and labor market depth are genuine constraints on scaling. The opportunity: companies with India manufacturing exposure are positioned for a structural 10–15 year tailwind of supply chain diversification flows.*

### Case 5 — The Tariff War Escalation (2025–2026)
By April 2025, the US had imposed:
- 145% tariffs on most Chinese goods
- 25% tariff on all imported automobiles
- Steel and aluminum tariffs (25%) on virtually all suppliers
- "Reciprocal tariffs" averaging 20%+ on goods from 60+ countries before a 90-day pause

The average US effective tariff rate of 7.7% in 2025 was the highest since 1947. The estimated consumer cost: $1,500–$3,800 per household annually. GDP impact: approximately -1%.

By early 2026, bilateral US-China trade had fallen sharply, driving three parallel supply chain responses: Chinese firms routing exports through third-country intermediaries (Vietnam, Mexico), US companies accelerating nearshoring and friendshoring, and a global surge in bilateral trade deal negotiations as countries sought to secure tariff exemptions through diplomatic alignment.

*Investment implication: The tariff regime created a new geography of manufacturing advantage. Countries that secured or maintained low US tariff rates — particularly in Southeast Asia and Mexico — became preferred FDI destinations. The "tariff premium" embedded in supply chain location decisions will persist even if tariff levels moderate.*

## 5. The Strategic Prisoner's Dilemma

Here is why the new industrial policy era is structurally self-reinforcing:

**If Country A subsidizes strategic industries and Country B does not, Country A gains competitive advantage in those sectors.** Country B faces a choice: respond with its own subsidies (matching) or absorb the loss of those industries.

Every major economy has concluded — after watching China's industrial policy gains — that absorbing the loss is unacceptable for sectors deemed strategic. So they all subsidize. And the result is a global prisoner's dilemma where:

- Every country subsidizes strategic sectors
- The total global subsidy spend rises
- The efficiency losses from globally duplicated production capacity mount
- But no individual country can unilaterally stop, because stopping means ceding the strategic sector to whoever doesn't stop

This dynamic has **no endogenous resolution**. There is no WTO enforcement mechanism powerful enough to restrain it (the WTO dispute system has been effectively paralyzed since 2019). There is no bilateral US-China agreement on industrial policy on the horizon. The subsidies will continue, the supply chains will continue to bifurcate, and the costs will continue to compound.

**The investor's conclusion:** This is not a temporary distortion. The bifurcated, industrial-policy-driven global economy is the medium-term structural condition. Investment frameworks built on the assumption of globally-optimized, efficiency-driven supply chains will systematically mislabel what they are seeing.

## 6. The Investment Framework: Navigating the New Economic Statecraft

### Framework: The Policy-Driven Investment Map

```
SECTOR              DOMINANT INSTRUMENT     INVESTMENT IMPLICATION
Semiconductors      Subsidies (CHIPS)       Long capex suppliers; fab operators face
                    + Export controls       margin pressure from policy uncertainty
                    
EVs / Batteries     Subsidies (IRA/IAA)     Policy reversal risk (as IRA showed);
                    + DCRs + Tariffs        bifurcated supply chains → cost inflation
                    
Solar/Wind          Subsidies + Tariffs     China dominates cost curve; non-China
                                            producers need policy support to survive
                    
Steel/Aluminum      Tariffs (persistent)    Domestic producers insulated; downstream
                                            users face structural cost headwind
                    
Pharma/Biotech      Subsidies (PLI, BARDA)  Geographic diversification premium;
                                            India manufacturing exposure attractive
                    
Defense             Procurement mandates    Long domestic producers in NATO/allied
                                            countries; supply chain localization forces
```

### Five Investable Themes

1. **Long industrial policy beneficiaries in stable jurisdictions** — Companies that receive durable subsidy support in politically stable environments (US defense primes, EU advanced manufacturing, Indian PLI recipients in pharma) have government-backed competitive positions. The risk is policy reversal (as IRA showed); screen for bipartisan support and treaty-level commitments.

2. **Long supply chain duplication capex** — If every major bloc requires domestic or partner-country manufacturing for strategic goods, the world needs multiple sets of factories for the same products. The beneficiaries are industrial real estate, factory automation, and engineering/construction companies serving this build-out.

3. **Short globally-optimized single-supply-chain manufacturers** — Companies whose competitive advantage depended on a single globally-optimized supply chain (high Asia concentration) face structural margin compression as they are forced to duplicate. Consumer electronics and commodity manufacturing are most exposed.

4. **Long "geopolitical neutral" manufacturing hubs** — Vietnam, India, Mexico, and Morocco have become preferred FDI destinations precisely because they are not in the crossfire of US-China economic competition. Diversified EM equity exposure to these countries captures the supply chain relocation trade.

5. **Monitor policy uncertainty as a distinct risk factor** — The IRA repeal demonstrated that industrial policy can be reversed for political reasons unrelated to economic performance. Companies with business models dependent on a single government program carry policy duration risk that standard DCF analysis does not capture. A valuation discount is warranted.

## 7. The Synthesis: Economic Statecraft as the New Normal

Lessons 48, 49, and 50 form a unified framework for understanding the post-globalization economic order:

- **Lesson 48 (Deglobalization):** The integrated global economy is fragmenting into geopolitical blocs. This is structural, not cyclical.
- **Lesson 49 (Resource Nationalism):** States are using their natural resource endowments as strategic leverage, constraining supply chains from the raw materials end.
- **Lesson 50 (Industrial Policy):** States are using subsidies, tariffs, and mandates to control where strategic manufacturing capacity is built, constraining supply chains from the production end.

Together, they describe a world where **both the inputs (resources) and the transformation capacity (manufacturing) are being renationalized**. The post-WWII assumption that the market allocates production most efficiently — and that states should step back and let it — has been definitively rejected by every major power simultaneously.

**The investor who internalizes this framework stops asking "what is the most economically efficient outcome?" and starts asking "what does the geopolitical logic require?" Those are different questions, and they often give different answers.**

## Key Concepts

- **Economic statecraft:** Using economic instruments for geopolitical objectives, not just efficiency maximization
- **Three main instruments:** Subsidies (location pull), tariffs (import cost raise), domestic content requirements (supply chain anchor)
- **Washington Consensus collapse:** Free trade ideology died between 2018 and 2022 under the weight of the China shock, COVID-19, and the Ukraine war
- **The prisoner's dilemma:** Once one major power uses industrial policy, others cannot unilaterally stop — creating self-reinforcing global subsidy escalation
- **Policy reversal risk:** IRA repeal demonstrated that industrial policy is not durable infrastructure — it is politically contingent and can be reversed
- **Supply chain bifurcation:** DCRs + tariffs across multiple blocs force companies to build parallel supply chains for each market, inflating capex permanently

## Market Connection

The new economic statecraft is **not a Trump phenomenon or a Biden phenomenon — it is a structural regime shift** that will outlast any single administration. The EU, China, India, Japan, South Korea, and the UK are all running explicit industrial policies with no intention of stopping. The US has demonstrated it can both build (IRA) and tear down (IRA repeal) industrial policy — which means **political risk is now a core variable in any supply chain investment thesis**, not an exogenous factor to be noted and ignored.

The most important mispricing the market is still making: treating industrial policy as temporary and supply chain globalization as the return state. The evidence from five years of data is the opposite. **Industrial policy is the new equilibrium. Globalization was the interlude.**
