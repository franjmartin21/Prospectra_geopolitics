## 1. The Structural Force Behind the Trend

The China+1 strategy — keeping some manufacturing in China while building real capacity in at least one alternative country — is not new. What *is* new as of 2026 is that it has moved from contingency planning to operating doctrine across a critical mass of multinational manufacturers.

Three events collapsed the timeline:

**2018–2019: The First Tariff Shock.** Section 301 tariffs on Chinese goods created the first large-scale cost disruption to China-centric supply chains. Most companies responded by absorbing costs or routing goods through third countries (tariff circumvention). Very few actually moved production.

**2020–2022: COVID & the Just-in-Time Failure.** The pandemic exposed the fragility of single-source, concentrated supply chains. The just-in-time inventory model — optimized for cost, not resilience — failed catastrophically. Companies began stockpiling inventory and diversifying suppliers. Resilience entered the CFO vocabulary.

**2022–present: The Taiwan Risk Premium.** TSMC's dominance in advanced semiconductors and the intensifying military pressure on Taiwan created a category-one tail risk that boards could no longer ignore. The realization: a Taiwan Strait conflict would not just disrupt semiconductors — it would trigger the single largest shock to global supply chains in modern history. Every company with China exposure had to model that scenario.

By 2026, China+1 is no longer a supply chain strategy. It is **a geopolitical risk management posture**.

## 2. The Three Main Destinations — and Why None of Them is China

### Vietnam: The Electronics Assembly Champion

Vietnam is the most mature China+1 destination for electronics and light manufacturing. The numbers are stark:
- Manufacturing FDI flows reached ~$27.6B in 2025
- H1 2026 manufacturing FDI: ~$10.76B
- Industrial park vacancy in Tier-1 zones: below 3% (effectively full)
- Electronics inspection demand rose 30% YoY in 2025

**Why Vietnam wins on electronics:** proximity to China's supplier ecosystem (most components still come from China), a disciplined manufacturing workforce, improving infrastructure, and a government that has structured its FDI regime around attracting exactly this kind of investment.

**The ceiling:** Vietnam cannot replace China. Its population is roughly 97 million vs. China's 1.4 billion. The labor pool for complex, high-skill manufacturing is limited. Industrial capacity is approaching saturation in the short-term.

**Investment signal:** Vietnamese listed equities (VNM ETF) and industrial real estate plays in the region. Watch FDI approvals data as a leading indicator.

---

### Mexico: The Nearshoring King for North America

Mexico wins on a different logic: proximity to the US market, USMCA rules-of-origin incentives, and speed-to-market. A product made in Mexico can reach US distribution centers in 2–5 days. A product from Vietnam takes 3–4 weeks.

For automotive (still Mexico's industrial core), electronics assembly, and capital-intensive manufacturing where logistics costs matter, Mexico is the dominant nearshoring destination in the Western Hemisphere. Q1 2026 total FDI: ~$23.6B.

**The complication:** The USMCA review cycle creates policy uncertainty. Companies are making 10–15 year capital commitments to Mexican factories while the trade framework governing them is being renegotiated every 6 years. This is a structural risk in the investment thesis.

Additionally, the July 2026 analysis flagged a shift in character: nearshoring to Mexico is now driven by resilience logic rather than pure cost logic — meaning the capex is real, but the return profile is measured differently (insurance premium, not margin expansion).

**Investment signal:** Mexican peso (MXN) as a nearshoring barometer, Mexican industrial REITs and infrastructure plays, US manufacturing equipment exports.

---

### India: The Long-Horizon Bet

India's ambition is to capture China+1 in higher-value manufacturing — electronics (iPhones: ~25–28% of global output now made in India), defense, pharmaceuticals, and semiconductors. Total FDI in 2025: ~$81B.

India wins on demographics (largest labor force in the world), a strengthening digital infrastructure, and a government now explicitly positioning itself as the alternative to China for Western manufacturers.

**The honest constraint:** India lacks China's supplier depth. In China, a factory can source 90% of its components within a 200km radius. In India, many components still need to be imported — often from China. Building that supplier ecosystem takes a decade or more. One UBS analysis put it directly: *"No single economy has the potential to match the scale, size and competitiveness of China's onshore supply chain network."*

**Investment signal:** India manufacturing ETFs (INDY, SMIN), PLI (Production Linked Incentive) scheme beneficiaries — electronics assemblers, pharma API manufacturers, defense equipment makers.

## 3. Why Markets Misprice This Thesis (Back to the Opening Question)

The company that moved 20% of production to Vietnam and saw its stock rise — what went wrong?

**Markets priced the announcement, not the execution.** Supply chain diversification is structurally complex and capital-intensive. The actual challenges:

1. **Component dependencies don't move.** The factory moved to Vietnam, but the components still come from Chinese suppliers. You've diversified assembly geography but not supply chain exposure.

2. **Productivity ramp takes years.** A new factory in Vietnam operates at 60–70% of China productivity for 2–3 years while the workforce is trained and processes are debugged. Margins fall before they improve.

3. **Fixed cost duplication.** Running two supply chains is inherently more expensive than running one. Unless the volume in the new location is large enough to achieve economies of scale, unit costs rise.

4. **The China factory doesn't close.** For most multinationals, exiting China entirely isn't feasible — China is also a major *customer*, not just a producer. So the China factory stays open at reduced utilization, generating idle capacity costs.

The satisfaction data from the 2026 Reshoring Initiative survey captures this precisely: **67% of OEMs that have reshored report being satisfied**, down from 96% in 2025. Execution reality is catching up with strategic intent.

**What this means for investment:** 
- **Short-horizon plays on announcements will be punished.** Markets over-price the announcement; reality arrives 12–18 months later.
- **The durable investment is in the enabling infrastructure**: industrial real estate, logistics networks, power generation, and water infrastructure in the destination countries. These benefit from the flow of capital regardless of whether any individual company's factory succeeds.
- **Be long the destination country's financial system**: banking, insurance, infrastructure bonds in Vietnam, Mexico, and India are long-duration beneficiaries of sustained FDI flows.

## 4. The China Question: Is It Really Losing?

This is the contrarian move that investors must take seriously.

**China is not losing manufacturing.** It is *upgrading* it. As labor-intensive electronics assembly moves to Vietnam, China is moving up the value chain — into advanced manufacturing, robotics, EVs, batteries, and the chemical and material inputs that the new supply chains still depend on.

The 2026 reality: Vietnam and India make the iPhone, but China makes the OLED screen, the advanced NAND flash memory (increasingly), and the precision tooling used in the factory. The supplier depth that took 30 years to build does not relocate in 5.

**The investment implication of this:** Don't be simply "short China, long Vietnam." The more accurate thesis is:
- **Long the enabling inputs for the transition** (Chinese advanced materials, industrial automation)
- **Long the destination country infrastructure** (industrial real estate, logistics, power)
- **Long the complexity premium** — companies that manage multi-node supply chains will command higher margins because fewer competitors can do it

## 6. Databricks Angle

**Dataset:** UN Comtrade bilateral trade data + FDI flows by country (UNCTAD World Investment Report, annual)

**Pipeline Idea:** Build a Supply Chain Shift Detector that monitors:
- YoY changes in bilateral export data (e.g., China → US vs. Vietnam → US for specific HS code categories)
- FDI approval data in Vietnam (MPI), India (DPIIT), and Mexico (Ministry of Economy)
- Industrial park occupancy rates (scraped from CBRE Asia, JLL Southeast Asia reports)

**Signal Output:** A "Supply Chain Realignment Index" by destination country — a composite of FDI inflows, export growth by product category, and infrastructure investment. This becomes the leading indicator for the investment thesis above.

**Feature Engineering:** Lag analysis — how long does it take for a shift in FDI flows to show up in bilateral trade data? If the lag is 18–24 months, FDI becomes a predictive signal for trade rebalancing.

## CEO Portfolio Note

The supply chain realignment theme has a 5–7 year runway. It is not a trade. The durable positions are in destination country infrastructure and the enabling inputs (Chinese advanced materials, industrial automation equipment) that the new supply chains still depend on. Avoid the single-stock "moving from China" announcement plays — the execution gap between intent and margin delivery is 18–24 months and markets consistently over-price the announcement.
