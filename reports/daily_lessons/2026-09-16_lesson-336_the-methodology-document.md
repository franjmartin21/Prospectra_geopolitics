## I. What a Methodology Document Is and Is Not

Before building one, define the target.

**What it is not:**

- A list of data sources with a paragraph about "our proprietary analytical process"
- A pitch deck appendix that says "we use GDELT, FRED, and Bloomberg to generate geopolitical risk scores"
- A marketing document that leads with testimonials and ends with a call to action
- A white paper that explains geopolitical theory without connecting it to the specific outputs Prospectra produces

Each of these may be useful in other contexts. None of them constitute a methodology document. A sophisticated institutional allocator who has seen genuine methodology documentation — from a quantitative hedge fund, a credit rating agency, or a sovereign risk consultant — will recognize the difference immediately. Presenting a marketing document in the format of a methodology document is worse than presenting no methodology document at all. It signals that you do not know what institutional credibility requires.

**What it is:**

A methodology document is a replicable-process specification that enables an informed reader to:

1. Understand precisely what inputs the process uses (data sources, update frequency, geographic coverage, known gaps)
2. Understand precisely what transformation is applied to those inputs (the GRI scoring formula, weighting rationale, calibration mechanism)
3. Understand precisely what outputs are produced (country scores, sector signals, investment implications) and what they represent (not claims of predictive accuracy — a credible framework is honest about what its outputs mean and don't mean)
4. Evaluate the calibration history (how the model's outputs have compared to outcomes over the available track record)
5. Identify the known limitations and edge cases where the methodology is least reliable

A methodology document that satisfies these five requirements is one that a skeptical allocator can read, question, and — critically — reference in their own investment committee documentation when they recommend Prospectra. That last point is the commercial pivot: an institutional allocator who adds Prospectra to their intelligence stack does not just want to believe it is rigorous. They need to be able to demonstrate to their own investment committee that they did due diligence before paying $6,000–$14,000 per year for it. The methodology document is what makes that demonstration possible.

## II. The Historical Precedent — How Analytical Frameworks Earn Institutional Trust

The challenge Prospectra faces is not new. Every analytical framework that has achieved institutional adoption has solved the same credibility problem. Three examples establish the pattern.

### Example 1 — The Credit Rating Agency Methodology (1909–present)

When John Moody published his *Manual of Railroad Securities* in 1909 and introduced bond ratings, the challenge was identical to Prospectra's: how does a skeptical institutional investor know that "Aaa" means something precise rather than "Moody thinks this bond is good"?

Moody's answer was methodological disclosure. The rating process was specified in enough detail that a trained analyst could understand why a bond received a particular rating — not reproduce the rating themselves, but understand the logic well enough to challenge it if the analysis was wrong. This methodological transparency was the foundation of institutional trust, not accuracy. Rating agencies are wrong regularly. But their methodology is documented precisely enough that when they are wrong, the error can be traced, the gap identified, and the methodology updated. That auditability is what institutional adoption requires.

**The lesson for Prospectra:** Institutional credibility does not require being right. It requires being documented and auditable enough that the error, when it occurs, can be understood and improved upon.

### Example 2 — The Factor Model Revolution in Quantitative Finance (1976–2000)

When Stephen Ross published Arbitrage Pricing Theory in 1976, the academic framework was rigorous but not institutionally deployed. The translation from academic framework to institutional use — from "this theory makes sense" to "we allocate capital against this" — required a methodology document: a specification of which factors, measured how, weighted by what process, with what rebalancing logic, and what the expected properties of the output were.

Barra (later MSCI Barra) built an institutional franchise on exactly this: translating quantitative academic frameworks into documented, auditable, calibrated methodologies that institutional risk officers could present to their investment committees. Their documentation specified every parameter, every data source, every calibration cycle. The methodology document was not just a sales artifact — it was the product.

**The lesson for Prospectra:** The methodology document is not ancillary to the product. For institutional buyers, the methodology document *is* the product — or at least its institutional-grade wrapper. A GRI score without a methodology document is a number. A GRI score with a methodology document is a systematic analytical output.

### Example 3 — GDELT's Own Documentation (2013–present)

Prospectra is built in part on GDELT, and GDELT's academic and institutional adoption provides a relevant case study. The GDELT Project achieved institutional credibility not because early-adopting academics simply trusted that it was rigorous, but because the methodology was documented in detail: the CAMEO event coding framework was specified, the update cadence was published, the known limitations (US media over-representation, recency effects in conflict coding) were explicitly acknowledged, and the calibration against known conflict datasets was published.

Academics and analysts who use GDELT can cite the methodology documentation when they need to defend the choice of data source to a peer reviewer or committee. That citeability is the commercial asset.

**The lesson for Prospectra:** Acknowledging limitations is not weakness — it is credibility. A methodology document that says "our GRI coverage is strongest in countries with English-language media and GDELT reporting density above X events/year; coverage in countries below this threshold should be interpreted with caution" is more credible than one that claims uniform coverage. Institutional buyers do not expect perfection. They expect honest calibration.

### Section 3 — The GRI Scoring Methodology (3 pages)

**Purpose:** Specify the transformation from inputs to outputs with enough precision that a trained analyst can understand the scoring logic and challenge it if the inputs were misapplied.

**Content:**

**3.1 — The GRI Formula**

The Geopolitical Risk Index (GRI) is a composite score on a 0–100 scale, where:
- 0–20 = Stable (low geopolitical risk; no structural disruption signals)
- 21–40 = Elevated (structural tensions detectable; watch for escalation indicators)
- 41–60 = Stressed (active geopolitical disruption; investment implications are live)
- 61–80 = Critical (significant disruption; position sizing should reflect elevated tail risk)
- 81–100 = Crisis (extreme disruption; defensive positioning warranted in exposed asset classes)

The GRI for country *i* at time *t* is calculated as:

```
GRI(i,t) = w₁ × EventIntensity(i,t) 
          + w₂ × ToneScore(i,t) 
          + w₃ × ActorNetwork(i,t)
          + w₄ × EconomicLinkage(i,t)
          + w₅ × RegimeStabilityPrior(i,t)
```

Where:
- **EventIntensity(i,t):** Z-scored event count relative to 12-month rolling baseline for country *i*, winsorized at ±3σ. Prevents outlier news cycles (e.g., a presidential election that generates 10× normal event volume) from dominating the score.
- **ToneScore(i,t):** GDELT GKG average tone for conflict/governance/political themes, scaled 0–100 (0 = maximally negative, 100 = maximally positive). Inverted for GRI: high positive tone → low risk contribution.
- **ActorNetwork(i,t):** A binary indicator (0/1) upgraded to a 0–0.5 gradient in the current build — measures whether primary actors in the event data include state-level antagonists with sanction/military-action history. High actor-network score = geopolitical actors, not just domestic political events.
- **EconomicLinkage(i,t):** A GDP-trade-weighted linkage score capturing the economic exposure of Prospectra's target asset classes to geopolitical disruption in country *i*. An energy crisis in a country that supplies 15% of global LNG is weighted more heavily than the same crisis in a country with no significant commodity export.
- **RegimeStabilityPrior(i,t):** A slowly-decaying Bayesian prior based on Polity V and Freedom House institutional scores, updated quarterly. A country with stable democratic institutions that temporarily scores high on EventIntensity is up-weighted toward reversion; a country with autocratic institutions and high EventIntensity is down-weighted toward persistence.

**3.2 — Weight Calibration**

Current weights: w₁ = 0.30, w₂ = 0.20, w₃ = 0.25, w₄ = 0.15, w₅ = 0.10

Weight rationale: EventIntensity and ActorNetwork carry the largest weights because they are the most real-time signals — they capture what is happening now. ToneScore and EconomicLinkage provide directional context. RegimeStabilityPrior is a stabilizing prior that prevents the model from over-reacting to temporary news spikes.

Weight recalibration is conducted quarterly, using the signal outcome data from `prospectra.signals.outcomes` (Lesson 325). If signal accuracy degrades by >5 percentage points in a trailing quarter, the weight recalibration is triggered ahead of the quarterly schedule.

**3.3 — GRI Delta and Signal Trigger**

The primary investment signal is not the GRI level but the **GRI Delta**: the week-over-week change in the GRI for a country or region, compared to its 6-month rolling standard deviation.

A **Signal** is triggered when:
- GRI Delta ≥ +1.5σ (upward spike: risk increasing faster than baseline variance)
- GRI Level crosses a tier boundary (e.g., from Elevated to Stressed)
- GRI Delta ≥ +1.5σ AND the ActorNetwork component accounts for >40% of the delta (state-level actor involvement in the spike, not just media noise)

### Section 4 — From GRI to Investment Implication (2 pages)

**Purpose:** Specify the translation from a GRI score to a directional investment view, with explicit falsification conditions.

**Content:**

The investment implication is not implied by the GRI score alone — it requires a sector/asset class mapping that specifies which assets are economically exposed to disruption in the flagged country or region.

**The exposure mapping framework:**

| GRI Signal Country/Region | Primary Exposed Asset Classes | Directional View | Logic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Middle East (CAMEO code: SAU, IRN, IRQ) | Brent crude, WTI, energy equities (XLE), EM sovereign spreads | Bullish oil / cautious EM credit | Supply disruption risk; Iran-Saudi tensions historically correlate with 5–8% Brent premium |
| Taiwan Strait (TWN, CHN) | Semiconductor equipment equities (AMAT, LRCX, ASML), Taiwan equities (EWT), USD/TWD | Bearish SOX / USD safe haven | Taiwan = 70% of advanced logic chips; TSMC disruption cannot be hedged in equipment supply chain within 18 months |
| Eastern Europe (UKR, RUS, POL, DEU) | European natural gas (TTF), EUR/USD, EU defense equities | Bullish TTF / bearish EUR / bullish EU defense | Energy infrastructure vulnerability + European growth uncertainty |
| Latin America (ARG, BRA, COL) | EM FX (BRL, COP), sovereign credit spreads | High country specificity required | Political risk transmission in Latin America is idiosyncratic, not regional; GRI delta must be high in specific country, not region |

**The falsification structure — required for every published signal:**

Every Prospectra investment signal must specify, at time of publication:
1. **Directional call:** Asset class, direction (long/short/underweight/overweight), timeframe (6–18 months)
2. **Entry condition:** What level or event the position becomes actionable
3. **Falsification condition:** The specific, observable development that would indicate the thesis is wrong (not just "if the price goes down")
4. **Exit condition:** The observable development that signals the thesis has played out

Example:
> *Signal: Long energy sector (XLE) on MENA GRI spike above 65 (Critical tier). Falsification condition: Iran-Saudi diplomatic back-channel talks confirmed by UN or third-party mediation — this would indicate de-escalation path, reversing the supply disruption premium. Exit: Brent crude exceeds $120/barrel for 3 consecutive weeks (profit target) OR Iran-Saudi normalization announcement (falsification trigger).*

**Calibration notes:** Signals are evaluated on a binary correct/incorrect basis with a 30-day buffer past the stated timeframe. A signal that was directionally correct but whose falsification condition was triggered is marked **falsified** (not incorrect — a falsification is a functioning methodology, not a failure). A signal whose timeframe expired without a clear directional outcome is marked **inconclusive** and excluded from the accuracy rate denominator.

**Known calibration weaknesses:**
1. Commodity signals have the strongest GRI correlation (0.72 correlation with Brent 6-month forward) — energy asset implications are most reliable
2. EM FX signals are noisier — country-specific political risk is harder to systematize than commodity supply chain disruption
3. Duration of signals is the largest source of error — the GRI is better at identifying *that* a geopolitical disruption is occurring than at predicting *when* the market will price it

### Section 6 — Known Limitations and What Prospectra Is Not (1 page)

**Purpose:** Demonstrate analytical honesty — which is itself a credibility signal.

**Content:**
1. **Prospectra is not a forecasting service.** The GRI measures structural conditions, not specific event outcomes. We cannot predict when a conflict will start, which party will win an election, or whether a particular sanction will pass. We can identify when structural conditions make these events more or less probable.
2. **GDELT coverage bias.** English and Western European media is over-represented in GDELT. Events in low-media-density countries (Central Africa, Central Asia, smaller Pacific island states) are systematically under-represented. GRI scores for these countries should be treated as directional, not cardinal.
3. **The 6-month minimum horizon is structural, not arbitrary.** Geopolitical price impacts take months to transmit through supply chains, capital flows, and risk premiums. Prospectra signals are designed for investors with 6–18 month horizons. Using them to make short-duration tactical trades is outside the methodology's intended operating envelope.
4. **The track record has survivorship limits.** The published signal log reflects all signals issued since Prospectra's inception, including wrong calls. There is no selection bias in the accuracy rate calculation. However, the historical record covers [N months], which is insufficient to fully validate the framework across a complete geopolitical cycle. The methodology is well-grounded theoretically; the empirical track record is nascent.

**The directional view:** Research services with documented, auditable methodologies and published calibration histories trade at a significant premium to undifferentiated intelligence newsletters. The methodology document is a multiple-expansion lever, not just a sales tool. For Prospectra, completing and publishing the methodology document should be treated as an infrastructure investment with both commercial and eventual valuation implications.

## Databricks Angle

**Build Priority: The Methodology Document as a Living Databricks Dashboard**

The methodology document should not be a static PDF. It should be a living document backed by the Databricks pipeline, with sections that auto-update as new data arrives.

The sections that can be automated:

```python
# prospectra.methodology.live_calibration
# Auto-updates the Track Record section of the methodology document

from pyspark.sql import functions as F
from pyspark.sql.window import Window

signals_outcomes = spark.table("prospectra.signals.outcomes")

accuracy_summary = signals_outcomes \
    .filter(F.col("outcome").isNotNull()) \
    .filter(F.col("outcome") != "inconclusive") \
    .groupBy(F.date_trunc("quarter", F.col("issue_date")).alias("quarter")) \
    .agg(
        F.count("*").alias("total_signals"),
        F.sum(F.when(F.col("result") == "correct", 1).otherwise(0)).alias("correct"),
        (F.sum(F.when(F.col("result") == "correct", 1).otherwise(0)) / 
         F.count("*")).alias("accuracy_rate")
    )

# Output to methodology_live table
accuracy_summary.write \
    .mode("overwrite") \
    .saveAsTable("prospectra.methodology.live_calibration")
```
