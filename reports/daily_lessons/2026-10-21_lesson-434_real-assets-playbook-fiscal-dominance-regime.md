## The Core Thesis: Why 60/40 Breaks in Fiscal Dominance

For four decades, the classic 60% equity / 40% bond portfolio was built on a single load-bearing assumption: **when stocks fall, bonds rally**. This negative correlation — the ballast that made the portfolio work — rested on a specific monetary regime: central banks that fought inflation aggressively and cut rates in recessions, making bonds the "safe haven" that absorbed equity volatility.

Fiscal dominance destroys that assumption.

When governments force monetary policy to accommodate their debt loads — keeping real rates artificially negative or suppressed — bonds stop being the hedge. They become the source of loss. The correlation between stocks and bonds **turns positive** during inflationary episodes, and both asset classes depreciate together in real terms. The diversification benefit evaporates.

This is not hypothetical. It happened in the 1970s. It happened in the UK in 2022 (Liz Truss's 44-day experiment). And it has been happening progressively in 2025-2026 as term premium repricing has hurt both equity valuations (higher discount rates) and bond prices simultaneously.

**The structural implication:** fiscal dominance regimes require a different ballast. That ballast is **real assets** — assets whose intrinsic value is not denominated in nominal fiat currency and whose returns are driven by physical scarcity, productive capacity, or contractual inflation pass-through.

## The Four Pillars of a Real Assets Portfolio

### Pillar 1 — Precious Metals (Gold as Monetary Bedrock)

Gold's role in fiscal dominance regimes is misunderstood by most investors who frame it as "crisis insurance." That framing is too narrow. Gold is a **monetary alternative** — it holds value precisely because it cannot be debased by a government's printing press.

The key mechanism is **real interest rates**. Gold's expected return correlates negatively with real rates (nominal rate minus inflation expectation). When fiscal dominance forces real rates deeply negative — as they are in 2026 — the opportunity cost of holding gold falls to zero or below, and it becomes structurally attractive independent of any particular crisis.

**The 2026 data point:** Gold has crossed $5,000/oz. This is not a speculative bubble — it is the market pricing in the regime shift. Central banks (particularly in BRICS nations explicitly reducing dollar reserve holdings) are the marginal buyer. This is institutionalized demand, not retail speculation.

**Investment implication:** A 5–10% strategic allocation to gold (physical or LBMA-allocated, not futures) is not a "hedge" — it's a portfolio pillar. The mistake is treating it as tactical.

### Pillar 2 — Commodities & Upstream Natural Resources (The Scarcity Premium)

Commodities are the most direct inflation pass-through available to investors. Their prices rise with the underlying goods they are used to produce. But there's a more powerful argument specific to 2026: **the supply side is structurally constrained** in ways that don't respond quickly to price signals.

Why the supply constraint is sticky:
- **Energy capex underinvestment (2015-2021):** Decade of underinvestment in fossil fuel infrastructure during the green transition debate. Now the transition has slowed while demand hasn't — creating a window of structural undersupply.
- **Critical minerals monopolization:** Rare earths (China 85%+ of refining), cobalt (DRC + Chinese-owned operations), lithium (Latin American political instability) — supply is geopolitically constrained and cannot be quickly scaled.
- **Deglobalization production costs:** Reshoring increases the energy and materials intensity of production. More factories = more commodity demand per unit of GDP.

**Investment implication:** Upstream natural resource equities (miners, producers, royalty streamers) offer leveraged exposure to commodity prices with equity liquidity. Long-horizon tilt toward: copper (electrification demand, constrained supply), uranium (nuclear renaissance), and diversified commodity producers with strong balance sheets. Avoid leveraged commodity ETFs — contango destroys returns over time.

### Pillar 3 — Infrastructure (The Inflation-Linked Cashflow Machine)

Infrastructure assets — toll roads, pipelines, regulated utilities, airports, digital infrastructure — have one structural property that makes them uniquely valuable in fiscal dominance regimes: **contractual or regulatory inflation pass-through**.

Many infrastructure assets have revenues explicitly or implicitly linked to inflation. Toll roads with CPI-escalators, regulated utilities with rate-base returns, LNG terminals with indexed contracts. This turns inflation from an enemy into a tailwind.

The political dimension: fiscal dominance governments *spend* on infrastructure (it's politically popular). The US Infrastructure Act, EU Green Deal, and similar programs globally are pumping capital into the sector. This creates a crowding-in effect where public money de-risks private investment.

**Investment implication:** Listed infrastructure (unlisted is better but illiquid) provides genuine inflation protection with equity-like returns. Overweight: energy transition infrastructure (grid, storage, renewables with PPAs), digital infrastructure (data centers, fiber, towers), and water infrastructure (the most geopolitically stable of all physical commodities).

### Pillar 4 — Real Assets in Defensive Geographies (Political Risk Filtering)

This is where geopolitics intersects directly. Not all real assets are equal — their value depends on the rule of law governing the underlying property rights. An oil field in a country that can nationalize it is a different asset from the same field in Norway.

The fiscal dominance playbook must incorporate political risk into the real asset selection:

**Tier 1 (core allocation):** Australia (commodities + stable governance), Canada (energy + rule of law), Nordics (infrastructure + low sovereign risk), Singapore (financial infrastructure hub).

**Tier 2 (selective exposure):** Chile (copper but political volatility), Qatar (LNG but single-commodity risk), UAE (diversifying aggressively — interesting structural play).

**Avoid or minimize:** Russia (sanctions + expropriation risk), China (domestic regulatory risk for foreign investors), Venezuela, Argentina (restructuring risk), Nigeria (currency convertibility).

## The 1970s Analog: What Actually Worked

The last time fiscal dominance was structural was the 1970s. The lesson is instructive:

| Asset Class | 1970s Real Return | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Gold | +1,300% nominal | S&P500 down in real terms for the decade |
| Oil & Energy equities | Dramatically outperformed | OPEC as structural supply constraint |
| US Treasuries | Deeply negative in real terms | Classic fiscal dominance outcome |
| S&P 500 | Flat to negative in real terms | Despite nominal gains, inflation-adjusted = lost decade |
| Real estate | Mixed — inflation help, rate hurt | Location and leverage mattered enormously |
| Commodities (broad) | Significantly positive real returns | Structural scarcity narrative |

The key lesson from the 1970s: **the transition back to low inflation** (Volcker shock, 1980-1982) was brutal for commodities and gold. The regime ended — and ended fast. That is the tail risk for any real assets allocation today. Fiscal dominance is structural until it isn't: a credible debt consolidation, a deflationary shock, or a Volcker-style political commitment to austerity can reverse the regime.

**The discipline this demands:** size positions for a 3–7 year cycle, not forever. Maintain optionality. Don't become so embedded in the inflation narrative that you can't exit when it changes.

## Building the Actual Portfolio Tilt

For a long-horizon investor operating under the thesis developed in Lessons 425–433, here is the framework (not a specific allocation — Bolo makes those calls):

**What to overweight:**
- Gold and silver (monetary metals, non-yielding but no debasement risk)
- Copper, uranium, and critical mineral producers (structural demand + constrained supply)
- Listed infrastructure with inflation pass-through mechanisms
- Short-duration sovereign debt in Tier 1 fiscal-credible currencies (Norway, Australia, Canada, Singapore) — not US Treasuries as a "safe haven" in a fiscal dominance world
- Commodity-exporting EM currencies where fiscal positions are *improving* (Brazil, Indonesia, Malaysia — selectively)

**What to underweight:**
- Long-duration nominal bonds in fiscally stressed countries (US 30yr, EU periphery)
- REITs in high-debt economies (real estate good, but rising rates pressure NAVs)
- Consumer discretionary equities heavily exposed to households with depleted savings
- EM sovereign bonds in countries without reserve currency backstop (the L433 playbook)

**The position the CEO issues:** *Structural overweight to real assets — gold, critical minerals, and inflation-linked infrastructure — is the portfolio-level conclusion of the fiscal dominance arc. This is a 3–7 year thesis, not a trade. The falsifying condition: credible fiscal consolidation in G7 economies combined with a sustained decline in real interest rates back toward pre-2022 levels.*

## The 60/40 Replacement Framework

If not 60/40, then what? The framework emerging from institutional investors in 2025-2026:

**The "All-Weather for Fiscal Dominance" allocation:**
- 30% global equities (tilt: commodity-exposed, defensive moats, value over growth)
- 15% real assets (commodity producers, natural resource equities)
- 10% gold and monetary metals
- 15% listed infrastructure with inflation linkage
- 15% short-duration sovereigns (Tier 1 geographies only)
- 10% TIPS and inflation-linked bonds (fiscal credible issuers only)
- 5% tail risk hedges (long volatility, out-of-money puts on equity, FX options on dollar weakening)

This framework doesn't promise the Sharpe ratio of the old 60/40 in a benign regime. It sacrifices some upside in exchange for dramatically lower real return erosion during a sustained inflationary fiscal dominance period.
