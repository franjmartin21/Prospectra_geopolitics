## Opening Framing Question

In FY2026, the United States federal government will spend more on **interest payments on its national debt** than on its entire defense budget.

This has never happened before.

Not during the Cold War. Not during the Korean War or Vietnam. Not during the 1980s Reagan defense buildup that bankrupted the Soviet Union through an arms race. For the entirety of the American Century — the period roughly from 1945 to the present in which the United States underwrote the liberal international order through military presence, financial architecture, alliance commitments, and reserve currency privilege — the cost of financing US debt has always been a secondary line item relative to the cost of projecting power.

That is now over.

Interest payments on the US national debt are running at **$88 billion per month** — roughly $1.039 trillion for FY2026 — while the total federal deficit will reach an estimated **$1.9 trillion (5.8% of GDP)**. US debt held by the public now stands at **101% of GDP** and is projected by the Congressional Budget Office to reach 120% by 2036, well past the previous record of 106% set immediately after World War II.

Meanwhile, the United States is simultaneously maintaining 100,000 troops in Europe for NATO, funding a Pacific Deterrence Initiative at $14.7 billion aimed at Taiwan contingency planning, managing a reduced but still active commitment to Ukraine, and running an open-ended posture in the Middle East following the Iran war escalation.

**Here is the question that frames this lesson:**

*Every major power in history has eventually reached the point where its financial constraints and its strategic commitments pulled in opposite directions. Britain reached it at Suez in 1956. Spain reached it in the 1640s. Rome reached it in stages over two centuries. The question is not whether this dynamic applies to the United States. The question is: where on that curve is the US right now — and what happens to global asset prices as that constraint bites harder?*

## Part I: The Fiscal Architecture — What the Numbers Actually Mean

### The Interest Rate Trap

The US fiscal situation has a structural feature that makes it unusually dangerous: the debt was accumulated during a period of historically low interest rates (2010–2022), but the existing stock of debt is now being rolled over at rates 3–5 times higher.

When interest rates were near zero, a $25 trillion debt stock cost perhaps $300–400 billion per year in interest. When rates normalized to 4–5%, that same debt stock (now grown to nearly $35 trillion) costs over $1 trillion per year. The principal is not being paid down — it is being refinanced at higher rates each year as bonds mature. This creates a structural ratchet: even if the deficit stayed flat, interest expense would still grow.

This is the **debt trap** dynamic. The only exits are:
1. **Austerity** — cutting spending or raising taxes to run primary surpluses. Politically extremely difficult, particularly with defense at a political floor and entitlements untouchable.
2. **Financial repression** — engineering negative real interest rates to erode debt/GDP over time. Requires inflation above interest rates, which damages bond holders and undermines the dollar's reserve appeal.
3. **Nominal growth outrunning debt** — if US GDP grows faster than debt accumulates, debt/GDP can stabilize. Possible, but requires sustained high growth during a period of demographic headwinds and deglobalization.
4. **Dollar debasement** — a more aggressive form of financial repression where the reserve currency itself is inflated away. The geopolitical risk here is that it accelerates de-dollarization by foreign holders.

The Trump administration's FY2027 defense proposal — $1.5 trillion, adding $5.8 trillion to the national debt with interest costs included — makes option 1 essentially impossible if enacted and makes options 2 and 4 progressively more likely.

### The Foreign Holder Dynamic

The US cannot simply ignore what foreign central banks do with their Treasury holdings — because the US depends on foreign buyers to finance its deficit at manageable interest rates.

The current picture is concerning:

- **China** has reduced Treasury holdings to $652–659 billion, the lowest level since 2008, down from a peak of $1.3 trillion in 2013. The reduction is gradual but relentless.
- **Japan**, the largest foreign holder at $1.191 trillion, shed approximately $47 billion in the first quarter of 2026 as the Iran war energy shock forced the Bank of Japan to defend the yen.
- The **Iran war energy shock** triggered a broader EM central bank liquidation of dollar reserves as countries defended their currencies against the oil price spike. This pattern — a geopolitical shock forcing foreign liquidation of USTs — is likely to recur in future crises.

The structural question: if the US needs to issue roughly $2 trillion in new Treasuries each year to finance its deficit, and foreign buyers are reducing rather than increasing their holdings, who absorbs the supply? The answer, increasingly, is domestic buyers (money market funds, US banks, the Fed) — but at rates that reflect the credit and duration risk of a highly indebted sovereign.

## Part II: The Historical Template — Britain at Suez

No case study is more instructive for the current US situation than the collapse of British imperial power between 1945 and 1956.

### The Setup

Britain emerged from WWII as a nominal victor but a financial wreck. The war had cost Britain roughly £27 billion (equivalent to perhaps $1 trillion today), it had borrowed heavily from the US under Lend-Lease, its industrial base was exhausted, and it was running large current account deficits. The pound sterling, still a reserve currency, was under chronic pressure.

Critically, Britain **maintained the pretense of great power status** — with global bases, colonies, a nuclear program, commitments in Korea, the Middle East, Malaya, and Europe — for a decade after the fiscal reality had already made those commitments unsustainable. The gap between Britain's strategic posture and its actual financial capacity grew quietly, invisibly, until it suddenly became visible to everyone at once.

### The Suez Moment (1956)

When Egypt's Gamal Abdel Nasser nationalized the Suez Canal in 1956, Britain and France intervened militarily. The operation was a tactical military success — Anglo-French forces were winning on the ground. It was a strategic catastrophe.

President Eisenhower — angry that Britain and France had acted without US coordination and threatened to destabilize the Middle East during the Cold War — applied **financial pressure**. The US refused to support the pound through the IMF, effectively engineering a run on sterling. In one week, Britain lost tens of millions of pounds in reserves and faced the prospect of a currency collapse.

Britain withdrew from Suez within days.

The lesson is not merely that the US used financial leverage to humble Britain. The deeper lesson is that **Britain's strategic options had already been constrained by its fiscal position before Suez happened** — Suez simply made that constraint visible and inescapable. Britain could not sustain the operation because it could not sustain the currency, because it could not sustain the debt, because it had spent 10 years living beyond its strategic means.

Within 15 years of Suez, Britain had withdrawn from "East of Suez" entirely, decolonized most of its empire, devalued the pound, and fundamentally repositioned as a medium European power rather than a global one.

**The pattern:**
1. Fiscal overextension hidden beneath reserve currency privilege and political will
2. A triggering event that exposes the gap between posture and capacity
3. A forced, rapid, and disorderly retreat from strategic commitments
4. Long-term repositioning of the nation's role in the international system

## Part III: The US Today — Where on the Curve?

### What's Different

The US is not Britain. Key distinctions matter:

- **Dollar hegemony is deeper than sterling hegemony ever was.** Global dollar invoicing, dollar-denominated commodity markets, and SWIFT's dollar architecture give the US structural advantages Britain never had. The "exorbitant privilege" is real.
- **The US military-industrial complex is more integrated into domestic political economy.** Defense spending is a jobs program for 50 states, not just a strategic posture — which makes cuts politically harder but also more politically costly to impose.
- **US debt is denominated in its own currency.** Britain had dollar-denominated debts in 1956. The US cannot be Suez'd in the same way — it can always print dollars to service dollar debt, at the cost of inflation.
- **US alliances are treaty-based.** The Article 5 NATO commitment is a legal obligation. Walking away from it would not be strategic retreat; it would be a breach of treaty that rewires every ally's security calculus immediately.

### What's the Same

But the structural dynamic rhymes:

- **The gap between strategic posture and fiscal capacity is widening.** The US is committed to deterring Russia in Europe, China over Taiwan, and Iran in the Middle East — simultaneously — while paying $1 trillion per year just to service existing debt. Adding $1.5 trillion in annual defense spending on top of $1.9 trillion in annual deficits pushes that gap further.
- **Reserve currency privilege is not infinite.** The gradual diversification away from dollar reserves — visible in central bank gold purchases, the China-Russia bilateral trade in non-dollar terms, and the incipient use of the yuan in Gulf energy pricing — is exactly what preceded sterling's decline. The process is slow until it isn't.
- **The triggering event will not be scheduled.** Britain did not plan for Suez to be the moment its fiscal constraint became visible. The US fiscal constraint could become visible in a Taiwan crisis, a dollar funding squeeze, a Treasury market accident, or a US domestic political decision to simply reduce commitments because the domestic fiscal politics of maintaining them has become untenable.

### The DOGE Paradox

The current administration's "Department of Government Efficiency" cuts — targeting civilian federal employees, foreign aid, and regulatory apparatus — reduce federal spending at the margin but leave the fiscal arithmetic essentially unchanged. The US federal budget is dominated by three categories: entitlements (Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid), defense, and interest. DOGE targets the 15% of the budget that is discretionary non-defense spending. Cutting it to zero would not close a $1.9 trillion deficit.

The paradox: the administration is simultaneously cutting foreign aid (reducing soft power and alliance maintenance spending) while proposing $1.5 trillion in defense spending (increasing hard power spending on credit). This is a strategic posture that costs more and invests less in the relational architecture that makes that hard power usable.

## Part IV: Investment Implications

### 1. US Treasuries — The Core Contradiction

The US fiscal trajectory is structurally bearish for Treasury bonds over a 5–10 year horizon:
- Rising supply (deficit financing)
- Declining foreign demand (central bank diversification)
- Structural inflation pressures (fiscal dominance dynamics)

**Directional view:** Long-duration Treasuries (10Y+) are structurally challenged. The near-term remains anchored by the USD's reserve currency status, but the 10-year outlook is for higher nominal yields and potentially negative real returns if inflation is used to erode debt. Duration underweight as a structural portfolio position.

### 2. Gold — The Fiscal Alarm

Central banks globally have been the marginal buyers of gold for three consecutive years, most of them reducing dollar reserves and adding gold as the non-dollar, non-yielding reserve asset that carries no counterparty risk. This trend accelerates when US fiscal deterioration becomes more visible.

**Directional view:** Gold is the cleanest expression of the "fiscal solvency of the hegemon is declining" thesis. Structural long. The Prospectra framework has flagged gold in multiple prior lessons — this fiscal dynamic reinforces that position with a different mechanism (not just inflation, but reserve diversification).

### 3. European Defense Equities (Lesson 186 connection)

The US fiscal constraint is the primary driver of European defense rearmament. If the US is genuinely going to ask NATO allies to shoulder more of the collective defense burden — and the FY2026 NDAA's troop floor requirements suggest this is now a structural debate, not a rhetorical one — European defense contractors benefit from a spending cycle that is now structural, not cyclical.

**Directional view:** Long European defense (Rheinmetall, Leonardo, Thales, KNDS), with a 5–7 year horizon. The US fiscal constraint makes European rearmament self-sustaining independent of any particular administration.

### 4. Dollar (DXY) — Slow Erosion, Not Collapse

Dollar reserve diversification is a 10–20 year process, not a 6-month trade. But the structural direction is clear: the dollar's share of global reserves has declined from ~71% in 1999 to approximately 58% today, and the fiscal dynamics described here accelerate rather than reverse that trend.

**Directional view:** Dollar weakness on a 5–10 year structural basis. For portfolio construction, this argues for non-dollar assets and commodities as structural allocations, not tactical trades. EM currencies in current account surplus countries (BRL, INR, IDR) as long-term diversification.

### 5. Defense Contractors (US) — The Budget Ceiling Risk

US defense contractors benefit from the proposed $1.5T FY2027 budget. But the fiscal math creates a ceiling: at some point, debt service costs force a political reckoning with the defense budget that markets have not priced. The UK defense sector was structurally impaired for 30 years after Suez as British retrenchment compressed the budget.

**Directional view:** US defense contractors (LMT, RTX, NOC) benefit in the near term (1–3 years) from spending proposals, but carry a medium-term (5–10 year) risk that the fiscal reckoning eventually arrives. European defense is a structurally cleaner long.

## CEO Portfolio Note

The single most important structural investment implication of this lesson is this: **we are in the early-to-middle stages of US fiscal constraint becoming the binding constraint on American strategic power.** This will unfold over a decade, not a year. The investment implications are not "buy gold today" — gold is already partially pricing this. The implications are structural portfolio architecture decisions:

- Systematic non-dollar diversification (not tactical currency trades — structural asset allocation)
- European defense as a structural overweight (not a cyclical trade)
- Duration underweight as a structural position (not a rate prediction)
- Gold as a reserve diversification play (not just an inflation hedge)

The lesson of Britain is not that investors who saw Suez coming made fortunes. It is that investors who had gradually positioned for the structural trend — sterling weakness, UK equity underperformance relative to US, commodity strength — captured the decade-long shift. The equivalent structural repositioning for US fiscal exhaustion is the thesis we are building toward.
