## 1. The Pricing Paradox

Geopolitical risk is simultaneously:
1. The most consequential driver of asset price regimes over the long run
2. The hardest risk to price using standard financial models

This paradox creates persistent mispricing — and persistent mispricing creates alpha opportunities for disciplined, long-horizon investors.

Why is geopolitical risk so hard to price?

**Black-Scholes doesn't work.** Standard option pricing models assume log-normal return distributions — i.e., that large moves are exponentially unlikely. Geopolitical events produce non-normal distributions with fat tails. The models that underpin derivatives pricing, portfolio risk management, and institutional risk systems are structurally incapable of pricing regime-change risk.

**It doesn't fit the institutional calendar.** Most institutional investors operate on quarterly reporting cycles. A geopolitical thesis that plays out over 3-7 years is structurally hard to hold. Career risk — the risk of underperforming for 6 quarters before the thesis resolves — creates massive incentives to ignore long-duration geopolitical risk.

**It's hard to falsify until it happens.** Quantitative risk models require historical precedent and defined probability distributions. Geopolitical events are often genuinely novel — the market had no prior model for Russia invading Ukraine, or for U.S.-Israeli military strikes on Iran. When events have no close analogue, models assign them low probability by default.

**The "it won't happen" prior.** Markets, like humans, suffer from normalcy bias: the assumption that the current peaceful-ish equilibrium will persist. This prior is usually correct in the short run — most geopolitical crises don't escalate to market-moving events. But when they do, the correction is sharp and the prior is revealed as deeply wrong.

**The result:** Markets systematically underprice low-probability, high-impact geopolitical events. This is the foundation of the opportunity.

## 2. The Caldara-Iacoviello Framework — Measuring What Markets Won't

Dario Caldara (Federal Reserve) and Matteo Iacoviello (Federal Reserve) published one of the most important papers in modern macro finance: *"Measuring Geopolitical Risk"* (American Economic Review, 2022). They built the **Geopolitical Risk Index (GPR)** — the benchmark academic and practitioner tool for quantifying geopolitical risk across time.

**How it's constructed:**
- Automated text-search across 10 major newspapers (New York Times, Financial Times, Wall Street Journal, Guardian, Chicago Tribune, and others)
- Counts monthly articles related to adverse geopolitical events as a share of total news articles
- Runs back to 1900, providing 120+ years of data
- Published monthly; freely available at matteoiacoviello.com

**Two sub-indices:**
- **GPRT — Geopolitical Threats Index:** Counts articles on war threats, peace threats, military buildups, nuclear threats, and terror threats — *before* events happen
- **GPRA — Geopolitical Acts Index:** Counts articles on war beginning, war escalation, and terror acts — *after* events happen

The distinction between GPRT and GPRA is analytically crucial. GPRT captures **risk perception and anticipation.** GPRA captures **realized geopolitical shock.** Their divergence tells you whether the market is over- or under-estimating risk relative to what actually unfolds.

**What the research finds:**
- Higher geopolitical risk (higher GPR) forecasts lower investment, lower employment, and lower economic output with a 6–12 month lag
- Higher GPR is associated with higher disaster probability and larger left-tail outcomes across equities, commodities, and EM assets
- Crucially: **both GPRT and GPRA drive market effects** — meaning it's not just the event that matters, it's the *threat* of the event

**The 2026 context:** The composite geoeconomic risk indicator reached an all-time high in March 2026 (ECB Financial Stability Review, May 2026). The Iran strikes, Hormuz closure, stalled Ukraine ceasefire negotiations, and U.S.-China semiconductor escalation combined to produce the highest aggregate geopolitical risk reading in the dataset's history — surpassing even the Cold War peak years. Markets recovered from the initial shock within weeks.

This is the mispricing gap in real time.

## 3. How Markets Actually Respond to Geopolitical Shocks — The Event Study Evidence

Decades of academic event studies produce a consistent pattern. It is critical to understand this pattern because it tells you *when* to act.

**The typical market response curve:**

```
[Phase 1 — Shock & Repricing]    [Phase 2 — Assessment]    [Phase 3 — Resolution]
      Day 0 to Day 5                  Day 5 to Day 30             Day 30 to 18 months
 
 Sharp drawdown (5–15%)         Gradual assessment of      Rebound OR structural
 VIX spike                      scope and escalation       re-rating — depends on
 Oil spike (if energy-related)  probability                whether regime changed
 Gold bid
 Flight to dollar/T-bills
```

**Two fundamentally different outcomes:**

**Type A — Transient Shock:** The event is severe but doesn't change the underlying geopolitical regime. Markets overreact in Phase 1 and recover in Phase 3. Historical examples:
- 9/11 (2001): S&P fell ~12% in the week after reopening, recovered within 3 months
- Gulf War I (1990): Oil spiked to $40, equities fell 20%; both reversed quickly post-ceasefire
- SARS (2003): Regional equity panic, recovered within 2 months

**Type B — Structural Regime Shift:** The event changes the underlying structure of geopolitical relationships, supply chains, or trade patterns. Markets initially underreact, then undergo a prolonged re-rating. Historical examples:
- Russia-Ukraine (2022): European energy infrastructure permanently repriced; Russia removed from global financial system; European defense spending structurally re-rated upward. These effects were *still compounding in 2026.*
- Iran Hormuz closure (2026): Whether this is Type A or Type B is the central analytical question of the current period. If Hormuz reopens within 3–6 months: Type A. If it catalyzes a durable shift in Gulf energy pricing, dollar settlement, and Asian supply chain routing: Type B.

**The investor's core task:** Distinguish Type A from Type B as early as possible, and position accordingly.

**Type A signals:** Clear political exit path, both sides have incentive to de-escalate, no structural change to trade routes or financial architecture, historical precedent for quick resolution.

**Type B signals:** Structural change to supply chains, financial architecture, alliance relationships, or energy routing; no clear political exit for at least one party; historical precedent rare or absent; creates new equilibrium rather than restoring old one.

## 4. The Mispricing Window — Why It Persists and How to Exploit It

The Carlyle CEO's observation is statistically well-documented. Here is why the mispricing window exists and persists:

**Reason 1: The Quarterly Reporting Trap**
Institutional investors managing $100B+ AUM cannot hold a thesis that underperforms for 6-12 quarters. If you overweight defense in 2020 based on a geopolitical escalation thesis, and the thesis takes until 2022 to pay off, you've lost clients and possibly your job. The geopolitical alpha opportunity exists precisely because institutional capital cannot hold long enough to collect it.

**Reason 2: Geopolitical Risk Isn't in the Models**
The risk management systems at most large institutions use VaR (Value at Risk), stress tests based on historical data, and factor models. None of these capture regime-change risk. When the GPR index is at an all-time high but historical VaR models say risk is normal, the risk manager wins the internal argument. Capital allocation stays where the model says it should.

**Reason 3: The Overreaction-Underreaction Asymmetry**
Markets overreact to *visible* short-term events (a bad earnings report, an interest rate surprise) because the connection between event and price is direct and immediate. They underreact to *slow-moving* structural risks (a deteriorating geopolitical relationship, a supply chain becoming fragile) because the connection to price is distant and uncertain. Geopolitical risk is almost always slow-moving before it snaps — and then markets are shocked.

**Reason 4: Information Disadvantage**
Most market participants don't have deep geopolitical expertise. They rely on mainstream financial media, which lags academic and policy analysis by months to years. By the time the FT publishes a cover story on "the end of globalization," the trade has been over for two years.

**The exploitation strategy for long-horizon investors:**
1. Identify when the GPR index diverges significantly from market-implied volatility (VIX, OVX, MOVE index)
2. When GPR is elevated and market vol is suppressed, the market is systematically underpricing risk
3. This creates an asymmetric entry: position for the structural scenario at a time when the market is still pricing the benign scenario
4. Size positions for a long horizon — the mispricing window closes eventually, but timing the close is impossible; surviving until it closes is the discipline

## 5. Risk Premium Decomposition — The Practitioner Toolkit

How do practitioners actually measure geopolitical risk in asset prices? These are the instruments and signals that tell you what the market is pricing:

**5.1 Credit Default Swaps (CDS)**
CDS spreads on sovereign debt are the purest market signal of geopolitical risk. When Iran's CDS (when tradeable), Russia's CDS (500bps+ after 2022), or Turkey's CDS widens, it captures the market's probability-weighted view on default — which is itself a function of geopolitical stability. CDS spreads embed political risk + economic risk + liquidity risk; separating them requires comparing CDS to bond spreads and economic fundamentals.

**5.2 Sovereign Bond Spreads (EM)**
The spread between, say, Turkish 10-year dollar bonds and U.S. Treasuries is a direct measure of the political risk premium. Russia's spread went from ~200bps pre-February 2022 to essentially infinite (market close) within a week. These moves are the market's real-time repricing of regime risk.

**5.3 Options Volatility (VIX, OVX, MOVE)**
- **VIX** (equity volatility): Measures 30-day implied vol on S&P options. Spikes during geopolitical shocks — but importantly, can remain suppressed even when structural risk is elevated (exactly the mispricing condition)
- **OVX** (oil volatility): The geopolitical risk barometer for energy markets. Hormuz closure + supply disruption = OVX spike. During the Iran 2026 crisis, OVX hit levels comparable to COVID (2020) and the Gulf War (1990)
- **MOVE index** (Treasury volatility): When geopolitical risk is accompanied by fiscal concerns (defense spending, sanctions costs), MOVE rises along with VIX

**The golden signal:** When GPR index is elevated but VIX/OVX are suppressed, you have the purest version of geopolitical mispricing. The market is telling you it doesn't believe the geopolitical risk is real. History says this is wrong with a lag.

**5.4 Gold vs. Real Rates**
Gold price = 1 / (real rates) in the baseline model. But gold consistently trades *above* this implied level during geopolitical stress — the excess is the geopolitical risk premium. Tracking gold's deviation from its real-rate-implied price gives a direct measure of how much fear premium the market is embedding. In 2026, gold has been trading 15-20% above its real-rate implied level — an historically large geopolitical premium.

**5.5 Commodity Term Structure**
When geopolitical risk is elevated and supply-specific (oil chokepoint, mineral supply chain disruption), commodity forward curves go into steep backwardation (spot price >> forward price). Backwardation signals acute supply fear — the market is paying a premium to get the physical commodity now. Hormuz closure → maximum oil backwardation. The slope of backwardation is a real-time geopolitical signal.

**5.6 Currency Positioning (CoT Data)**
The CFTC Commitments of Traders (CoT) report shows speculative positioning in FX futures. When hedge funds pile into dollar longs and EM short positions coinciding with GPR elevation, it confirms markets are pricing geopolitical flight. When positioning reverts before GPR does, it creates an entry window.

**The market is pricing a faster mean reversion than the structural evidence supports.** This is the mispricing window.

## 7. Investment Implications

Synthesizing 85 lessons into a risk pricing framework:

**Rule 1: Separate the signal from the noise**
Not all GPR elevation creates investable opportunities. The signal is when:
- GPR elevation is structural (policy change, alliance fracture, infrastructure disruption), not event-driven (election, diplomatic incident)
- Market volatility (VIX, OVX) is not commensurate with GPR elevation — the divergence is the opportunity
- The regime consequences have asset-class-specific implications you can express with instruments available to you

**Rule 2: The Type A/Type B classification determines your trade**
- **Type A:** Sell volatility, buy the dip in high-quality assets during Phase 1 shock, hold through Phase 3 recovery
- **Type B:** Add exposure to structural beneficiaries (defense, non-Gulf energy, gold, EM commodity exporters), reduce exposure to structural victims (EM energy importers, European industrials dependent on cheap energy, dollar-denominated EM debt)

**Rule 3: Horizon-match your position to the regime**
A Type B thesis resolves over 3-7 years. Size positions so you can survive 6+ quarters of the thesis being "wrong" (i.e., markets not yet recognizing the structural shift). If you can't hold a position for 3 years, don't express a Type B thesis.

**Rule 4: Gold is your geopolitical risk hedge, not your speculation**
Gold pricing already embeds a geopolitical risk premium. Holding gold is not betting on geopolitical crisis — it is *having appropriate insurance* against the scenario where the current geopolitical risk regime worsens. Every portfolio with real geopolitical exposure should carry gold as the no-counterparty hedge.

**Rule 5: Watch the second-order effects**
Markets price the first-order effect quickly (oil rises when Hormuz closes). The mispricing is in the second-order effects: what does expensive energy do to European industrial margins over 2 years? What does correspondent banking de-risking do to EM trade finance costs? What does CIPS volume growth do to dollar invoice share over 5 years? These second-order, long-horizon effects are where the systematic edge lives.

## 8. Databricks Angle — The Geopolitical Risk Pricing Engine

This lesson describes the **core analytical engine** of the entire Databricks project. Every pipeline we've discussed ultimately feeds into answering this question: is the market correctly pricing geopolitical risk right now?

**Key data sources:**
- **GPR Index (Caldara-Iacoviello):** Free monthly download at matteoiacoviello.com. Historical back to 1900. This is your primary geopolitical risk series.
- **Country-Specific GPR:** Same methodology applied at the country level — available for 40+ countries. Critical for EM political risk assessment.
- **GDELT Project:** Daily event data that can approximate a real-time GPR signal (GDELT uses similar text-analysis methodology). Available in Google BigQuery — use it to build a daily GPR approximation between the monthly official releases.
- **VIX, OVX, MOVE:** Available via FRED or Yahoo Finance. These are your market-implied risk series.
- **Gold price, real 10-year TIPS yield:** Both on FRED. The spread between actual gold price and real-rate-implied gold price is your "fear premium" series.
- **CDS spreads (sovereign):** Available via Bloomberg, Markit, or third-party sources. Build a rolling EM Political Risk Spread index from 10-15 country CDS levels.

**The core pipeline:**

```
Stage 1 — Risk Quantification:
GDELT daily events + GPR monthly
  → Daily GPR approximation by region/country
  → Market-implied risk (VIX, OVX, MOVE, CDS spreads)
  → Risk Divergence Score: GPR_approx / Market_Vol_composite

Stage 2 — Regime Classification:
Risk Divergence Score > threshold → "Mispricing Alert" flag
Historical event catalog (from GDELT) → Type A / Type B classifier
  → Input: duration, escalation pattern, regime-change indicators
  → Output: P(Type B) for each flagged event

Stage 3 — Signal Generation:
P(Type B) × Asset Class Exposure Mapping
  → Directional signal by asset class
  → Signal strength proportional to divergence magnitude and Type B probability
  → Output: weekly signal dashboard with conviction levels

Stage 4 — Track Record:
Every signal logged with:
  - Date, asset class, direction, conviction
  - Thesis summary (Type A or Type B, horizon estimate)
  - Outcome tracker (updated monthly)
  → This IS the investment log (reports/investment_log.md)
```

**Specific features to engineer:**
- `gpr_vix_divergence` — monthly: (GPR percentile rank - VIX percentile rank). Positive = market underpricing. Historical correlation with forward 6-month market corrections: strongly positive during regime shifts.
- `gold_fear_premium` — weekly: (Actual gold price - real-rate-implied gold price) / real-rate-implied. Rising = geopolitical fear premium expanding.
- `oil_backwardation_index` — daily: (Spot WTI - 12-month forward WTI) / Spot. Measures supply fear intensity.
- `type_b_probability` — event-level: Logistic regression output using event features (duration, escalation rate, alliance structure change, trade route disruption flag).
- `geopolitical_alpha_signal` — composite weekly signal: combines divergence, gold fear premium, backwardation, and Type B probability into a single 0-100 score. Above 70 = strong positioning signal.
