## Opening Frame

Here is the question that drives this lesson:

*When a belt of states spanning 5,000 km across Africa's waist simultaneously ejects Western military and diplomatic presence, turns to Russia and China for security guarantees, and sits atop some of the world's most strategically important mineral deposits — is that a regional crisis, or is it the opening chapter of Africa's geopolitical realignment?*

Between 2020 and 2023, the Sahel experienced the highest concentration of military coups anywhere on earth in a generation. Mali (2020, 2021), Guinea (2021), Burkina Faso (2022, again 2022), Niger (2023), Chad (2021, transitional). Gabon (2023). Each coup was distinct in its immediate triggers. But together they represent something structural: the **collapse of the post-colonial security architecture that France — and by extension the West — built and maintained across francophone Africa for sixty years**.

What replaced it is not stable. It is contested. Russia's Wagner Group (now rebranded as Africa Corps under the Russian Ministry of Defense) moved into Mali, Burkina Faso, and Niger with astonishing speed. China deepened its economic positioning. The United States pulled back from Niger — home to one of its most strategically important drone bases in Africa. France's Operation Barkhane — 14 years, thousands of soldiers, billions of euros — ended in humiliation as military governments that owed their existence to French training expelled French forces.

The Sahel is now a live theater of great power competition on a continent of 1.4 billion people with the world's fastest-growing population, the largest reserves of critical minerals essential to the energy transition, and — crucially for investors — sovereign debt, equity markets, and FX regimes that are re-pricing in real time as the geopolitical architecture shifts.

This lesson completes the transition from the Middle East power arc (Lessons 110–114) to Africa. It gives you the structural framework for understanding what is happening in the Sahel, why it matters to portfolios that may never directly invest in Mali or Burkina Faso, and what the investment signals are for gold, uranium, African sovereign debt, and European political risk.

## 1. The Geography of Fragility — Why the Sahel?

The Sahel is the semi-arid belt stretching from Senegal in the west to Sudan in the east, roughly 5,000 km long and 500-800 km wide, separating the Sahara Desert to the north from the more fertile sub-Saharan regions to the south.

**The geographic constraints that create the political fragility:**

- **Minimal arable land and chronic food insecurity:** The Sahel receives 200-600mm of rainfall annually — the minimum threshold for rain-fed agriculture. Climate change has been relentlessly compressing this window southward. The UN estimates that 35 million people in the Sahel face acute food insecurity in 2026, a number that has roughly doubled since 2020.

- **Resource curse geography:** The Sahel sits atop enormous mineral wealth that its governments have never been able to convert into broad-based development. Niger has some of the world's largest uranium reserves (supplying ~15% of EU uranium imports historically). Mali has significant gold production. Burkina Faso has gold. The mineral wealth generates revenue flows that enrich elites and attract foreign mining companies but does not build the institutional infrastructure — schools, roads, hospitals, civil service — that would stabilize governance.

- **Ethnic and religious fragmentation:** The Sahel is an ethnic mosaic. Tuareg, Fulani, Hausa, Bambara, Mossi — these are not just cultural categories. They are political identities that colonial borders bisected, creating states with minimal shared national consciousness and deep internal fault lines that jihadist groups have systematically exploited.

- **The jihadist insurgency:** Al-Qaeda in the Islamic Maghreb (AQIM) and its successor Jama'at Nusrat al-Islam wal-Muslimin (JNIM), plus the Islamic State's Sahel affiliate (ISGS), have controlled significant territory across Mali, Burkina Faso, and Niger. By 2026, JNIM effectively governs large portions of central and northern Mali and significant areas of Burkina Faso — including areas with gold and mineral concessions. This is not a temporary insurgency. It is a territorial governing power.

## 2. The Coup Belt — What Happened and Why

### Mali: The Prototype (2020–2021)

The sequence:
- August 2020: Military officers overthrow President Ibrahim Boubacar Keïta, citing corruption and failure to address the jihadist insurgency. The coup is widely popular in Bamako.
- May 2021: A second coup within the transitional government. Colonel Assimi Goïta — trained at US military schools, among others — consolidates power.
- 2022: Mali expels French forces from Operation Barkhane and invites Wagner Group (African Corps) to provide security. France responds by withdrawing all forces. The AU suspends Mali.
- 2023-2026: Mali-Wagner partnership operates. Wagner/African Corps deploy across central and northern Mali. Human rights organizations document massacres of civilians — most notably the Moura massacre (March 2022, 500+ civilians killed in what UN investigators concluded was a summary execution by Malian Armed Forces and "foreign military personnel" identified as Wagner). The jihadist insurgency has not been defeated; it has in some areas intensified.

**The Mali template established:**
1. Military coup with genuine popular legitimacy (frustration with Western-aligned civilian government's failure to deliver security)
2. Expulsion of Western forces and diplomats
3. Invitation to Wagner/Russian partners
4. AU suspension → transition without external accountability pressure
5. Jihadist insurgency continues or worsens despite Russian "solution"

### Burkina Faso: Double Coup (2022)

- January 2022: Lieutenant Colonel Paul-Henri Damiba overthrows President Roch Kaboré. Security rationale.
- September 2022: Captain Ibrahim Traoré overthrows Damiba. At 34, Traoré becomes one of the world's youngest heads of state.
- 2022-2023: Burkina Faso expels French forces (including the Sabre special operations unit), terminates the defense agreement, and signs cooperation agreements with Russia.
- 2024: Reports emerge of Wagner/African Corps presence in Burkina Faso. Traoré's government imposes media blackouts on reporting jihadist attacks — a direct attempt to prevent independent assessment of security trajectory.
- 2025-2026: Burkina Faso has arguably the most acute security crisis in the Sahel. JNIM and ISGS control or contest roughly 40-60% of national territory. Major cities including Ouagadougou experience attacks. The mining sector — gold is Burkina Faso's primary export — is directly disrupted as jihadists attack convoys, shut roads, and impose parallel taxation on mining operations.

**Gold investor alert:** Burkina Faso is the fourth-largest gold producer in Africa. Mining companies operating there — including Endeavour Mining, West African Resources, and others — face direct operational risk from jihadist activity. This is not tail risk; it is current operating reality.

### Niger: The US Drone Base and the Uranium Chokepoint (2023)

- July 2023: Presidential Guard officers arrest President Mohamed Bazoum. General Abdourahamane Tiani declares himself head of the National Council for the Safeguard of the Homeland (CNSP).
- The coup triggers an immediate US and EU response: Niger hosted **Air Base 201 at Agadez** — the largest US drone base in Africa, costing approximately $110 million to build and critical to US surveillance and counterterrorism operations across the Sahel and into Libya. The US initially refused to declare it a "coup" to avoid automatic aid cutoffs required by law.
- March 2024: Niger terminates the military cooperation agreement with the United States and demands withdrawal of US forces. By late 2024, the US completes its withdrawal from Air Base 201 — a strategic blow to US intelligence and counterterrorism architecture across the region.
- The strategic cost to the US: Loss of a tier-1 ISR (intelligence, surveillance, reconnaissance) platform. French and US departure from Niger leaves a 1,200km surveillance gap in the Sahel.
- Niger joins Mali and Burkina Faso in the **Alliance of Sahel States (AES)** — a mutual security pact announced September 2023, explicitly positioned as an alternative to ECOWAS (the West African regional body they view as a French proxy).

**The uranium dimension:** Niger produces approximately 4-5% of global uranium and was responsible for roughly 15% of EU uranium supply via Orano (formerly Areva). France's nuclear power fleet is partially dependent on Nigerien uranium. Post-coup, Orano suspended operations; France diversified toward Kazakhstan and Canada. Niger's new government has signaled intent to renegotiate all mining contracts on terms more favorable to the state — a resource nationalism move with direct implications for the uranium supply chain and European nuclear energy security.

### Chad: The Managed Transition (2021)

Chad is the partial exception to the full Western rupture narrative:
- April 2021: President Idriss Déby dies in combat against rebel forces. His son Mahamat Idriss Déby takes power via the military council. France — which has a permanent military base in N'Djamena — tacitly accepts the succession, calculating that Mahamat Déby is preferable to a full breakdown in a state that borders Libya, Sudan, Niger, and the Central African Republic simultaneously.
- Chad remains nominally in the Western orbit while navigating intense pressure from the AES bloc and maintaining its own deals with various actors.
- **Investment flag:** Chad is an oil producer. It exports via pipeline through Cameroon to the Atlantic coast. The stability of that pipeline — and the sovereign debt it underlies — is contingent on Mahamat Déby maintaining his balancing act.

## 3. The Russian Play — Wagner/Africa Corps

### What Russia Offers That France Didn't

Russia's pitch to Sahel juntas is simple and compelling to leaders facing jihadist insurgencies and Western human rights conditionality:

1. **No governance strings attached.** France's Barkhane operation came with implicit and explicit pressure on civilian governance, corruption, and democratic transitions. Russia does not care. The Wagner model is: security services in exchange for mining concessions, cash, and diplomatic alignment.

2. **Brutal effectiveness in controlled contexts.** Wagner demonstrated in Syria and later in the Central African Republic (CAR) that it can stabilize a regime's urban core and protect key asset corridors — mines, roads, capital cities — through extreme violence that Western-trained forces will not and cannot legally employ.

3. **Mining concessions as the business model.** In CAR, Wagner received diamond and gold mining rights as partial payment. This model has been replicated. African Corps is not an aid program — it is a for-profit security company with state backing, extracting mineral resources to fund its operations and generate revenue for Russian state and private actors.

### The Wagner/Africa Corps Reality Check

The brutal assessment:
- **Wagner has not solved any insurgency in the Sahel.** In Mali, jihadist attacks have continued and in some periods intensified. The Moura massacre — the signature Wagner operation — did not degrade JNIM's capacity; it generated mass civilian alienation that aids jihadist recruitment.
- **What Wagner has achieved:** Protecting capital cities, key mineral corridors, and the physical person of junta leaders. It is a regime-protection service, not a counterinsurgency force.
- **The cost:** Several hundred documented civilian massacres across Mali and Burkina Faso. Destruction of the trust-based civil-military relationships that are essential to successful counterinsurgency. Long-term destabilization of the social fabric in areas Wagner operates.

**For investors:** The Wagner/Africa Corps presence is a signal that **mining operations in areas they secure are commercially operable in the near term but carry long-term governance and reputational risk**. A gold mine secured by Wagner in northern Mali is producing today. It is also one policy change — a new government, a UN sanctions push, a US mining company partner demanding compliance — away from an operational suspension.

## 4. The French Withdrawal — Strategic Consequences

### The Scale of the Failure

France's Operation Barkhane was the largest French military operation since the Algeria war. At peak: 5,500 soldiers across five countries, 1,500 vehicles, drones, fighter aircraft, total cost exceeding €1.5 billion/year.

The stated mission: degrade jihadist groups and allow Sahel states to develop the security capacity to manage their own territory.

The outcome after 9 years (2013-2022): JNIM doubled its area of operations during Barkhane's peak years. Jihadist attacks increased 300% from 2015 to 2022 in Mali, Burkina Faso, and Niger. The insurgency was not degraded — it expanded.

Why did Barkhane fail? The structural answer, rather than the operational one:
1. **Counterinsurgency requires political legitimacy, not just military force.** Barkhane was militarily proficient but operated in states where the civilian government had minimal popular legitimacy. Killing jihadist commanders does not address the grievances — corruption, ethnic marginalization, lack of economic opportunity — that drive recruitment.
2. **France's dual role as former colonial power and security guarantor created irreconcilable tensions.** Sahel populations increasingly perceived French forces as propping up corrupt elites rather than protecting civilians.
3. **The "foreign solution" problem:** No amount of external military force can substitute for indigenous political settlements between states and their marginal communities.

### Investment Implication of the French Withdrawal

France had positioned 60+ years of post-colonial economic relationship — mining contracts, infrastructure investments, banking presence (through BNP Paribas, Société Générale, BCEAO), and development finance — on a security guarantee.

With the security guarantee gone:
- **French mining assets face contract renegotiation or nationalization risk.** Orano's Niger uranium operations are the flagship example. TotalEnergies has assets in Niger. These positions are now subject to resource nationalism pressure from governments that have no debt to France's military umbrella.
- **BCEAO (West African CFA franc zone) stress:** The CFA franc is still pegged to the euro and managed partly through the French Treasury. If AES states formally exit the CFA zone — discussed but not yet executed — it would be a monetary shock to eight countries with a combined GDP of ~$400B and would affect French banking exposure in the region significantly.

## 5. China's Role — Patient Capital and Quiet Positioning

China's approach to the Sahel is different from Russia's. Where Russia provides security (Wagner), China provides capital and infrastructure.

**China's Sahel positioning:**
- **Infrastructure financing:** China's ExIm Bank and China Development Bank have financed roads, dams, government buildings, and telecommunications infrastructure across Sahelian states.
- **Mining investment:** Chinese firms have significant positions in gold, manganese, and other minerals across West and Central Africa.
- **Telecommunications:** Huawei has built significant telecommunications infrastructure in Mali, Niger, and Burkina Faso — giving China intelligence collection capabilities that are a direct strategic concern for Western governments.
- **No alignment demand:** Like Russia, China offers infrastructure and investment with no governance conditionality.

**China's key difference from Russia:** China is a patient capital provider with a long-term horizon. Russia is extracting value in the short term via Wagner's mining concessions. China is building relationships that position it for the 2040-2060 period when African demographics and mineral demand may make Africa the most consequential continent for global resource supply chains.

**The BRI and CFA:** China has not moved aggressively to replace the CFA franc or ECOWAS monetary architecture — it doesn't need to. Economic penetration through investment is more durable than monetary system control.

## 6. Critical Minerals — The Investment Core

The Sahel/West Africa region contains deposits critical to the energy transition and technology supply chains:

| Mineral | Key Country | Global Significance | Current Risk Level |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Uranium** | Niger (major), Mali | ~5-15% of global supply; critical for EU nuclear fleet | HIGH — post-coup renegotiation risk, Orano suspension |
| **Gold** | Mali, Burkina Faso, Guinea | West Africa is 2nd largest gold-producing region globally | MODERATE-HIGH — jihadist operational disruption; resource nationalism |
| **Lithium** | Mali (Goulamina deposit) | One of largest undeveloped lithium spodumene deposits in Africa | MODERATE — Chinese JV (Ganfeng Lithium) operational; political risk elevated |
| **Manganese** | Burkina Faso | Steel production input | MODERATE |
| **Cobalt** | DRC (adjacent, not Sahel but affected by instability arc) | Critical battery mineral | HIGH — DRC instability; artisanal mining human rights risk |
| **Bauxite** | Guinea | World's largest reserves (~25% globally) | MODERATE — Guinea coup (2021); Simandou iron ore mega-project ongoing |

**The uranium case in depth:**

Niger's uranium was foundational to France's nuclear energy security. France gets ~70-75% of its electricity from nuclear power — the highest share of any country. French nuclear power plants run on uranium fuel, and until 2023 Niger was a significant supplier. The post-coup renegotiation and suspension of Orano operations did not immediately crater France's nuclear fuel supply — France holds multi-year fuel stockpiles, and has diversified toward Kazakhstan and Canada. But it does demonstrate that:

1. European nuclear energy security has a geopolitical supply chain risk that was not fully priced before 2023
2. Niger's ability to leverage its uranium for diplomatic and financial benefit is real — the AES governments understand this
3. Any future escalation (nationalization of Orano assets, new contract terms significantly disadvantageous to French interests) would be a material event for French nuclear energy economics and for the uranium price globally

**Investment read on uranium:** The post-coup Nigerien disruption is one of multiple supply-side constraints feeding the ongoing uranium bull case (others: Kazakhstan production concentration, Kazatomprom supply management). Long uranium via physical uranium funds (Sprott Physical Uranium Trust) or uranium miners (Cameco, NexGen, Paladin) remains one of the cleaner energy transition + geopolitical risk trades in the Prospectra framework.

## 7. European Migration Policy — The Hidden Transmission Channel

Here is the investment signal that most macro investors miss entirely:

**The Sahel collapse is a European political risk event.**

Sub-Saharan Africa's population is growing at approximately 2.5% per year — the fastest of any major region. The Sahel's population will roughly double by 2050 on current trajectories. Jihadist violence, climate stress, food insecurity, and the absence of economic opportunity are combining to produce migration pressure that moves northward — through Niger (historically a key transit country), Libya, and across the Mediterranean.

**Niger was critical to EU migration management:** The EU paid Niger approximately €1.5 billion between 2016 and 2023 under the Emergency Trust Fund for Africa and EU-Niger cooperation frameworks — specifically to manage migration flows through Niger, which is the primary transit corridor for sub-Saharan migrants heading toward Libya and the Mediterranean.

Post-coup Niger terminated its cooperation with the EU on migration management. The practical effect: migration through the Niger corridor has increased. This feeds directly into:

1. **European far-right political strength** — migration is the primary driver of far-right electoral gains across France, Germany, Italy, and Scandinavia. The Sahel collapse is a fuel supply for European political fragmentation.
2. **EU-Africa relations reframing** — the EU is being forced to reconsider its entire Africa strategy, from pure development finance toward an approach that incorporates security, migration, and economic opportunity simultaneously.
3. **French domestic politics** — France's failure in the Sahel has become a domestic political liability. The humiliation of troops being expelled from former colonies that France spent €15+ billion defending feeds Marine Le Pen's and Éric Zemmour's narratives. This is a live factor in French politics and — given France's weight in the EU — in European politics.

**Investment transmission:** European political fragmentation driven by migration strengthens far-right parties that are skeptical of EU integration, fiscal solidarity (ESM, EIB), and Ukraine military aid. If the Sahel continues to generate migration pressure, it is a structural headwind for European sovereign spread convergence, a tailwind for short EUR trades in risk-off scenarios, and a persistent uncertainty discount on EU institutional durability.

## 8. African Sovereign Debt — The Repricing

The Sahel coups and the broader geopolitical realignment have not happened in a vacuum. They coincide with the most significant stress in African sovereign debt since the debt crisis of the 1990s.

**The macro context:**
- African sovereigns issued Eurobonds aggressively during the low-rate era (2010-2021), often at 6-9% yields, on the basis of commodity revenue projections that assumed continued Chinese demand and stable global rates.
- The 2022 rate shock and USD strengthening created an acute refinancing cliff: when dollar rates rise, the servicing cost of dollar-denominated African sovereign debt in local currency terms surges. 
- **Zambia (2020), Ghana (2022), and Ethiopia (2021)** became the most visible sovereign debt restructurings; Kenya narrowly avoided default in 2024.

**How does Sahel geopolitical realignment affect African sovereign debt?**

1. **Withdrawal of Western development finance:** The World Bank, IMF, EU, and bilateral Western donors have reduced disbursements to coup governments (conditionality). This closes a key refinancing channel at the worst possible time.

2. **Loss of IMF program eligibility:** Countries like Mali and Burkina Faso that are under military governance outside democratic frameworks are effectively excluded from the IMF program pipeline — which means they cannot access the balance-of-payments support that most African frontier markets rely on as a backstop.

3. **Russian "alternative finance" is not comparable:** Russia does not have the capital to replace Western development finance at scale. China can partially offset — and has done so — but Chinese lending comes with commercial terms (export credits, resource-backed loans) that can deepen long-term debt sustainability problems.

4. **Contagion risk to ECOWAS cohesion:** If the AES states successfully decouple from ECOWAS and the CFA franc zone without catastrophic economic consequences, it creates a precedent that other West African states might consider. Nigeria, Senegal, Côte d'Ivoire — the ECOWAS anchor states — have so far held, but the CFA architecture is under genuine ideological pressure.

**Investment implication:** African frontier sovereign debt is in a period of structural repricing. The Sahel collapse is an amplifier, not the sole cause. **Côte d'Ivoire, Senegal, and Morocco** — states that have maintained democratic governance and Western partnerships — are distinguishing themselves from the coup belt. The spread differential between "stable democratic" and "coup-affected" African sovereigns has widened meaningfully and is a structural investment signal, not a transient one.

## 9. Investment Implications — Asset by Asset

### Gold

**Direct:** Burkina Faso and Mali are major African gold producers. Jihadist disruption to mine operations, road closures, and security costs are compressing margins for operations in insecure areas. Endeavour Mining, West African Resources, Hummingbird Resources, and smaller producers have flagged operational challenges.

**Systematic signal:** Burkina Faso/Mali production disruptions → marginal supply constraints → support for gold price, particularly in a period when Chinese demand is also structurally elevated.

**Portfolio view:** Gold miners with significant Sahelian exposure are trading at a discount to their production profile that partly reflects geopolitical risk premium. That premium is real. Do not underweight it. If you want gold exposure, prefer producers with diversified geographies (Canada, Australia, South Africa) over pure-play Sahelian exposure.

### Uranium

**Direct:** Niger disruption → reduced near-term supply → bullish uranium price signal.

**Portfolio view:** This reinforces the existing uranium long thesis. The Sahel adds a supply-side risk premium to the structural energy transition demand story. Cameco, NexGen Energy, and Sprott Physical Uranium Trust remain the preferred vehicles.

### African Eurobonds (Frontier Sovereign Debt)

**Differentiated view required:**
- AES member states (Mali, Burkina Faso, Niger): **Avoid**. Political exclusion from Western finance, jihadist operating environment, resource nationalism risk, no IMF backstop.
- Neighboring ECOWAS stable states (Côte d'Ivoire, Senegal, Ghana-post-restructuring): **Selective exposure on spread widening**. These sovereigns benefit from their contrast with the AES states, but they absorb migration and security externalities.
- Kenya and Nigeria: Large enough to trade independently of the Sahel narrative. Kenya's 2024 near-default crisis was resolved; Eurobond access restored. Nigeria is fundamentally an oil story with a fiscal management overlay.

### CFA Franc Zone

If AES states formally exit the CFA — not yet done, but discussed — it would be a monetary shock. Eight CFA franc countries (WAEMU) share the West African CFA franc, pegged to EUR. A partial exit would require:
1. Creation of new national currencies in AES states
2. Massive devaluation against the EUR (the AES governments would be unable to defend any new currency peg)
3. Inflation surge in AES states
4. Stress on the remaining WAEMU members

**Monitoring indicator:** Any formal legislative or executive move in Mali, Burkina Faso, or Niger to prepare for CFA exit. This would be a 3-6 month leading indicator of a currency crisis with regional contagion potential.

### European Political Risk (Indirect)

Migration from the Sahel feeds European far-right electoral performance. This is a **slow-moving but structural** tailwind for:
- Short EUR (European political uncertainty discount)
- Short European financials in specific scenarios (if ECB policy becomes politicized by fragmentation)
- Long defense equities (NATO spending pressures amplified by African instability)

## 10. Signal vs. Noise

**Signal:**
- **Niger uranium:** Any announcement from Nigerien government on Orano contract status, nationalization legislation, or new mining framework. Uranium price impact: immediate.
- **Burkina Faso security:** Monthly fatality data from ACLED (Armed Conflict Location & Event Data) — if attacks on mining corridors exceed 2x 12-month average, operational risk for gold producers is acute.
- **AES CFA exit legislation:** Any formal parliamentary discussion = high-impact signal.
- **ECOWAS summit outcomes:** If ECOWAS moves to impose hard sanctions or military intervention (Niger, 2023, did not result in this) — would reshape regional security calculus.
- **Wagner/Africa Corps troop movement:** Expansion to new countries (e.g., Senegal, Guinea-Bissau) signals further French-zone rollback.
- **EU migration corridor data:** Irregular crossings via Central Mediterranean (reported monthly by Frontex) — above 12-month average signals Sahel pressure transmitting to Europe.

**Noise:**
- Junta government statements about "successful security operations" in any Sahel country — these are systematically unreliable; always cross-reference with ACLED.
- French government statements about "maintaining influence" in Africa — the strategic reality is a managed retreat.
- Individual mining company press releases about "operational normalization" in insecure zones — always verify against security situation independently.
- ECOWAS communiqués on democratic transition timelines — the AES states have consistently ignored them.

## 11. CEO Portfolio Note

The Sahel collapse is three investment stories simultaneously:

**Story 1: Resource Nationalism Risk to Specific Extractive Positions**
If you hold uranium or gold via companies with concentrated Sahelian exposure, you hold an uncompensated geopolitical risk. The supply disruption upside for uranium price is real — but owning Orano-type exposure to Nigerien operations is not the right vehicle. Prefer diversified producers or physical commodity exposure.

**Story 2: African Sovereign Debt Differentiation**
The spread between "stable democratic" and "coup-affected" African sovereigns will likely remain elevated for years. This creates persistent carry opportunities in the stable tier. Ghana's post-restructuring Eurobonds (return to market), Côte d'Ivoire 10Y (Ba2/BB), and Senegal (Ba2/B+) represent the tier of African sovereign debt that benefits from the contrast. Entry on any broad EM risk-off widening.

**Story 3: The European Transmission Channel**
This is the most underpriced. The Sahel collapse is a sustained driver of European migration pressure, which is a sustained driver of European political fragmentation. The investment expression is not direct — but it reinforces the case for being cautious on EU institutional cohesion trades (spread convergence in peripheral European bonds depends on political solidarity that migration pressure erodes) and for maintaining defense sector exposure as European governments increase spending in response to multiple simultaneous threats.

**Prospectra's horizon note:** All three of these stories play out over 2–5 years. They are not trades. They are structural thesis elements that should inform portfolio tilts — toward resource-diversified miners, toward differentiated African EM, away from naive EU institutional trust — with patience.
