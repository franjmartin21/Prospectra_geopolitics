## Opening Question

Africa holds roughly **30% of the world's known mineral wealth** yet captures only **10% of global critical mineral revenues**. The continent sits atop the materials the 21st century runs on — cobalt, copper, lithium, manganese, graphite, platinum group metals — and it is simultaneously the most competed-over real estate on the planet and one of the most underweighted in institutional portfolios.

**The question:** Is that gap a failure of governance, a consequence of structural power imbalances, or is it about to close — and if it closes, who wins?

That is the central question of African geopolitics in 2026. Everything else — the security crises, the external power competition, the debt traps — is downstream of it.

---

## Core Concept: The Mineral Wealth Paradox

### The Resource Curse, Revisited

The classical "resource curse" thesis — that mineral wealth correlates with authoritarianism, conflict, and underdevelopment — was formulated in the 1990s observing oil-dependent states. It captured something real: when a government can fund itself from commodity rents rather than taxation, it has less incentive to build institutions, provide services, or negotiate with its own population.

But the resource curse is not destiny. Botswana used diamond revenues to build a functioning middle-income state. Namibia is successfully leveraging green hydrogen ambitions into infrastructure investment. Rwanda, with no major mineral wealth, has built a service economy and regional logistics hub. The curse is not geological — it is political and structural.

The 2020s have added a new layer: **geopolitical competition has dramatically increased the bargaining power of mineral-rich African states**, potentially rewriting the extraction-without-benefit equation that defined the colonial and post-colonial era. When both Washington and Beijing need cobalt for their energy transition and defense supply chains, the DRC's President Tshisekedi has leverage he would not have had in 2005.

Whether African governments can convert that leverage into structural economic gains — rather than better rents for the same extraction model — is the defining question.

The global energy transition — EVs, grid batteries, wind turbines — is structurally dependent on the DRC maintaining political stability and open export channels.

**This is a systemic vulnerability for the West that rivals Taiwan's semiconductor position, though it receives a fraction of the strategic attention.**

## The Three-Power Contest

### China: The Incumbent

China's position in African minerals is not simply commercial — it is strategically constructed over 25 years of state-directed investment. By 2025, Chinese firms held ownership stakes in **15 of the 19 major cobalt mines in the DRC**. CMOC (China Molybdenum Company) became the world's largest cobalt producer after its acquisition of the Tenke Fungurume and Kisanfu mines.

The model: Chinese firms mine, Chinese logistics move ore, Chinese refineries (Zhejiang, Guangdong) process, and Chinese battery makers (CATL, BYD) absorb output. Approximately **95% of the DRC's cobalt export goes to China** — not because African governments want it that way, but because China built the only industrial system that can absorb it.

This is what upstream dominance with downstream integration looks like. It is enormously difficult to disrupt without building an alternative refining system from scratch, which takes a decade and billions of dollars.

### The United States: The Latecomer

The US was largely absent from African mining for two decades while China was building positions. The Biden-era Minerals Security Partnership and the subsequent Trump administration's Section 232 investigation (Presidential Proclamation, January 14, 2026) have sharpened the strategic focus, but converting attention into supply chain reality is a slow process.

The 2026 DRC-US cobalt arrangement — a tripartite MoU between DRC state miner EGC, US-based EVelution Energy, and commodity house Trafigura — is the most concrete step yet. It signals Washington is willing to use state-backed mechanisms (DFC financing, bilateral diplomacy) rather than relying purely on private capital. But the volumes involved are small relative to Chinese-controlled production.

The US strategic problem: American companies are not willing to pay political risk premiums that Chinese state-owned enterprises are, because Chinese SOEs are not purely return-maximizing entities. The playing field is structurally uneven.

### The European Union: The Framework Builder

The EU's Critical Raw Materials Act (2024) and Global Gateway initiative have produced a flurry of strategic partnerships — with the DRC, Zambia, Namibia, Rwanda, and South Africa. The EU's bet is that rules, standards, and blended finance (mixing grants, concessional loans, and private capital) can build a more sustainable, less extractive partnership than China's model.

This is a credible long-term approach. It is also slow and underfunded relative to Chinese state investment. The EU cannot match Chinese infrastructure financing at scale — the TAZARA railway upgrade ($1.4B from China's CCECC, linking the Copperbelt to Dar es Salaam) illustrates the gap. The EU can offer better terms on a smaller number of strategic projects.

## Concrete Example: The DRC Cobalt Pivot of 2026

The DRC's moves this year illustrate how the mineral wealth paradox is starting to shift.

**October 2026 — The Quota Play:** Kinshasa lifted its blunt cobalt export ban (imposed in 2023 as a supply shock signal to Beijing) and replaced it with structured export quotas tied to domestic processing commitments. The logic: an export ban was self-defeating (it hurt DRC revenues without forcing refinery investment). Quotas tied to local value-add requirements create leverage while keeping revenues flowing.

**May 2026 — The US Deal:** The EGC-EVelution-Trafigura MoU created a channel for DRC cobalt to flow into US battery supply chains, partly outside the Chinese-controlled refinery system. It is not transformative at current scale, but it establishes a commercial template and signals that Kinshasa is actively diversifying its buyer base.

**The DRC-Zambia Battery Zone:** Both governments are constructing a cross-border special economic zone designed to retain value-add locally — moving from raw ore export to cathode precursor and eventually cell manufacturing. This is ambitious. It requires sustained political will, infrastructure investment, and anchor customers committed to African-sourced, African-processed materials.

**What it tells us:** African governments are more sophisticated actors than the passive rent-takers the classic resource curse model assumed. They are using the geopolitical moment — genuine competition between China, the US, and EU — to extract better terms. The question is whether they can translate leverage on paper into structural industrial development.

## Investment Implications

### Framework: Three Layers of Exposure

**Layer 1 — Commodity Prices (most direct, least control)**

Cobalt, copper, and lithium prices are directly affected by African supply dynamics. The DRC's export policy creates price volatility. Cobalt in particular has been chronically oversupplied post-2022 (Chinese mine expansion drove the price collapse); the 2026 quota regime is an attempt to restore pricing power. Watch for:
- **Cobalt recovery thesis:** If the DRC quota holds and Chinese refinery builds slow, cobalt could re-rate from multi-year lows. Historically cobalt has shown extreme volatility (5x swings in a cycle).
- **Copper structural bull:** The Copperbelt grades are superior to most alternatives. DRC + Zambia copper production matters for the long-term grid build-out thesis.

**Layer 2 — Miners with African Exposure**

| Company | Exposure | Consideration |
|---|---|---|
| Glencore (GLEN.L) | DRC cobalt + Zambia copper dominant | Direct play on Copperbelt thesis; political risk priced in |
| Ivanhoe Mines (IVN.TO) | DRC (Kamoa-Kakula — world-class copper) | Premium asset, premium valuation |
| CMOC (603993.SS) | DRC cobalt + copper; Chinese entity | China-listed, geopolitical risk on US side |
| Eurasian Resources Group (private) | DRC, Kazakhstan; Russian-linked | Not investable from Western portfolio POV |
| First Quantum Minerals (FM.TO) | Zambia copper (Sentinel, Kansanshi) | Political risk history (Panama was the lesson) |

**Key principle:** African mining positions require a **political risk premium** that most Western DCF models systematically underestimate. License revocations, export bans, and windfall taxes are not tail risks — they are cyclical events. Build them into position sizing.

**Layer 3 — The Infrastructure and Services Ecosystem**

The less discussed but potentially more attractive opportunity: the infrastructure and logistics build-out that mineral competition is funding.

- **Port and rail infrastructure:** Tanzania's Dar es Salaam port, the TAZARA line, Lobito Corridor (DRC-Zambia-Angola) are all receiving capital from competing external powers. Companies with EPC, logistics, and digital infrastructure exposure benefit.
- **Financial services:** African Development Bank-linked instruments, blended finance vehicles, and EM sovereign bonds from mineral-rich states (Zambia, DRC, Namibia) are becoming more interesting as revenues potentially stabilize.

**The CEO's directional view:**

The medium-term setup (12–24 months) favors:
1. **Long copper via quality African-exposed miners** (Kamoa-Kakula is the world's highest-grade undeveloped copper district). The EV + grid transition copper demand is structural; African supply is the marginal ton.
2. **Selective cobalt recovery exposure** — not a full position, but the quota experiment deserves monitoring. If supply discipline holds, the downside is well-priced at current levels.
3. **Avoid** DRC-sovereign-linked instruments until governance indicators improve. The country has the resources; it does not yet have the institutional stability to backstop credit.

**Timeframe for the structural story:** 5–10 years. This is not a 2027 trade. The industrial development thesis — DRC actually hosting battery precursor manufacturing — is a decade story if it works at all. Invest in the minerals and the best-positioned miners; do not get ahead of the industrialization narrative.
