## Opening Question

Simultaneously, Bloomberg is running a headline about "diplomatic tensions rising." A geopolitical trader in London just shorted crude. A sovereign wealth fund in Singapore just bought it.

**Who is right? And more importantly — why did they reach opposite conclusions from the same event?**

The answer is almost never about intelligence — it's about the *stack* they're processing it through. Today's lesson is about building yours.

## 1. The Problem: Information Abundance, Signal Scarcity

We live in the era of maximum geopolitical noise. In 1990, a serious analyst could read the *Financial Times*, track State Department cables (with a lag), and have a reasonable picture of global risk. Today:

- **GDELT** ingests 100,000+ news articles per day in 65 languages
- **Twitter/X** generates geopolitical commentary in real-time from 10,000+ accounts
- **Substack** has produced an explosion of geopolitical commentary — much of it confident, most of it wrong
- Every bank has a geopolitical risk team publishing weekly notes
- Every think tank publishes "analysis" that is actually advocacy

The paradox: *more information produces worse decisions* unless you have a structured system to filter it.

**The Investment-Grade Information Problem:** Most geopolitical information is structured to explain the past, not anticipate the future. The analyst's job — your job — is to find the narrow class of signals that contain *forward-looking pricing implications* that the market has not yet incorporated.

## 2. The Geopolitical Intelligence Stack: Five Layers

Think of your information infrastructure as a five-layer stack, from raw to refined:

### Layer 1: Primary Sources (Tier 1 — Highest Signal)
These are the documents and statements that *create* geopolitical reality, not describe it:

| Source | What to Watch | Investment Signal |
|---|---|---|
| **UN Security Council resolutions** | Draft language, veto patterns, abstentions | Multilateral consensus on sanctions, peacekeeping, arms embargo |
| **Central bank minutes** (Fed, ECB, PBoC, BoJ) | Geopolitical language in economic justification | CB governors naming geopolitical risk = it's priced by policy |
| **G7/G20 communiqués** | Language evolution on China, Russia, trade | Consensus forming on economic statecraft |
| **IMF Article IV consultations** | Country risk assessments, reserve adequacy | Pre-crisis warning for EM sovereign risk |
| **BIS quarterly reports** | Cross-border lending flows, dollar funding stress | Systemic liquidity warning before visible crisis |
| **Congressional Budget Justifications** (U.S.) | Defense spending line items, country-specific | Long-horizon signaling of U.S. strategic priorities |
| **National Security Strategies** (U.S., EU, China) | Competitor framing, sector protection language | Which industries are being militarized |

**Why primary sources are underused:** They require reading long, technical documents and translating jargon into implications. Most market participants outsource this to analysts — which creates alpha for those who don't.

### Layer 2: Institutional Research (Tier 1 — High Signal, Low Noise)
These institutions have access, mandates, and incentives to be *accurate* — not popular:

- **RAND Corporation** — U.S. defense and policy scenarios (not advocacy)
- **IISS (London)** — Military Balance annual, arms flows, conflict mapping
- **Bruegel (Brussels)** — European economic statecraft, sanctions design
- **Chatham House** — Energy geopolitics, Middle East/Russia depth
- **Carnegie Endowment** — EM political risk, nuclear proliferation
- **PIIE (Peterson Institute)** — Trade economics, sanctions effectiveness
- **Bank for International Settlements** — Financial stability, cross-border flows

**How to use them:** Don't read everything. Build a keyword alert system. These institutions often publish working papers *months* before a geopolitical issue becomes market-relevant.

### Layer 3: Premium Data Services (Quantitative Signal)
These services turn geopolitical text into numbers:

| Service | What It Provides | Databricks Integration |
|---|---|---|
| **GDELT Project** (free) | Event counts, tone, actor networks by country/day | Direct CSV ingestion; event-to-asset lag analysis |
| **ACLED** (free for research) | Conflict events with geographic precision | Conflict intensity → commodity supply disruption scoring |
| **IAEA Safety Reports** | Nuclear facility incident data | Uranium/energy supply signal |
| **OECD Trade in Value Added** | Supply chain exposure by sector/country | Trade war impact mapping |
| **WTO Dispute Settlement** | Active trade disputes with timelines | Sector tariff risk tracker |
| **FRED (St. Louis Fed)** | Macro + geopolitical policy rates, reserves | Dollar stress index construction |

**The free intelligence edge:** GDELT alone, properly processed, contains more forward-looking signal than most $50,000/year advisory subscriptions. The edge isn't buying better data — it's *processing* public data with better structure.

### Layer 4: Expert Networks (Qualitative Depth)
No model replaces judgment from people who have been in the room:

- **Former diplomats / ambassadors** — Especially those from countries currently in geopolitical tension. Their reading of signaling vs. posturing is irreplaceable.
- **Ex-central bankers** — On CB independence, political pressure, reserve management
- **Sector specialists** — Ex-OPEC economists on oil policy, ex-Pentagon on defense procurement
- **Local journalists** (not wire services) — Country-level primary reporting, often months ahead of major outlets

**Access model:** Academic conferences, think tank events (many are free or low-cost), LinkedIn outreach, and increasingly, specialist Substacks where practitioners write independently.

**Warning:** Expert networks are high-signal but high-bias. Former officials often rationalize their former institution's decisions. Build a habit of asking: *what would make this expert's view wrong?*

### Layer 5: Market Intelligence (Reflexive Signal)
Markets *are* intelligence aggregators — with systematic biases:

- **Options markets / implied volatility by country** — The market's current geopolitical risk premium
- **CDS spreads** (credit default swaps) — Sovereign credit market's political risk assessment
- **Currency positioning** (COT reports, EM FX flows) — Where institutional money is moving ahead of events
- **Commodity curve structure** — Backwardation/contango tells you supply/demand expectation, not fear

**The reflexive trap:** When market intelligence becomes your primary source, you're a price-taker, not a signal-reader. Use Layer 5 to *calibrate* your thesis (is the market already pricing what you see?) — not to generate it.

## 3. The Filtering Framework: SIFT for Geopolitical Information

Before any piece of intelligence enters your decision-making system, run it through SIFT:

**S — Source Authority:** Who produced this? What is their institutional incentive? A defense contractor funding a think tank report on "rising threats" is not independent analysis.

**I — Independence:** Is this signal or an echo? The same Reuters dispatch quoted by 50 outlets is one data point, not 50.

**F — Falsifiability:** Does this claim make a prediction that can be tested? "China is becoming more aggressive" is not falsifiable. "China will increase patrols in the Taiwan Strait by Q4" is.

**T — Timing Relevance:** Is this new information, or is it a restatement of what the market already knows? The investment value of a piece of intelligence is directly correlated with how *new* it is relative to market pricing.

## 4. Historical Case Study: The 2022 Russia-Ukraine Intelligence Failure

In late 2021, the U.S. government began publicly warning that Russia was massing forces for an invasion. The intelligence was declassified and shared openly. European governments and most market participants dismissed it as American hyperbole.

**Why the failure?**

Most analysts were operating primarily from Layer 5 (market intelligence) — European gas prices and Russian equities weren't pricing imminent invasion risk, so analysts concluded the U.S. was wrong.

**What did the signal-readers do?**

A minority of analysts — primarily those with access to Layers 1 and 2 — noticed:
1. **U.S. Congressional Budget Justifications** had been quietly increasing Ukraine security assistance for 18 months
2. **IISS Military Balance** showed Russian logistics positioning inconsistent with an "exercise"
3. **GDELT tone data** on Russian diplomatic language toward Ukraine had been deteriorating for 6 months
4. **IMF Article IV** for Ukraine flagged reserve adequacy risk in ways consistent with pre-conflict preparation

The information was publicly available. The market didn't process it because most participants don't have a structured stack for doing so.

**The investment result:** Those who built positions in European defense equities (Thales, Rheinmetall, Leonardo), natural gas (TTF), and Ukrainian sovereign shorts in Q4 2021 captured one of the most historically significant geopolitical trades of the decade.

## 5. Investment Implications

### The Intelligence Arbitrage Thesis
Geopolitical intelligence creates investment opportunities through *information processing asymmetries* — not information secrecy. The edge is structural and repeatable:

1. **Most investors don't read primary sources.** Diplomatic language, UN voting patterns, CB committee minutes — these are publicly available and systematically underanalyzed.

2. **Most investors overweight recent, dramatic information.** A missile test gets covered for 72 hours. The quiet expansion of a port facility — building power projection capability — gets no coverage for 2 years, then is "suddenly" strategically relevant.

3. **Most investors conflate narrative with signal.** "Experts say X" is not a signal unless X is falsifiable and hasn't been priced.

### Directional Framework by Layer
| If Your Edge Comes From... | Likely Horizon | Likely Asset Class |
|---|---|---|
| Layer 1 (primary source shifts) | 6–18 months | Sector equities, sovereign bonds |
| Layer 2 (institutional research) | 3–12 months | Commodities, EM FX |
| Layer 3 (GDELT/quantitative) | 1–6 months | Commodities, volatility |
| Layer 4 (expert networks) | Varies by expert | Position sizing, conviction |
| Layer 5 (market positioning) | Days–weeks | Risk management only |

**Principle:** Build your thesis from Layers 1–3. Validate with Layer 4. Calibrate with Layer 5. Never reverse the order.
