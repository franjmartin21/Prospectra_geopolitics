## Part I: What Jackson Hole Actually Is (and Why the Papers Matter More Than You Think)

The Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City has hosted the Jackson Hole Economic Symposium annually since 1978. The initial purpose was modest — invite a few economists, discuss agricultural policy in a scenic location, try to get Paul Volcker to attend. Volcker came in 1982, and the event was never small again.

**The structural design:**

The symposium runs Thursday–Saturday, typically the last full week of August. The structure is:
- **Thursday:** Academic paper presentations. Researchers present papers commissioned months in advance by the KC Fed. These are not opinion pieces — they are full economic research papers, peer-reviewed by discussants, and the topic selection is deliberate.
- **Friday morning:** The Fed Chair keynote. This is the market-moving event.
- **Friday afternoon and Saturday:** Panel discussions, often with international central bankers (ECB, BOE, BOJ, BIS, EM central bank governors).

The papers are published online the morning they are presented. By convention, markets get access to them at the same time as the conference participants.

**Why the papers are undervalued by markets:**

Most market participants — including most professional investors — treat Jackson Hole as a single event: the Friday morning keynote. The papers are treated as academic noise. This creates a systematic gap: the papers tell you, 24 hours before the keynote, what intellectual framework the Fed Chair is working within.

The relationship is not coincidental. The KC Fed curates papers in conversation with the Fed Chair's office. The topics chosen for any given year reflect what the Fed's internal intellectual debates center on. A symposium themed around "supply-side inflation persistence" signals a different policy function than one themed around "labor market transformation." The Chair's keynote is the applied statement of the intellectual agenda the papers represent.

**Historical examples of paper → keynote signal:**

| Year | Symposium Theme | Key Paper | Keynote Signal Embedded in Theme |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2005 | "The Greenspan Era: Lessons for the Future" | Raghuram Rajan's "Has Financial Development Made the World Riskier?" | Embedded early warning of financial fragility Greenspan was dismissing |
| 2010 | "Macroeconomic Challenges" | Bernanke's QE2 signal | The paper environment primed the dovish turn |
| 2022 | "Reassessing Constraints on the Economy and Policy" | Papers on inflation regime shifts | Powell's 8-minute, deliberately brief, hawkish speech: the framework was already set |
| 2023 | "Structural Shifts in the Global Economy" | Papers on supply-side structural change | Powell's "higher for longer" framing aligned exactly with the structural persistence themes |

The 2022 case is instructive. Powell spoke for only 8 minutes — the shortest Jackson Hole keynote in memory. Why? Because the intellectual case for the policy shift had been made in the papers. He didn't need to explain the framework; he just needed to announce the commitment. The papers did the heavy lifting.

## Part II: How to Read a Jackson Hole Paper for Policy Signal

A Jackson Hole paper is not a central bank statement. It uses academic language, citations, and regression tables. But embedded in it are clues about what the central bank views as the operative constraints on policy.

**The five questions to ask of any Jackson Hole paper:**

**1. What is the problem being named?**
The problem a paper is asked to analyze is the problem the Fed believes is real and important. If the paper is about "the neutral rate in a fragmented global economy," the Fed is worried that r* has shifted and they don't know where it is. That uncertainty biases them toward caution and data-dependence — Scenario B or D in our matrix.

**2. What are the policy levers the paper discusses?**
If the paper discusses forward guidance skeptically, it signals the Chair agrees. If it discusses QE as a primary tool, the Chair is pre-positioning for its future use. Papers are not neutral on policy instruments; they advocate implicitly.

**3. Who are the discussants?**
The KC Fed chooses discussants who will push back on the papers. If the discussants are all inflation hawks, the paper is either hawkish itself or the conference wants to stress-test a dovish framework. If the discussants are known doves, the opposite.

**4. What is NOT in the program?**
Absence of topics is as informative as presence. If no paper addresses dollar hegemony in a year when the dollar's reserve status is under genuine pressure, the Fed is signaling it does not view this as an immediate policy variable. If no paper addresses labor market slack in a year when unemployment has risen, the Fed is prioritizing inflation over employment in its actual thinking.

**5. What do the international central bank papers say?**
The ECB and BOJ typically present research at Jackson Hole. If their papers are thematically aligned with the Fed's — all focused on structural inflation, or all focused on neutral rate uncertainty — there is a global central bank convergence signal. If they diverge (Fed hawkish, ECB dovish), policy divergence is ahead, and that is a currency and fixed income trade.

## Part III: The 2026 Jackson Hole Intellectual Agenda — What We Know and What to Look For

Based on the macro environment entering the symposium and the intellectual evolution of the prior 24 months, here is the likely agenda architecture and what to watch:

**The probable symposium theme for 2026:**

The 2025 symposium addressed the "soft landing" problem — whether the Fed had achieved it and what the terminal rate implied. By 2026, the intellectual debate has shifted. The operative questions entering this symposium are:

1. **What is the neutral rate in a geopolitically fragmented world?** The r* debate (the neutral interest rate that neither stimulates nor restricts the economy) has been central to Fed policy since 2023. But r* estimates have been revised upward repeatedly. The geopolitical fragmentation thesis (supply chain disruption → structural inflationary bias → higher neutral rate) directly affects what the "right" rate is. This is the most likely central intellectual question.

2. **Has the post-pandemic inflation been a structural break or a cyclical episode?** The answer determines whether the Fed can return to pre-2020 policy norms or whether the 2–3% inflation era is over. This is an existential question for the framework.

3. **How does geopolitical risk price into monetary policy?** This is newer and connects directly to our curriculum. If supply shocks caused by geopolitical events (energy embargoes, trade wars, shipping disruptions) create persistent inflation, should central banks respond differently to geopolitical inflation than to demand-driven inflation? This is the academic question that our entire GSI framework maps onto.

**What to look for when the papers drop Thursday morning:**

- **Keywords signaling Scenario A (hawkish):** "Persistent inflation expectations," "supply-side structural constraint," "risk of premature easing," "credibility imperative"
- **Keywords signaling Scenario B (conditional):** "Balanced risk," "data-dependent assessment," "labor market moderation," "gradual normalization path"
- **Keywords signaling Scenario C (dovish):** "Inflation sustainably converging," "appropriate recalibration," "policy space for adjustment," "demand restraint achieved"
- **Keywords signaling Scenario D (ambiguity/financial stability):** "Neutral rate uncertainty," "macro-financial stability tradeoffs," "endogenous risk," "non-linear dynamics"

**A structural note on Warsh and the academic agenda:**

Kevin Warsh is unusual among Fed chairs in one specific dimension: he has written more about the Fed's communication framework and institutional credibility than almost any other Fed governor in recent history. His pre-chair career included explicit critiques of forward guidance as a tool. This means he is likely to be highly attentive to how the academic papers frame the credibility question — and his keynote may directly engage with the paper themes in a way that previous chairs have not.

Watch for: does his keynote cite specific papers from Thursday? If yes, he is telling you which intellectual framework he has adopted. If he ignores them entirely, he has already made his policy decision independently of the academic debate — which is itself a signal about how he runs the institution.

## Part IV: The International Panel — Reading the Global Divergence Signal

The Friday afternoon and Saturday sessions at Jackson Hole include international central bankers. This is where geopolitical and monetary signals converge most visibly.

**Why the international panels matter now more than ever:**

In the pre-2020 world, Jackson Hole international panels were largely ceremonial — a display of coordinated credibility. Post-2022, they have become substantive, because monetary policy divergence is now a geopolitical variable.

**The three divergences to watch:**

**1. Fed vs. ECB divergence:**
If the ECB is cutting while the Fed is holding, the euro weakens, the dollar strengthens, and EM FX faces dollar-denominated debt pressure. Listen for the ECB president's framing: are they positioning for convergence with the Fed or are they comfortable with divergence?

**2. Fed vs. BOJ divergence:**
The BOJ's rate normalization has been the carry trade trigger we tracked in Lessons 240–241. If the BOJ signals at Jackson Hole that they are pausing normalization to wait for the Fed to cut first, the carry trade unwind is postponed — yen stays weaker, carry positions stay intact. If the BOJ signals continued normalization regardless of the Fed, USD/JPY will be the most volatile currency pair of the fall.

**3. EM central banks' implicit message:**
EM central bank governors attend but rarely speak publicly. However, the bilateral meetings (which are reported afterward) signal whether EM central banks are facing capital flow pressure from the Fed's stance. A hawkish Fed that keeps rates high creates capital outflow pressure for EM — the dollar stays strong, EM FX stays weak, EM sovereign spreads stay wide. This is the mechanism our GSI-linked EM FX position depends on.
