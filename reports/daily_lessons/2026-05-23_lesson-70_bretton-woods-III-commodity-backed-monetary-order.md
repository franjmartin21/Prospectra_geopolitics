## Core Concept: The Pozsar Thesis

In March 2022, two weeks after the West froze $300 billion of Russia's foreign exchange reserves following the invasion of Ukraine, Zoltan Pozsar — then head of short-term interest rate strategy at Credit Suisse — published a note titled *"Bretton Woods III."* It became one of the most discussed macro frameworks of the decade.

His argument in stripped-down form:

| Era | Name | Foundation |
|---|---|---|
| 1944–1971 | Bretton Woods I | Dollar backed by gold. **Outside money.** |
| 1971–2022 | Bretton Woods II | Dollar backed by U.S. Treasuries — claims on the U.S. government's future taxation. **Inside money.** |
| 2022–? | Bretton Woods III | Emerging order where commodity-rich nations demand commodity-backed settlement — gold, oil, critical minerals — rather than paper claims. |

**The key distinction: inside money vs. outside money.**

- **Inside money:** Claims on institutions — bank deposits, T-bills, Treasuries. Can be frozen, seized, or inflated away. Requires trusting the counterparty.
- **Outside money:** Physical commodities, gold. No counterparty risk. Cannot be frozen. Requires trusting physics, not politics.

The thesis is not that the dollar collapses. It is that the **marginal direction of global reserve management has permanently shifted** toward outside money — driven by geopolitical distrust, not economic calculation. The freezing of Russian reserves was the forcing event. It demonstrated to every commodity-exporting nation that dollar reserves are a geopolitical bet, not a neutral financial asset.

## Historical Grounding: France, 1965

The precedent is France under Charles de Gaulle, advised by economist Jacques Rueff.

De Gaulle understood that Bretton Woods I gave the U.S. an "exorbitant privilege" — America could print dollars to finance deficits; the rest of the world had to earn them through exports. The system was asymmetric. France began systematically converting its dollar reserves into gold at the Fed's redemption window. By 1971, enough countries had done this that President Nixon faced a choice: run out of gold or break the system. He chose to break the system. On August 15, 1971, the gold window closed.

The system did not end because economists decided it was theoretically flawed. It ended because geopolitical distrust of American fiscal discipline became operational.

**2022 is the same dynamic with a different forcing event.** The geopolitical distrust was not about American fiscal discipline this time — it was about American willingness to weaponize the reserve asset. And the lesson was learned immediately:

- **Russia:** Pivoted aggressively to gold, RMB, and bilateral settlement rails
- **China:** Accelerated development of CIPS (Cross-border Interbank Payment System) as SWIFT alternative
- **Saudi Arabia (2023):** Announced willingness to accept non-dollar payment for oil — the first public break from petrodollar convention since 1974
- **China-Brazil (2023):** Signed bilateral trade agreement in local currencies, bypassing dollar clearing
- **India:** Negotiated oil purchases from Russia in rupees and rubles
- **Central banks globally (2022–2024):** Bought gold at the fastest pace since 1967

None of these individually ends the dollar system. Together, they describe a structural shift at the margin — exactly the mechanism Pozsar identified.

## The Investment Implications

If Bretton Woods III is directionally correct — even at a slow pace — it has three major portfolio implications:

### 1. Gold: The Reserve Asset of the New Order

The buyers driving the post-2022 gold rally are not Western hedge funds or retail investors. They are sovereign central banks: PBOC, Reserve Bank of India, Turkish Central Bank, National Bank of Poland, Monetary Authority of Singapore. This is **geopolitically-driven sovereign demand** — structural, not cyclical.

The mechanism: central banks moving from inside money (Treasuries) to outside money (gold) creates a buyer that doesn't ask "what's the yield?" They ask "can this be frozen?" Gold answers "no." Treasuries answer "yes, it has happened."

**Directional view: Long gold. 6–18 month minimum horizon. Buyers are structural; the thesis holds as long as geopolitical fragmentation persists.**

### 2. Commodities: The New Collateral

If commodity-exporting nations demand settlement in kind or in commodity-linked currencies, commodities stop being only consumption inputs and start being reserve assets. Commodity-linked currencies — AUD, CAD, BRL, CLP, MXN, ZAR — get a structural bid. Energy and critical mineral producers see their bargaining power increase relative to financial centers.

**Directional view: Long commodity-linked equities and currencies on a 2–5 year horizon. The repricing is slow but structural.**

### 3. Dollar: Slow Erosion, Not Collapse

This is the most important nuance. Bretton Woods III is **not** a dollar collapse thesis. The dollar remains the dominant reserve currency — there is no alternative at scale. The RMB is not freely convertible. Gold cannot provide the liquidity a modern financial system requires. Bitcoin has no institutional backing.

What Bretton Woods III predicts is **share erosion**: from roughly 60% of global reserves in 2015, to ~57% today, to perhaps 45–50% over a decade. That is slow enough not to crash markets, but fast enough to matter for portfolio construction at our horizon.

**The countervailing view:** Many serious economists argue the dollar's dominance is structural — depth of U.S. capital markets, rule of law, no alternative. They may be right about pace but wrong about direction.

**How to hold both views:** Position for the direction (gold, commodities, commodity currencies) with a horizon long enough to survive being early. The cost of being 3 years early in gold is the opportunity cost of alternatives. The cost of being 3 years late if the thesis is right is far larger.

## The Feedback Loop Nobody Is Talking About

Here is the mechanism that makes Bretton Woods III self-reinforcing if it accelerates:

1. Central banks sell U.S. Treasuries to buy gold
2. Treasury yields rise as demand falls
3. U.S. government financing costs increase (deficit already ~$2T/year)
4. To finance the deficit without foreign buyers, Fed must buy Treasuries (monetization)
5. Dollar inflation rises, eroding real value of remaining dollar reserves
6. Remaining dollar reserve holders see confirmation that dollar is losing store-of-value function
7. Return to step 1 — cycle accelerates

This is a **negative feedback loop on the dollar** that doesn't require a single triggering event — it compounds slowly. The IMF COFER data is the early warning system. When dollar reserve share falls below ~55%, watch for acceleration.

- Published quarterly at data.imf.org/COFER
