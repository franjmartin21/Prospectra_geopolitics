## Opening Framing Question

*Before we begin, sit with this paradox:*

In April 2025, the US imposed 104% tariffs on Chinese goods. China immediately matched with 84% on US goods, then raised to 125%. The US raised again. By mid-April, the effective combined tariff wall between the world's two largest economies was pushing 200%+ — meaning bilateral trade in most goods had effectively stopped.

At that point, both sides were imposing massive costs on their own economies with diminishing strategic return. Both knew the other side knew this. **And yet neither moved first to de-escalate.**

Why? If two rational actors both prefer "mutual low tariffs" over "mutual high tariffs," why do they keep escalating? This is not a failure of rationality. It is rational behavior inside a poorly designed game. Today we learn to read the game — so you can predict where it goes, and position before the market does.

## Core Concept: The Trade War as a Prisoner's Dilemma

### The Basic Structure

Game theory offers a precise vocabulary for understanding why escalation happens even when both parties would prefer to avoid it.

The **Prisoner's Dilemma** structure:

|  | Partner: Cooperate (low tariffs) | Partner: Defect (raise tariffs) |
|---|---|---|
| **You: Cooperate** | Both gain — mutual free trade | You lose big (open market + their closed market) |
| **You: Defect** | You gain temporarily (protection + their openness) | Both lose — trade war |

If you cannot trust your partner's commitment, the rational move is always to defect — to protect your market before they protect theirs. This is the logic that produces trade wars even when both parties know free trade is the superior outcome in aggregate. The structure of the game, not irrationality, drives escalation.

**The key insight:** A trade war is not a negotiation failure. It is what rational actors do when they don't trust each other's commitments. To end a trade war, you don't just need better policy — you need a **credible commitment mechanism**.

### What Makes the Game Harder in Practice

Three factors make the trade war game more dangerous than the simple Prisoner's Dilemma:

**1. Asymmetric Vulnerability**
Countries are not equally hurt by trade restriction. The US runs a persistent trade deficit with China, which means China exports more to the US than the US exports to China. A dollar-for-dollar retaliation hurts China more in nominal terms — China literally runs out of US goods to tariff at lower volumes. This creates asymmetric escalation dynamics: the US has more retaliatory runway. China must compensate with non-tariff measures (regulatory pressure, export controls on critical minerals, financial market restrictions).

**2. Domestic Political Lock-In**
Once a government imposes tariffs, domestic industries restructure to benefit from protection. Steel producers invest in capacity. Workers get hired. Political constituencies form around the protection. This raises the *exit cost* of de-escalation: even if the economics favor removing tariffs, the political economy of removing them is much harder. This is why WTO Appellate Body rulings against US steel tariffs (Section 232) were simply ignored — the political cost of compliance exceeded the political cost of non-compliance.

**3. Signaling and Reputation**
Each move in a trade war is watched not just by the immediate partner but by all other trading partners. If the US backs down from China under pressure, it signals to the EU, India, Japan, and everyone else that US tariff threats are negotiable. This creates a **reputation externality**: de-escalating with one partner can trigger demands from others. This is why trade wars often last longer than economics alone would predict.

## The Escalation Ladder: Three Stages and Their Investment Signatures

### Stage 1 — Signaling (Low cost, high optionality)
Tariff *threats*, USTR investigations, public statements. No actual tariffs imposed. This is the cheapest phase for both sides. Investment signature: elevated volatility in exposed sectors, but no fundamental re-rating. Markets often overreact — creating a **buy-the-dip opportunity** in high-quality companies with manageable tariff exposure.

### Stage 2 — Initial Imposition (Moderate cost, selective targeting)
Tariffs imposed, initially on politically calculated goods. Classic US targeting: goods sourced from swing-state-relevant industries get protected; retaliation is aimed at US exports from key political districts (soybeans from Iowa, bourbon from Kentucky, Harley-Davidsons from Wisconsin). Investment signature: sector-specific disruption. Supply chains begin rerouting. Companies with flexible sourcing (multiple EM suppliers) outperform those dependent on a single origin.

### Stage 3 — Escalation (High cost, self-reinforcing)
Tit-for-tat cycles. Both sides have exhausted targeted goods and move to broad coverage. Effective tariff rates exceed 50–100%. Investment signature: structural shift. Supply chains don't just reroute — they rebuild. This takes 18–36 months. Companies that move fast (Vietnam, India, Mexico operations) are permanent winners; those that don't face margin compression for years.

### The De-escalation Trigger: What Breaks the Cycle

History shows de-escalation requires one of four conditions:
1. **Shared external shock** — a recession, pandemic, or war creates a political window. Both sides need economic relief; trade truce provides cover.
2. **Domestic political shift** — an election removes the leader who staked their identity to escalation. The new leader can de-escalate without losing face (it's the predecessor's problem).
3. **Asymmetric pain reaching threshold** — one side's economy deteriorates faster. The weaker side makes concessions before the stronger side needs to.
4. **Credible commitment mechanism** — a verifiable deal structure (a formal treaty, third-party enforcement, phased concessions with automatic snapback) makes cooperation self-enforcing. This is rarest but most durable.

## Historical Grounding: Smoot-Hawley and the 1930s as the Worst Case

The canonical worst-case study is the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act of 1930.

**What happened:** The US raised tariffs on over 20,000 imported goods to record levels (average ~45%). The stated goal: protect American farmers and manufacturers during the early Depression.

**The retaliation:** Within months, 25 countries retaliated. Canada, the US's largest trading partner, immediately imposed tariffs on 30% of US imports. The UK, Germany, France, and others followed. Global trade collapsed: US exports fell from $5.4 billion in 1929 to $2.1 billion by 1933 — a 61% decline.

**The mechanism:** Each country's retaliation hurt American exporters, who lobbied for more protection, which triggered more retaliation. The cycle was self-reinforcing and politically locked in. No coordination mechanism existed to break it.

**The investment lesson (1930–1933):** Every asset class was crushed — this is not a clean "sector rotation" story. But within the devastation, relative winners existed: gold (currency debasement hedge), domestic consumer staples (insulated from trade), US agriculture that had domestic demand support, and ultimately — after the 1933 policy pivot — infrastructure and materials benefiting from the New Deal.

**The key difference today:** The WTO, G20 coordination frameworks, and the modern Fed swap line architecture create exit mechanisms that 1930 lacked. The worst case is recoverable. But these mechanisms only work if governments choose to use them — and that choice is political, not economic.

## Concrete Example: The 2025–2026 US-China Escalation Arc

This is the live case study we're living through.

**Timeline:**
- **Feb 2025:** US imposes 10% tariffs on all Chinese imports citing fentanyl
- **March 2025:** Additional 10% brings total to 20%; China retaliates with export controls on rare earth processing
- **April 2025 ("Liberation Day"):** US announces reciprocal tariffs globally, China at 34% added; China responds with 34% across the board
- **April 9, 2025:** US raises China to 104%; China raises US to 84%, then 125%
- **April 9 pause:** US announces 90-day pause for all partners *except* China — leaving China facing 145%+ effective rates
- **Mid-2025 truce:** Trump-Xi backchannel negotiations. Partial rollback on both sides. Effective rates reduced but remain far above 2024 levels
- **2026 IEEPA ruling:** US Supreme Court partially restricts IEEPA-based tariff authority; administration shifts to Section 232 and Section 301 authority
- **2026 Canada escalation:** US-Canada tensions spike on separate issues; 50% tariffs on $20bn of Canadian goods; Canada matches dollar-for-dollar. A new front opens.

**What the game theory predicts correctly:**
- Both sides escalated past the economically rational threshold (Stage 3) before de-escalating
- De-escalation came via domestic political shift on both sides (trade narrative became economically costly) plus a shared interest in financial market stability (both stock markets cratered during peak escalation)
- Non-tariff retaliation (China's rare earth export controls, regulatory pressure on Apple/Tesla in China) was the asymmetric compensation for China's lower tariff runway
- The truce is fragile because no credible commitment mechanism exists — snapback is always one policy decision away

**Investment outcome (2025–2026 arc):**
- Vietnam and India were the clear structural supply chain winners — manufacturing FDI accelerated
- US semiconductor capital equipment (AMAT, KLAC, LRCX) sold off hard then recovered as deals excluded some tech
- Agricultural commodities (soybeans, corn) whipsawed with each truce/escalation announcement
- Long-duration US Treasuries underperformed as tariff-inflation concerns kept the Fed constrained

## Investment Implications

### The Four Investment Regimes of a Trade War

A properly calibrated investor doesn't hold one trade war view — they hold **four conditional views** mapped to each escalation stage:

| Stage | Macro Signal | Asset Class Implications |
|---|---|---|
| Signaling | Elevated uncertainty, no fundamental change | Reduce sector-specific overweights; add optionality |
| Initial imposition | Inflation pick-up, supply chain disruption | Long commodities, domestic producers; short import-dependent retailers |
| Escalation | Growth slowdown, earnings revisions down | Reduce risk overall; long gold, short global cyclicals; overweight defense |
| De-escalation | Risk-on, global trade recovery | Long EM exporters (Vietnam, India beneficiaries), global logistics, cyclicals |

**The critical skill:** Reading which stage you're in and anticipating the transition *before* the market consensus does. The market is notoriously bad at Stage 3 → de-escalation transitions — it stays pessimistic too long, creating re-entry opportunities.

### The Retaliation Asymmetry Framework

Before investing in any company with international revenue or supply chain exposure, apply this three-question filter inherited from Lesson 451:

1. **Tariff Exposure Score (from Lesson 451):** What fraction of the cost base is tariffed? What fraction of revenue is from retaliating-country markets?
2. **Supply Chain Flexibility:** Can the company re-source within 12 months at similar quality and cost? Companies that can are transition beneficiaries; those that can't are structural losers.
3. **Political Targeting Risk:** Is this company's product or sector symbolically important to the retaliating government? US agricultural exporters, luxury goods in China, tech companies with China revenue — all carry elevated political targeting risk beyond the economic tariff exposure.

### The CEO Portfolio Note

In a trade war environment with multiple active fronts (US-China, US-Canada, US-EU latent tension), **the highest-conviction position is geographic diversification within supply chains.** Companies that have already moved production to "tariff-neutral" geographies — Vietnam, India, Mexico (for goods outside USMCA disputes), Eastern Europe for EU supply — are structurally advantaged. The key is identifying which moves are already priced in versus which haven't been recognized by the market yet.

Secondary conviction: **commodities that benefit from supply chain rewiring** — copper (new factory construction), industrial gases (semiconductor fabs), specialized chemicals (reshoring manufacturing). These have 3–5 year demand tailwinds from the structural supply chain rebuild underway.
