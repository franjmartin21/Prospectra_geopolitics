## I. The Fundamental Asymmetry of Renewal vs. Original Sale

Most early-stage research firms approach the renewal conversation the same way they approached the original sale: with a pitch. They prepare a presentation. They highlight their best calls. They talk about the macro environment and why the next twelve months will be even more important to track.

This is the mistake.

The renewal conversation is not a second sales call. It is an **accountability audit** — conducted by the client, not by you. The client already has opinions about whether your product was valuable. Your job in the renewal conversation is not to convince them of a future hypothesis. It is to help them articulate what they already believe — and to give them the language and the framework to justify the renewal decision to their CFO.

The original sale was a pitch. The renewal is a review.

### The Asymmetry Table

| Dimension | Original Sale | Renewal Conversation |
|---|---|---|
| Client knowledge | Low — you are describing a promise | High — they have lived with the product |
| Primary obstacle | Trust (do you know what you're doing?) | Value proof (did it pay off?) |
| Your leverage | Analytical credibility + market timing | Track record + relationship capital |
| Their internal need | CFO says "convince me this is worth a trial" | CFO says "convince me this is worth another year" |
| Price discussion | Anchoring — you set the first number | Justification — you defend an existing number or argue for an increase |
| Emotional dynamic | Excitement about a new capability | Risk of sunk cost fallacy or disappointment |

The renewal is harder than the original sale in one specific way: every gap in your delivery over the past twelve months is now a live objection in the room, whether the client raises it explicitly or not. In the original sale, you had no gaps — only hypothetical ones. In the renewal, you have a real track record with real imperfections.

**This is not a weakness. Handled correctly, it is your strongest asset.**

## II. Historical Grounding — The Bloomberg Terminal Lesson

Before Bloomberg terminals became the ubiquitous $24,000/year infrastructure of institutional finance, Bloomberg had a serious renewal problem. In the late 1980s, they were selling to trading desks and treasury departments. The terminals were powerful, but the data was inconsistent, and early users regularly found gaps in coverage.

What made Bloomberg's renewal rate extraordinary — and eventually made the terminal nearly impossible to remove — was not product perfection. It was **workflow integration**. By the time a client hit renewal, Bloomberg had become embedded in the analyst's daily process. The analyst pulled bond yields from it every morning. The compliance team had built reporting templates around it. The operations desk used it for settlement confirmations.

The renewal conversation was not "was Bloomberg valuable this year?" It was "what would it cost us to remove it?"

Bloomberg understood something that most research firms miss: **the most powerful retention mechanism is not great analysis — it is operational integration**. If your product lives inside the client's workflow, the cost of cancellation is not just losing access to your data. It is rebuilding a process.

For Prospectra, this principle translates into a specific operational posture. Every feature you build, every deliverable you design, and every client touchpoint you create should ask: *Is this increasing Prospectra's integration into how this institution actually makes decisions?*

The GRI score is a step in that direction. The risk is that it remains a reference document — something the analyst reads and sets aside — rather than a score that enters the investment committee deck, the risk dashboard, or the quarterly client reporting.

By month twelve, you should know exactly how your subscriber is using the product. If the answer is "they read the weekly briefing and occasionally reference the GRI," you have a renewal risk. If the answer is "the GRI appears in three of their risk reports and the analyst cited our Russia analysis in the Q3 investment committee," you have a renewal lock.

## III. Concrete Example — Eurasia Group's Renewal Architecture (2005–2015)

Eurasia Group, founded by Ian Bremmer in 1998, grew from a boutique political risk consultancy to the dominant institutional geopolitical intelligence provider over roughly a decade. Their renewal architecture offers a direct case study.

**The core insight:** Eurasia Group did not sell research subscriptions. They sold **analytical relationships**. The product was not the written reports — it was access to the analyst. The reports were the evidence of analytical competence. The analyst call was the actual value driver.

In their early growth phase (roughly 2005–2012), their renewal rate was above 90% with major institutional clients. The primary driver was not accuracy — political risk is notoriously hard to predict with precision, and Eurasia Group had its share of misses. The driver was **analyst access**. Clients who renewed consistently cited one thing: "We can call Ian [or a senior analyst] when something breaks and get a real read within hours."

The lesson for Prospectra is uncomfortable but precise: **analytical access is a more durable retention mechanism than analytical accuracy**.

This does not mean accuracy is unimportant — the track record matters for the original sale and for building reputation. But in the renewal conversation, the client is asking a different question: *Do I have a trusted source I can rely on when the world gets messy?* That question is answered by relationship density, not by call accuracy.

**Translating this to Prospectra's current stage:**

By month twelve, your first institutional subscriber should have:
- At least 4–6 direct analytical calls with you (not just report delivery)
- At least 2 instances where you proactively reached out about an event relevant to their portfolio before they asked
- At least 1 instance where your analysis diverged from consensus and you were able to explain why

If those three conditions are met, you are in a strong renewal position regardless of whether every GRI call was directionally correct.

## IV. The Renewal Conversation Architecture

When the renewal meeting arrives, structure it in three acts — not a pitch.

### Act 1: The Accountability Review (15 minutes)

Open with the year in review, but lead with honesty, not highlights. The template:

> "Before we talk about the next twelve months, I want to walk through what we got right, what we got wrong, and what we changed as a result. This is the same framework we use internally, and I want you to have the full picture."

Cover:
- **2–3 calls we got directionally right** — with the specific GRI score, the position the call implied, and what happened
- **1–2 calls where we were early or wrong** — with a precise post-mortem (what the model missed, how we updated the methodology)
- **Framework changes made this year** — cite the revision log and what triggered each change

This act accomplishes two things simultaneously. First, it demonstrates analytical integrity — you are not hiding your misses, which immediately increases credibility. Second, it pre-empts the client's objections. They were going to raise the wrong calls anyway. You are better served raising them first, on your terms, with the analytical post-mortem already prepared.

### Act 2: The Value Capture Inventory (10 minutes)

Ask the client a direct question: "Can you walk me through the two or three moments this year where you found Prospectra most useful — and what you did with it?"

This question does three things:
1. Forces the client to articulate value in their own words (much more powerful than you claiming it)
2. Surfaces integration points you may not have known about
3. Identifies where the product is underused, giving you a renewal angle ("here's what you haven't yet utilized that would address X")

Listen carefully. The moments they cite reveal what they are actually buying. If they cite analytical depth on Russia, the renewal pitch is "we are expanding coverage." If they cite speed on the sanctions announcement, the pitch is "we are adding a real-time alert tier." If they struggle to name specific moments, you have a problem that better pricing will not fix.

### Act 3: The Forward Proposal (15 minutes)

Only after the accountability review and value capture inventory do you present the renewal terms. The sequence is non-negotiable — you cannot price a renewal before the client has articulated why they value the product.

The forward proposal covers:
- **Renewal terms** — same price with option to expand, or price increase with justification
- **Coverage expansion** — new countries or topics being added in Year 2
- **Product development** — what is being built that was not available in Year 1
- **Commitment incentives** — multi-year discount, priority analyst access, etc.

#### On Pricing: When to Increase and by How Much

The default for Year 2 should be a **10–15% price increase**, presented not as a cost-of-living adjustment but as a **scope expansion**. The framing matters:

> "We are renewing at $X, which reflects the addition of three new country GRI scores (Vietnam, Saudi Arabia, Mexico), the weekly briefing upgrade to include asset-specific directional views, and priority access to the Monday market call. If you would prefer to renew at the original scope, we can hold the price flat."

Never present a price increase as inflation. Always present it as scope. The client is not paying more for the same thing — they are paying slightly more for a materially better product.

If the client pushes back on price, do not immediately concede. The correct response:

> "I hear you. Let's look at the value capture inventory we just did. The Russia call in March, which you cited as informing the EM bonds reallocation — do you have a rough sense of the position size? ... Even at 5 basis points of protection on that position, the annual subscription pays for itself on a single call. I'm not saying every call will do that, but that's the frame I'd ask you to use when you're presenting this to your CFO."

This anchors the renewal in a specific, client-cited example, with a rough ROI calculation using their own numbers.

## V. The Renewal Risk Assessment Framework

Before every renewal conversation, score your client on these five dimensions (1–5 scale):

| Dimension | What to Assess | Score |
|---|---|---|
| **Workflow Integration** | Is the GRI in any formal reporting or decision process? | 1–5 |
| **Analyst Relationship Density** | How many direct conversations beyond report delivery? | 1–5 |
| **Usage Pattern** | Do they open weekly briefings? Cite GRI scores? Request calls? | 1–5 |
| **Internal Champion Strength** | Does the macro analyst have budget authority? Internal credibility? | 1–5 |
| **Competitive Exposure** | Are they using or evaluating a competing service? | 1–5 |

Scoring guide:
- **20–25:** Strong renewal. Push for multi-year commitment.
- **14–19:** Moderate renewal risk. Focus Act 1 on accountability, Act 2 on uncovering unused value.
- **Below 14:** High renewal risk. Consider a proactive "renewal rescue" conversation 60 days before expiration — do not wait for the formal renewal meeting.

## VI. Investment Implications

The renewal economics of institutional research subscriptions have a direct analogue in how to think about certain asset classes — particularly **SaaS companies**, **data providers**, and **financial information platforms**.

### The Net Revenue Retention (NRR) Signal

For research and data businesses, **Net Revenue Retention** — the percentage of prior-year revenue retained from existing customers, including expansions — is the single most important indicator of fundamental quality. An NRR above 110% means the business grows even if it never acquires a new customer. An NRR below 90% means the business is structurally shrinking.

The renewal conversation is the unit-level mechanism that produces NRR. Every pricing decision, scope expansion, and multi-year commitment that emerges from renewal conversations aggregates into the NRR figure.

**Investment framework:**
- Data/research businesses with NRR > 115% and growing: pricing power confirmed, mission-critical integration likely, low churn by construction
- NRR 95–110%: adequate but undifferentiated — likely substitutable, no pricing power
- NRR < 90%: structural attrition, often disguised by new customer acquisition; growth is a treadmill

**Relevant positions:**
- Long: mission-critical financial data (market structure, regulatory, alternative data with proprietary sources)
- Avoid: commoditized financial data where NRR trend is declining (replacement risk from free or cheaper alternatives accelerating)

The geopolitical angle: **FDI screening laws** are creating a new category of mission-critical regulatory data — who is on an SDN list, which entities require CFIUS review, which jurisdictions have new restriction categories. Companies sitting at the intersection of geopolitics and compliance data (regulatory intelligence, sanctions screening) have structurally increasing NRR because their customers face legal liability if they cancel.

**Dataset:** Build a subscriber engagement analytics pipeline in Databricks.

Track:

- Time-to-open (leading indicator of how mission-critical your product is perceived)
- Analyst call frequency per subscriber

**Pipeline idea:** A 90-day pre-renewal dashboard that aggregates engagement signals and generates an automated Renewal Risk Score (using the 5-dimension framework above). The dashboard should flag subscribers with declining engagement 90 days before renewal — giving you enough runway for a proactive retention campaign.

**Feature engineering candidates:**
- Days since last direct analyst contact (decay function — each additional day without contact reduces renewal probability)
- Report open rate trend (30-day rolling average vs. 90-day rolling average — a declining trend is a leading churn indicator)
- GRI score changes in subscriber's watch list countries (a major GRI move in a country they track is an opportunity for proactive outreach — and a missed opportunity if not acted on)

This pipeline is also your institutional credibility infrastructure. When a subscriber asks "how do other clients use the GRI?", the engagement analytics tell you — and let you share anonymized benchmarks ("our highest-engagement subscribers review the weekly briefing within 4 hours of delivery").

## XI. CEO Portfolio Note

Prospectra's renewal economics are a microcosm of the broader investment thesis in institutional data and research. The businesses with the most durable moats in this space — Bloomberg, MSCI, FactSet, Verisk, Wood Mackenzie — all share one structural feature: their data enters regulated or fiduciary decision processes, making cancellation a compliance risk, not just a cost decision.

The emerging category to watch: **geopolitical sanctions and FDI compliance data**. As FDI screening expands (CFIUS in the US, NSIA in the UK, equivalent frameworks in the EU, Australia, Japan), the number of transactions that legally require geopolitical risk assessment is growing by statute. Any research product that positions itself as part of a compliance workflow — not just an investment workflow — has structurally higher NRR by construction.

This is a Year 2 positioning question for Prospectra: are we an investment intelligence product, a compliance intelligence product, or both? The answer has significant implications for pricing power, renewal rates, and the type of institutional buyer you are selling to.
