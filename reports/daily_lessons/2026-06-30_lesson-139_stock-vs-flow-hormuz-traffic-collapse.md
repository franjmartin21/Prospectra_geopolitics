## Opening Question

Eleven days ago, Lesson 137 reported Hormuz commercial traffic at a wartime high — 70 vessel crossings on a single Wednesday, used as evidence that commercial shippers were pricing the war into insurance and continuing to operate. This week, multiple outlets citing the same data provider (Kpler) report Hormuz traffic has collapsed to roughly 5–7 vessels per day against a ~93/day normal baseline — crude oil flows down 95%, LNG down 99%, fertilizer cargo down 94% for the month. Both numbers are real. Both came from credible sources. How can "traffic at a wartime high" and "traffic down 95%" both be true descriptions of the same strait inside the same two weeks — and which one should an investor have been trading on at the time it was reported?

## Section 2: The Concept — Stock vs. Flow in Crisis Data

A vessel-crossing count answers the question "how many ships moved today." It does not, by itself, tell you whether the war risk has actually been priced in and accepted, or whether you are watching a backlog clear. **Stock** is the inventory of decisions already made and committed before the news changed — cargoes already loaded, charters already signed, voyages already underway. **Flow** is the rate of *new* decisions being made right now, under current information. When a shock hits, stock keeps moving for days or weeks on momentum — captains don't turn ships around mid-transit, and charterers don't walk away from contracts already signed — while flow can freeze almost immediately, because the next voyage hasn't been booked yet and nobody has to honor a commitment that doesn't exist. A traffic count that blends both will look like resilience right up until the stock clears, then air-pocket. That is very plausibly what happened between L137's 70-crossing Wednesday and this week's 5–7-vessel daily figures: the wartime-high count was substantially stock (vessels already mid-voyage or already chartered before the Kiku/Bahrain/Kuwait escalation), and the collapse is the flow rate showing what new bookings actually look like once owners and insurers re-priced the risk in real time.

**The 20-year precedent: the Black Sea grain corridor, 2022.** In the first one to two weeks after Russia's February 24, 2022 invasion, reporting on Black Sea shipping was genuinely mixed — some vessels already loaded or already in transit continued to move, which produced "traffic continues" headlines. But new charter bookings froze almost immediately as P&I clubs and war-risk underwriters pulled or radically repriced cover for the region; the stock cleared within roughly two to three weeks and flow did not meaningfully restart on its own. It took a dedicated institutional mechanism — the UN/Turkey-brokered Black Sea Grain Initiative, signed in July 2022, five months after the invasion, with an inspection regime and a defined safe-passage corridor — to bring flow back, and even then volumes normalized gradually as insurers built dedicated war-risk products for the corridor rather than reverting to pre-war terms. The generalizable lesson: a ceasefire or stand-down announcement is necessary but not sufficient to restart flow. Flow resumes only once a structural mechanism removes the underwriting uncertainty facing the *next* voyage, not the ones already committed.

**Why this matters for today's Doha talks and the Muscat Joint Hormuz Committee specifically:** the question is not whether the talks "succeed" in a press-release sense — Bürgenstock also produced a roadmap that didn't stop this week's escalation. The question is whether the Joint Hormuz Committee produces something with the structural shape of the Grain Initiative: a defined safe-passage arrangement, an inspection or verification regime, something underwriters can actually price. Absent that, expect the traffic count to stay near its flow-rate floor (5–7/day) regardless of how many more stand-down announcements follow, because the thing that needs to change — the marginal new voyage's risk calculus — hasn't changed.

**A second application: rate vs. volume in the tanker-equity puzzle.** If physical volume is down 95%, why is FRO still +89% year-to-date and only 13% off its all-time high, rather than collapsing alongside the vessel count? The same stock/flow logic, applied to price instead of quantity, supplies the likely answer: tanker revenue is rate times volume, and day rates for the small number of vessels still willing to transit (or owners able to reroute capacity to non-Hormuz routes in high demand) can spike precisely because volume is scarce — a war-risk premium paid to the ships that do sail. Tanker equities price expected revenue, not headline vessel counts, so a volume collapse and a resilient share price are not necessarily in tension; they may be the same scarcity showing up in two different units. This reframes the "decoupling-from-Brent" thesis (L131/L134) from a vague observation into a specific, checkable claim — see Section 4.

## Key Concepts Covered

- Stock vs. flow as a general lens for crisis data: a count that blends already-committed decisions (stock, which moves on momentum and clears over days-to-weeks) with new decisions made under current information (flow, which can freeze immediately) will look like resilience right up until the stock clears, then air-pocket — explaining how "wartime-high traffic" and "95% collapse" were both accurate descriptions of the same strait eleven days apart
- The Black Sea grain corridor (2022) as the 20-year precedent: stock-driven "traffic continues" headlines in the first two weeks, a flow freeze as underwriters repriced risk, and a five-month gap before a dedicated institutional mechanism (the Black Sea Grain Initiative) — not the ceasefire itself — restored flow
- Applying the same decomposition to price instead of quantity: tanker revenue (rate × volume) can hold up even as volume collapses, if day rates spike on scarcity — reframing the tanker decoupling thesis as a checkable rate claim rather than a vague equity observation
- A second TRACE-V-relevant evidentiary conflict: two outlets citing the same data provider (Kpler) for the same week produced vessel-count figures differing by a factor of two-to-six, flagged as an unresolved Tier 2 internal source conflict pending primary-source reconciliation
- The negotiating track itself has bifurcated: a nuclear sub-track (dormant this week) and a new Hormuz-management sub-track (Doha talks + Muscat Joint Hormuz Committee), run through different mediators (Qatar, Oman) — extending L138's "two independent tracks" framework with a split inside the diplomatic track itself
