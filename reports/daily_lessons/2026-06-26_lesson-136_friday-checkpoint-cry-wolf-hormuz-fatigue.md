## Section 2: The Concept — Threat Fatigue and the Cry-Wolf Discount

Here is the puzzle worth naming precisely: an attack happened, a fresh threat was issued, and the price fell anyway. That is not irrational, and it is not the market "ignoring the news." It is a specific, well-documented phenomenon: **repeated threats that don't produce sustained, confirmed disruption progressively lose their power to move price**, because each false alarm updates the market's estimate of how likely *this specific actor's threats* are to materialize into something that actually matters for supply. This is Bayesian updating with a name attached to the colloquial version: the cry-wolf discount.

**The 20-year precedent: North Korean missile and nuclear tests, 2006–2023.** North Korea's first nuclear test (October 2006) produced a real, if brief, market reaction — regional equities dipped, the won weakened. By the mid-2010s, tests of increasing technical sophistication (including 2017's Hwasong-15 ICBM, which North Korea claimed could reach the U.S. mainland) still moved markets, but by smaller and shorter-lived margins each time — a pattern researchers and traders both observed and named "North Korea fatigue." By 2022–2023, North Korea conducted a record number of missile tests in a single year, and individual tests routinely produced negligible market reaction. The mechanism was not that the missiles became less dangerous. It was that twenty years of threats and tests without a sustained, market-relevant disruption (a war, a closed shipping lane, a destroyed facility) taught market participants that this particular actor's brinkmanship, however real, reliably stopped short of the outcome that would actually move oil, equities, or currencies.

This project's own Hormuz episode is now far enough along to be running the same curve. By count, this is at minimum the fourth distinct threat-or-attack cycle in this conflict that this log has tracked: the June 18–20 transit-surge-then-reversal whiplash (L129→L131), the OPEC+/IMO de-escalation pricing into this week's Brent drop (L134), and now a live attack plus a fresh route-specific threat (today) that barely interrupted the same downtrend. Each cycle that fails to produce a sustained, multi-week supply disruption is itself evidence the market uses to discount the next one — which is exactly why a real attack, today, moved price *less* than the first Hormuz closure declaration did back in L129's window.

**Why this matters and where it's dangerous:** fatigue-based discounting can be the *correct* read — if this actor genuinely lacks the capability or intent to sustain a real closure, learning that over time is rational, not complacent. But the same mechanism is the textbook setup for underpricing the one time the threat is real. The 1973 oil embargo and 1990 Kuwait invasion both followed periods of discounted Middle East threats that had previously not materialized. The analytical job is not to decide today which case this is — it's to identify what would distinguish them, which Section 3 and the reflection questions return to.

## Key Concepts Covered

- Threat fatigue / cry-wolf discounting: why repeated threats that don't produce sustained disruption progressively lose price-moving power, illustrated by North Korea's missile and nuclear tests (2006–2023) producing shrinking market reactions over time
- The danger symmetric to fatigue: discounting is rational when it reflects an actor's genuine track record, but it is also the precise mechanism by which real disruptions get underpriced right before they happen (1973, 1990 precedents)
- Distinguishing a "confirmed" data point from a "highly likely, pending settlement" one — applying the project's TRACE-V evidentiary-tier discipline to grading positions mid-day, before a formal close exists
