## 1. The Geographic and Historical Foundation

The Sahel is the semi-arid band stretching from Senegal to Sudan, sitting between the Sahara Desert to the north and the tropical forests to the south. The name comes from the Arabic word for "shore" — the southern shore of the Sahara Sea.

For the purposes of this lesson, the **investment-relevant Sahel** is a narrower zone: **Mali, Burkina Faso, Niger, and to a lesser extent Chad** — four countries that share:

- Extreme poverty (Human Development Index bottom 20 globally)
- Post-colonial governance structures imported from Paris that never achieved internal legitimacy
- Islamic insurgencies with roots in the 2012 Libyan weapons cascade
- Massive untapped natural resources: **uranium (Niger), gold (Mali, Burkina Faso), lithium (Mali), manganese, phosphates**
- Young, fast-growing populations creating demographic pressure governments cannot absorb

**The colonial architecture:** France did not simply colonize these territories — it built a *monetary and political dependency architecture* that persisted decades after independence. The CFA franc, pegged to the Euro, managed by the French Treasury, meant these governments could not print money, devalue to competitiveness, or run independent monetary policy. French nuclear companies (Orano, formerly Areva) locked up uranium supply under terms signed in the 1960s. French military basing rights came bundled with political protection for governing elites — which increasingly meant protecting the *wrong* elites from their own populations.

## 2. The Coup Belt: What Happened and Why

| Country | Coup Date | Government Deposed | Junta Leader | Key Trigger |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mali | August 2020 (and again 2021) | Ibrahim Boubacar Keïta | Col. Assimi Goïta | Jihadist territorial gains, corruption |
| Guinea | September 2021 | Alpha Condé | Lt. Col. Mamadi Doumbouya | Authoritarian entrenchment, poverty |
| Burkina Faso | January 2022 (and Sept 2022) | Roch Kaboré / Paul-Henri Sandaogo Damiba | Capt. Ibrahim Traoré | Jihadist massacres, government paralysis |
| Niger | July 2023 | Mohamed Bazoum | Gen. Abdourahamane Tchiani | Presidential Guard action; anti-France sentiment |

**The pattern is not random.** Each coup shares three elements:

1. **A failing security situation** — Jihadist groups (primarily JNIM, affiliated with al-Qaeda, and ISGS, affiliated with Islamic State) had been seizing territory faster than governments could respond. France's Operation Barkhane was making tactical gains while the political situation deteriorated — the classic COIN paradox.

2. **Anti-French popular sentiment** — Decades of the CFA franc, extraction economics, and the perception that France protected corrupt elites had built enormous popular hostility. The juntas exploited this. Crowds in Bamako, Ouagadougou, and Niamey waved Russian flags. This wasn't pro-Russia sentiment — it was anti-France sentiment looking for a vehicle.

3. **Military frustration** — Officers who had been trained, equipped, and embedded with French forces watched territory fall while French air power sat idle over bureaucratic rules of engagement. The sense that France *could* help and was choosing not to — to protect the political relationship with Paris — was corrosive.

**The Alliance of Sahel States (AES):** In 2023, Mali, Burkina Faso, and Niger formalized their coordination into the Alliance of Sahel States — explicitly as a mutual defense pact and a bloc for renegotiating relationships with the West. They announced their withdrawal from ECOWAS (the West African economic bloc) in January 2024. This is significant: **it is the first time since independence that Francophone West African states have created an institutional framework explicitly designed to exit the French sphere of influence.**

## 3. France's Strategic Humiliation

Operation Barkhane (2014–2022) was French strategy in microcosm: deploy special forces and air power to suppress insurgency, train host-nation forces, maintain political control through elite relationships. It worked tactically and failed strategically.

The sequence:
- **Mali, 2022:** New junta demanded French forces leave. France complied within months. 2,400 troops out.
- **Burkina Faso, January 2023:** France asked to leave. 400 troops out within a month.
- **Niger, August 2023:** Junta demanded French ambassador leave within 48 hours. French forces (1,500 troops) evacuated by December 2023.

By early 2024, France had no military presence in Mali, Burkina Faso, or Niger — countries where it had operated continuously since independence. This is strategically equivalent to the United States being told to leave Germany, Japan, and South Korea simultaneously.

**What France lost:**
- Forward basing for Saharan operations
- Intelligence networks built over 60 years
- Diplomatic leverage over three governments
- Preferential access to uranium supply (the Orano/SOMAÏR mine in Niger represented ~5% of global uranium production)
- Credibility across Francophone Africa as the security guarantor of last resort

## 4. Russia's Africa Corps: The Junta Security Model

What filled the vacuum was not China (which stays out of military entanglements), not the US (which withdrew its own forces from Niger in mid-2024), but **Russia's Africa Corps** — the successor to Wagner Group following Yevgeny Prigozhin's death in August 2023.

The Africa Corps model is deceptively simple:

**What Russia offers:**
- Regime protection — soldiers physically embedded with leadership to prevent coups *against* the junta
- No human rights conditionality
- No democratic governance requirements
- Arms transfers at favorable prices
- Plausible anti-Western narrative (the "anti-colonial" framing)

**What Russia takes:**
- Mining concessions (gold primarily in Mali and Burkina Faso)
- Political alignment in multilateral forums (UN votes, ICC)
- Forward positioning for Russian strategic influence in the Saharan corridor

**The 2026 test case:** In August 2026, a mutiny broke out at Niger's Base 101. This was the first time the Africa Corps directly intervened to protect an African government from an *internal* rebellion — not from an external jihadist attack. The intervention succeeded. This is a qualitative escalation: it establishes the precedent that Russia is in the *regime survival* business, not merely the *counterinsurgency* business. The two are very different.

**The limits:** Africa Corps has ~200–300 personnel in Niger. It did not defeat the jihadist insurgency; it cannot. In April 2026, Tuareg rebels using FPV drones forced Russian troops out of Kidal in Mali — a significant tactical defeat. The Africa Corps is a regime protection force, not a counterinsurgency force. The underlying instability continues.

## 5. The Uranium-Gold Equation: Where Investors Live

This is where the geopolitics translates directly into asset pricing.

### Niger: Uranium

Niger holds approximately **5–7% of global uranium reserves** and was the world's 7th-largest uranium producer before the coup-related disruptions. The key facts:

- **Orano (French state nuclear company)** operated SOMAÏR — the dominant mine — under agreements signed in the 1960s. The junta nationalized it in June 2025 and initiated international arbitration (ICSID).
- **Niger is now selling SOMAÏR uranium independently**, circumventing French ownership. The legal status is disputed.
- **The US DFC approved $414M in financing** for Global Atomic's Dasa uranium project in September 2026 — the first major US investment in Niger's uranium sector and an explicit hedging move against China and Russia gaining a monopoly on supply.
- **An Australian group signed a deal** to relaunch another uranium project in September 2026.

**The investment signal:** Western governments are actively financing alternative uranium supply chains in Niger to prevent Chinese/Russian consolidation. This is a **strategic minerals play**, not a pure commodity play. The US, UK, and EU are all reclassifying uranium as a critical mineral requiring supply chain diversification. This creates structural demand pressure regardless of spot prices.

**Investment implication for uranium equities:**
- Orano's losses in Niger are sunk costs for the French state; the market has largely priced them in
- Junior uranium developers with Niger exposure (Global Atomic: GLATF) now have US DFC backing — significant de-risking
- The Sahel disruption tightens global uranium supply at exactly the moment when the global nuclear renaissance (US IRA, EU Green Deal nuclear reclassification, Japan restarts) is driving demand

### Mali and Burkina Faso: Gold

Mali is **Africa's 3rd largest gold producer**. Burkina Faso is in the top 10. Combined, the AES states represent a material fraction of West African gold output.

**The key dynamic:** Mine operators (Barrick Gold has major assets in Mali; multiple juniors in Burkina Faso) are navigating junta-imposed renegotiations, security deterioration, and Russian concession demands.

- **Barrick Gold renewed its license in Mali** in 2026, but under renegotiated terms that give the state a larger share.
- **Burkina Faso's junta** has imposed emergency mining regulations requiring government approval for gold exports and in some cases seizing gold to fund military operations.
- **Security costs have risen sharply** — mine operators now require private security contractors, helicopter logistics, and in some cases full site evacuations due to jihadist activity.

**Investment implication:** Mali and Burkina Faso gold mines should be priced with **political risk premiums** reflecting: (a) renegotiation risk — expect governments to demand larger cuts, (b) operational disruption — Jihadist attacks on supply lines and worker kidnappings, (c) gold royalty seizure under emergency decrees. Majors like Barrick are resilient enough to absorb these costs; juniors are existentially exposed.

## 6. The Migration Dimension: Europe's Security Interest

The Sahel is not just a distant minerals play — it is **directly connected to European domestic politics** through migration.

The Sahara crossing routes from sub-Saharan Africa to North Africa (and then to Europe) run through Mali, Niger, and Burkina Faso. The juntas have explicitly weaponized this:

- Niger under Bazoum had a formal partnership with the EU to control migration flows in exchange for financial support
- After the coup, the junta cancelled these agreements
- Migration flows through Niger to Algeria/Libya increased materially in 2024–2025

This gives the AES states **implicit leverage over Europe**: if EU sanctions become too aggressive, the juntas can open the migration spigot. This caps how hard Europe can push on governance or sanctions — a strategic constraint that narrows the West's options and strengthens junta durability.

**Investment implication:** European border security companies (Airbus Defence, Leonardo, Thales — who provide drone surveillance and detection systems) benefit structurally from sustained Sahelian instability creating migration pressure on European borders.

## 9. Signal vs. Noise

**Signal:** Africa Corps proving its regime-protection model in August 2026 (Niger mutiny suppression) — this is a qualitative escalation that extends Russian reach and durability in the region. This is structural.

**Signal:** US DFC uranium financing — the United States is explicitly treating Niger uranium as a strategic supply chain issue. This is structural.

**Noise:** Individual junta leader changes, specific attack incidents, AES diplomatic statements. The underlying dynamics (resource nationalism, France's exit, Russian presence, jihadist insurgency) persist regardless of which colonel is in power.
