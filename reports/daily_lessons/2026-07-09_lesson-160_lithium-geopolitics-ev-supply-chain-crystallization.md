## Opening Framing Question

*Imagine a world where one country controls 65-70% of the refining and processing capacity for the single most critical input into electric vehicle batteries, military drone systems, and grid-scale energy storage — simultaneously. Now imagine that country is China, and that the US and EU have spent the last five years urgently trying to build alternative supply chains — but that lithium prices crashed 85% from their 2022 peak before those alternative chains were commercially viable, wiping out the investment case for dozens of Western projects.*

*Here is your opening question: **Is the West's critical minerals scramble genuinely succeeding — or has the price cycle destroyed the capital formation that was supposed to fix the China dependency, leaving the West structurally more exposed in 2026 than it was in 2021?***

Sit with the tension: China's grip on processing is as strong as ever. Western mines exist. Western processing does not, at scale. The price cycle turned hostile at exactly the moment the West needed sustained investment. Is this accident, strategy, or just markets?

## The Core Concept: The Mineral Stack — Why Mining Isn't the Problem

Most political discussion of critical minerals focuses on "where the mines are." This is largely irrelevant to the actual geopolitical question. **The critical dependency is in processing and refining, not in mine ownership.**

Here is the mineral stack for lithium, from raw earth to battery cell:

```
Stage 1: Mining (Lithium in rock or brine)
  → Australia (hard rock, spodumene), Chile/Argentina (brine in salt flats),
    China (domestic deposits), Zimbabwe, DRC

Stage 2: Processing (Lithium hydroxide / lithium carbonate)
  → CHINA: ~70% of global lithium chemical processing
  → The bottleneck. Converting spodumene or brine to battery-grade chemical is
    capital-intensive, technically demanding, environmentally contentious.

Stage 3: Cathode Active Material (NMC, LFP, NCA)
  → CHINA: >80% of global cathode production
  → This is where the chemistry becomes the battery's energy density

Stage 4: Cell Manufacturing
  → CHINA: ~75-80% of global EV battery cell capacity (CATL, BYD, CALB, Gotion)
  → South Korea (Samsung SDI, SK On, LG Energy), Japan (Panasonic)

Stage 5: Battery Pack Assembly & EV Integration
  → US, Europe, China (more diversified at this stage)
```

**The insight:** Australia produces ~50% of global lithium ore. Chile holds the largest proven reserves. The US has domestic lithium deposits. But if there is no Western processing capacity to convert Australian ore or Chilean brine into battery-grade lithium hydroxide, the Western "we have the mines" talking point is strategically meaningless. A mine without a refinery is a hole in the ground.

China built this advantage deliberately, beginning in the 2000s, by:
1. Subsidizing domestic processing capacity to achieve cost advantage
2. Acquiring upstream mining assets globally (DRC cobalt, African lithium, Chilean lithium stakes)
3. Building vertically integrated supply chains from mine to cathode within China's borders
4. Deploying overcapacity to crash prices whenever Western competitors threatened to emerge

### The 2022-2026 Price Cycle: Strategy or Market?

- **2021-2022:** EV demand surged, lithium carbonate prices spiked from ~$8,000/tonne to >$80,000/tonne (10x in 18 months). Western lithium projects that had been uneconomic suddenly had IRRs above 30%.

- **2022-2024:** Western governments (US via IRA, EU via Critical Raw Materials Act) poured billions into alternative supply chain development. Dozens of projects announced in Australia, the US (Nevada, North Carolina), Chile, and Canada.

- **2023-2024:** Lithium prices collapsed. From $80,000/tonne peak to ~$10,000-12,000/tonne by early 2024 — an 85% crash. Root cause: China's battery manufacturers (CATL, BYD) rapidly expanded production capacity; Chinese domestic lithium supply came online; EV demand growth slowed below expectations in the US and Europe.

- **2025-2026:** Prices have partially recovered (lithium carbonate ~$11,000-14,000/tonne as of mid-2026), but remain well below the levels needed to justify new Western processing projects. The IRA critical minerals provisions partially survived the Trump administration's policy overhaul (the direct EV credits were pared back but the domestic processing tax credits retained). The EU Critical Raw Materials Act has moved slower than expected.

**The strategic question:** Did China deliberately overproduce to crash prices and kill Western investment? The honest answer: it doesn't matter. The *result* is the same whether it was strategy or markets — Western critical minerals investment dried up precisely when it was most needed. The geopolitical exposure persists.

## Historical Grounding: Three Precedents for Mineral Choke Points

### 1. OPEC's 1973-1974 Oil Embargo — The Processing Power of a Cartel

The oil embargo showed that physical supply control does not require ownership of the end user's refineries. Saudi Arabia and its partners controlled crude; the West controlled refining. The embargo worked at the crude stage because refineries cannot run without feedstock. **The lesson:** Whoever controls the most upstream-necessary commodity stage in a supply chain holds the leverage. For oil in 1973, it was crude. For lithium in 2026, it is processing — not mining.

### 2. Japan's Rare Earth Crisis (2010) — China's First Critical Minerals Shot Across the Bow

In 2010, China — which controlled ~97% of global rare earth production — cut export quotas and restricted supplies to Japan during a maritime dispute in the East China Sea. Japanese manufacturers (Toyota, Panasonic, Hitachi) faced sudden shortages of materials essential for electronics and hybrid vehicle motors. Japan spent the following decade aggressively diversifying, developing domestic rare earth recycling, and funding Australian rare earth projects. **The lesson:** China has demonstrated willingness to use mineral control as a diplomatic tool. It retreated under WTO pressure in 2012, but the signal was clear. The 2010 crisis created Japan's playbook; the West is still writing theirs.

### 3. Cobalt in the DRC — Resource Dependence as Human Rights Liability

Congo controls ~70% of global cobalt production. Western EV manufacturers (Tesla, GM, Ford) face reputational and ESG risk from DRC cobalt's association with artisanal mining and child labor. This created a secondary driver for Western battery chemistry diversification — toward lithium iron phosphate (LFP) batteries (which don't use cobalt) and battery recycling programs. **The lesson:** Critical mineral dependencies carry secondary political costs — ESG exposure, sanctions risk, governance risk — beyond simple supply security. The "clean" EV becomes problematic if the battery requires DRC cobalt mined by children or Chinese-processed lithium from Xinjiang-adjacent facilities.

## The Current Landscape: Where the West Actually Stands in 2026

### What Has Been Achieved:
- **US IRA Critical Minerals Tax Credits (45X):** Manufacturing credits for battery cells, modules, and critical materials processed in the US. Several projects in Nevada and North Carolina qualifying. But the capacity is marginal compared to Chinese scale.
- **US-Australia Critical Minerals Partnership:** Australia's Lynas (rare earths) and Pilbara Minerals (lithium) have secured US DoD offtake commitments and loan guarantees. Processing facilities in construction.
- **EU Critical Raw Materials Act (2024):** Targets for domestic EU processing to cover 40% of EU consumption by 2030 — ambitious and almost certainly missed.
- **Rio Tinto's Rincon Lithium Project (Argentina):** One of the few large-scale projects that survived the price crash; Phase 1 production online, Phase 2 under construction.
- **Albemarle & SQM (Chile):** Western-linked lithium producers that retained market share, though both are producing at reduced margins in the price environment.

### What Has Not Been Achieved:
- **No material Western lithium processing capacity.** The US does not have a single large-scale lithium hydroxide refinery operating at commercial scale outside pilot programs.
- **Battery cell manufacturing gap persists.** While US and EU gigafactories are under construction (Ford BlueOval City, AESC in Tennessee, ACC in France), they remain dependent on Asian cathode and separator supply.
- **China's CATL maintains ~37% global market share** (2025) despite Western policy pressure, and has continued expanding in Hungary and Morocco — creating EU-compliant manufacturing while keeping core IP in China.

### The China Policy Counter-Move:
In 2025, Beijing imposed export restrictions on several processing chemicals and precursor materials for battery cathodes. This is the Japan-2010 playbook applied more broadly — not a ban (which would be too blunt), but friction, licensing requirements, and quota management that add cost and uncertainty for non-Chinese manufacturers. The effect: Western battery makers face supply chain uncertainty without a domestic alternative.

## The Investor's Map: Lithium & Critical Minerals in 2026

### Tier 1 — The Structural Long: Companies With Processing Capacity or Pathway

**The investment thesis is not "buy lithium miners" — it is "buy whoever solves the processing bottleneck."**

- **Albemarle (ALB) — USA:** Largest Western lithium producer. Operates in Chile, Australia, US. The price crash gutted 2024 earnings; the stock fell ~70% from 2022 peak. At current levels (~$60-75/share range), the market is pricing sustained low lithium prices. The thesis: any supply disruption or demand surge from AI data centers (which need grid storage) or military electrification would revalue ALB rapidly. **High risk, high asymmetry.**

- **Pilbara Minerals (PLS.AX) — Australia:** Spodumene producer partnering with US DoD. Australian domicile provides geopolitical safety. Pure-play lithium exposure with no refining (yet) — this is the risk.

- **Livent / Arcadium Lithium (ALTM):** Formed from merger; significant lithium hydroxide capacity in Argentina and the US. The processing element is the differentiator from pure miners.

- **Lithium Americas (LAC):** Thacker Pass (Nevada) project — the largest US domestic lithium resource. GM-backed. Construction ongoing. The US government has provided a $2.26B DoE loan. If Thacker Pass produces, it is the first large-scale domestic US lithium mine. **Pre-revenue, development stage — high risk, strategic asset.**

- **MP Materials (MP) — Rare Earths:** Not lithium, but the same processing-bottleneck thesis applies. Mountain Pass, California rare earth mine + nascent US processing. DoD-backed. **The template for what Lithium Americas could become.**

### Tier 2 — The Battery Technology Hedge: Chemistry Alternatives

If lithium supply remains constrained and expensive, battery chemistry diversification becomes investment-relevant:

- **Sodium-Ion Batteries:** CATL has commercialized sodium-ion (no lithium required). Western equivalents (HiNa Battery, Faradion) remain pre-commercial. The risk to the lithium bull case: if sodium-ion scales, the lithium premium compresses.
- **Solid-State Batteries:** Toyota, QuantumScape, Solid Power. Still pre-commercial. Use lithium but less of it (higher energy density = smaller battery for same range). Mixed news: a positive for lithium demand efficiency, a negative for raw lithium volume.
- **Battery Recycling:** Li-Cycle (North America), Redwood Materials (founded by Tesla's former CTO JB Straubel). Processing lithium from recycled batteries circumvents Chinese processing dependence *for secondary supply*. Still nascent but strategically significant.

### Tier 3 — The European Defense Angle (Connecting to Lesson 159)

This is an underappreciated connection: **European rearmament requires lithium.**

Modern military systems are increasingly electrified:
- **Drones:** Ukraine's war demonstrated that drone attrition rates require thousands of units per month. Drones run on lithium batteries.
- **Military EVs:** The US Army's Optionally Manned Fighting Vehicle program, EU tactical vehicle electrification programs.
- **Energy storage for forward operating bases:** Replacing diesel generators with battery-backed microgrids reduces logistics tail and battlefield signature.

**The implication:** European defense spending (Lesson 159: $500-600B/year gap to 5% GDP) creates a *sovereign demand* for lithium that is price-inelastic. A government buying batteries for military drones does not walk away because lithium carbonate is at $15,000/tonne rather than $10,000. This creates a floor demand that commercial EV markets — which are elastic — do not provide.

**The investment angle:** Defense-electrification demand is structurally more stable than consumer EV demand. Companies with lithium supply contracts tied to defense customers have superior demand visibility.

## Macro Overlay: Lithium as a Currency War Battleground

The critical minerals competition has a monetary dimension that is rarely analyzed:

**The Petrodollar parallel:** Oil is priced and traded in dollars, giving the US structural seigniorage and financial surveillance over energy markets. **The US is explicitly trying to build a "lithium dollar" equivalent** — a system where critical mineral pricing, trade finance, and offtake agreements flow through US-aligned institutions.

The IRA's domestic content requirements are designed to create this: if EV battery credit eligibility requires materials from "foreign entities of concern" exclusion (i.e., non-Chinese sources), then over time, the global supply chain bifurcates. One chain for the US/EU market (priced in dollars, traded through Western institutions). A second chain for China-adjacent markets.

This mirrors the energy market bifurcation post-Ukraine sanctions (2022): a Western crude market at Brent, and a discounted "dark fleet" crude market for Russia-to-India/China trades. **Critical minerals are heading toward the same bifurcation** — two supply chains, two pricing systems, with China offering a discount to buyers willing to stay in the China system.

**Investment implication:** Western-aligned lithium projects will eventually command a *geopolitical premium* over Chinese-processed equivalents — not because the lithium is chemically superior, but because the Western supply chain will be the only one eligible for EU battery regulations and US defense contracts. This premium does not exist yet at scale. It will.

## CEO Portfolio Note

The lithium investment cycle is in the trough. That is the point of entry for long-horizon investors — not the peak, not when IRRs are obvious to everyone.

**The thesis:** Western critical minerals investment is structurally non-negotiable. Governments will not allow indefinite Chinese monopoly on battery inputs for EVs and defense. The question is not *if* Western processing capacity gets built — it is *who builds it and what price they'll need to make it viable.* The price will eventually be set by policy (offtake guarantees, loan programs, procurement requirements), not just markets.

**Current positioning:**
- **Long Albemarle (ALB):** High-quality, diversified lithium producer near cycle lows. Entry justified now; add on further weakness. 24-36 month horizon. Thesis falsified if: lithium prices remain below $12,000/tonne for >18 months, or if sodium-ion scales faster than expected.
- **Long Lithium Americas (LAC) — small position, risk capital only:** Pre-revenue development. The US government loan and GM backing provide credibility. If Thacker Pass enters production, the rerating is substantial. Binary risk.

**Macro connection:**
- **Long USD in lithium corridors:** If the bifurcation thesis is correct, Western critical minerals trade increasingly prices in USD through US-ally institutions. Dollar demand strengthens in the commodity bloc.

**The honest uncertainty:** The price recovery timeline is unknown. Lithium at $12,000-14,000/tonne is not the supply-incentive price for new Western projects (~$20,000-25,000 needed for most US/EU projects to clear IRR hurdles). Until price recovers, the investment is thesis-dependent, not cash-flow-supported. Size accordingly.
