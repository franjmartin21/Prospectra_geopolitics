## Part 1: The Architecture We Are Living In — Bretton Woods II

### 1.1 — Bretton Woods I (1944–1971): The Gold-Dollar Anchor

The original Bretton Woods system, negotiated in 1944 as the Allied powers planned the post-war economic order, was architecturally simple:

- All major currencies were pegged to the US dollar at fixed exchange rates
- The US dollar was pegged to gold at $35 per troy ounce
- The US held approximately 70% of the world's monetary gold reserves at the end of World War II, giving the system its credibility
- The IMF and World Bank were created to manage balance-of-payments pressures and development finance

**The implicit contract:** The US would maintain dollar-gold convertibility, keeping monetary discipline. Other countries would hold dollars as reserves rather than gold, trusting that convertibility held.

**The inherent flaw (Triffin Dilemma, 1960):** For the dollar to serve as the world's reserve currency, the US had to run persistent current account deficits — supplying dollars to the rest of the world. But persistent deficits eroded confidence in dollar-gold convertibility. The more successful the dollar system became, the more it undermined the gold backing that made it credible.

By the late 1960s, the Vietnam War and Great Society spending had inflated the US money supply far beyond what the gold reserve could support at $35/oz. European central banks, led by France (De Gaulle explicitly cited Bretton Woods as an "exorbitant privilege"), began converting dollar reserves to gold. The US gold reserve fell from $22 billion in 1958 to $10 billion by 1971.

**The Nixon Shock (August 15, 1971):** Nixon unilaterally suspended dollar-gold convertibility. Bretton Woods I ended.

The system that replaced it was not designed. It emerged.

### 1.2 — Bretton Woods II (1971–?): The Petrodollar Arrangement

The dollar's post-1971 persistence as the global reserve currency was not automatic. It required a new anchor to replace gold. That anchor was oil.

**The 1974 Petrodollar Deal:** In 1974, Henry Kissinger negotiated an agreement with Saudi Arabia:
- Saudi Arabia would price all oil exports in US dollars
- Saudi Arabia would invest its oil surpluses ("petrodollar recycling") in US Treasury securities
- In return, the US would provide military protection and security guarantees to the Saudi regime

The deal was extended to the broader OPEC membership. It created a structural demand for dollars: any country that needed to import oil — which meant every country — needed to acquire and hold dollars. This demand for dollars was entirely independent of the US gold stock. It was anchored in physical necessity.

**The mechanism of dollar hegemony under Bretton Woods II:**

1. **Dollar demand creation:** Oil exporters price in dollars → oil importers hold dollar reserves → dollar demand is structurally embedded in global trade
2. **Petrodollar recycling:** OPEC surplus dollars flow back to US Treasuries → US borrows cheaply regardless of fiscal discipline → the "exorbitant privilege" Valéry Giscard d'Estaing named in 1965 persists in a new form
3. **Sanctions power:** Because almost all international transactions settle in dollars through SWIFT and US correspondent banks, the US Treasury can threaten or impose financial exclusion on any country running dollar transactions
4. **Network effects:** The larger the share of global trade priced in dollars, the higher the cost of switching to an alternative, which makes switching less likely

**What Bretton Woods II is NOT:** A formal agreement. There is no Bretton Woods II treaty. It is a self-reinforcing network of incentives, habits, infrastructure, and implicit security guarantees that has persisted for five decades because no single actor has had sufficient incentive and capability to break it — until now.

### 1.3 — The Structural Erosion Underway Before 2026

Bretton Woods II's erosion did not begin with the Iran war. The data trail is longer:

| Indicator | 2001 | 2015 | 2024 | Trend |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dollar share of global FX reserves | 73% | 66% | 57% | Declining |
| Dollar share of global trade invoicing | ~80% | ~75% | ~68% | Declining |
| Number of bilateral currency swap agreements (non-dollar) | ~12 | ~34 | ~60+ | Rising |
| BRICS New Development Bank local currency loan share | 0% (est.) | 10% | 30% (target) | Rising |
| Countries sanctioned by US Treasury OFAC | ~10 | ~20 | ~30+ | Rising |

**The most important driver:** The proliferation of US sanctions. Every country that the US sanctions has both a motive and an incentive to build non-dollar infrastructure. By 2024, the US had sanctioned or threatened to sanction so many countries — Russia, Iran, Venezuela, Cuba, North Korea, Myanmar, and portions of China — that a large fraction of the world's population lived in countries with structural incentives to reduce dollar exposure.

The sanctions tool was so widely deployed that it was losing a key attribute: credibility as a deterrent. When every actor outside the G7 faces potential sanctions exposure, the marginal deterrent effect of sanctioning the next actor declines.

**Saudi Arabia did not renew its petrodollar arrangement in June 2024.** The original 1974 agreement lapsed, and the kingdom chose not to formally renew it. This was not a rupture — Saudi Arabia continued pricing oil in dollars in practice. But the symbolism was stark: the cornerstone of the Bretton Woods II architecture was not formally recommitted.

## Part 2: Bretton Woods III — Zoltan Pozsar's Framework and What 2026 Has Confirmed

### 2.1 — The Pozsar Thesis (February 2022)

In February 2022, as the West imposed unprecedented financial sanctions on Russia following its invasion of Ukraine, Credit Suisse macro strategist Zoltan Pozsar published a widely-read note arguing that we were witnessing the birth of "Bretton Woods III."

His argument, compressed:

- **Bretton Woods I** was anchored by gold (commodity money)
- **Bretton Woods II** was anchored by US Treasuries (inside money — financial claims on a sovereign)
- **Bretton Woods III** will be anchored by commodities — specifically oil, gas, metals, and food — priced outside the dollar system

The mechanism: Russia's commodity exports (oil, gas, wheat, fertilizer) were now being routed outside SWIFT, priced partially in yuan and rubles, and settled through non-Western payment infrastructure. China was building the cross-border yuan payment system (CIPS) as an alternative to SWIFT. Saudi Arabia was conducting yuan oil trade discussions with Beijing.

**Pozsar's prediction:** "The rest of the world will be forced to price the world in commodities rather than in claims on the US government — and as commodity prices rise, the dollar will weaken against commodity-linked currencies."

### 2.2 — What Has Been Confirmed vs. What Has Not (July 2026)

Four and a half years after Pozsar's note, the Iran war provides the clearest stress test yet. Here is the scorecard:

**Confirmed:**

✓ **The yuan oil trade is real and scaling.** China now buys over 80% of Iran's seaborne oil exports, settled in yuan. Russian oil to China (approximately 2 million barrels/day) is largely settled in yuan or rubles. The yuan's share of global oil settlement has risen from near-zero in 2020 to an estimated 8-10% in 2026.

✓ **Blocking statutes are functional.** China's May 2026 invocation of its blocking statute — ordering domestic firms to ignore US sanctions on Iranian oil — is the most significant test of whether a non-US jurisdiction can create a "sanctions-free zone." The test is ongoing, but thus far China has not backed down, and Chinese firms have continued purchasing Iranian oil.

✓ **Iran's Hormuz yuan demand is a direct attack on the petrodollar architecture.** Demanding yuan payment as the price of Hormuz access is explicitly designed to link the world's most important physical chokepoint to a non-dollar payment system. The symbolism — and the commercial precedent — is intentional.

✓ **Dollar reserve share at a 25-year low.** Dollar share of global reserves: approximately 57% in 2024, down from 73% in 2001. Economist Jim O'Neill (who invented the BRIC acronym) stated in July 2026 that technological advances had made dollar alternatives "more realistic than they appeared 18 months ago."

**Not Yet Confirmed (the limits of Bretton Woods III):**

✗ **No single alternative currency has replaced the dollar.** The yuan is not a credible reserve currency at scale — China's capital account remains largely closed, yuan-denominated assets are illiquid by global standards, and no country willingly holds large yuan reserves without a strategic reason to do so.

✗ **SWIFT remains dominant.** CIPS (China's alternative) processes a fraction of SWIFT's volume and is primarily used for yuan-denominated transactions within the China trade sphere.

✗ **The "commodity-backed" system is not formalized.** Pozsar's Bretton Woods III implies a systematic, multi-country arrangement. What exists in 2026 is a patchwork: bilateral yuan deals, Russia-China energy barter, Iran-China yuanization, Saudi trials of yuan settlement. A patchwork is not a system.

**CEO Assessment:** Pozsar was directionally right but architecturally premature. We are not in Bretton Woods III. We are in the transition — a world of "dollar-centered competition" rather than dollar exclusivity. The defining monetary shift of the 2020s is not dollar replacement; it is dollar *fragmentation* — the emergence of a bifurcated financial system in which two parallel settlement architectures coexist: dollar-SWIFT for the G7 and aligned countries, and yuan-CIPS-barter for the non-aligned world.

### 2.3 — The Bifurcation Model: What the 2030 Architecture Looks Like

Based on current trajectory, the most likely 2030 monetary architecture is:

**The Dollar Zone (G7 + aligned):**
- Dollar remains the primary reserve currency for approximately 45 sovereign central banks
- SWIFT processes ~75-80% of global inter-bank settlement
- US Treasury market remains the world's deepest liquid asset for reserve accumulation
- US sanctions retain meaningful enforcement power within this zone

**The Non-Dollar Zone (China + BRICS+ + sanctioned states):**
- Yuan settles approximately 15-20% of global oil trade
- CIPS handles settlement for China-linked transactions
- BRICS CBDC interoperability framework (India's 2026 proposal) enables local-currency settlements between member states
- Gold plays a significant role as a neutral reserve asset between the two zones

**The Swing Tier (Middle Powers — India, UAE, Turkey, Brazil, Indonesia, Saudi Arabia):**
- Maintain access to both systems simultaneously
- Price exports in dollars for Western customers, yuan for Chinese customers
- Hold mixed reserves: dollars, yuan, gold, and SDRs
- Extract premium from both sides for flexibility — classic "strategic indispensability" (see Lesson 195 on Turkey)

**The structural implication:** The world is not moving from dollar hegemony to yuan hegemony. It is moving from dollar hegemony to **multi-system coexistence** with a contested middle. The contest will be fought over the swing tier — which settlement architecture do India, Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Brazil anchor their reserves and trade to?

## Part 3: The Iran War as Petrodollar Accelerant

### 3.1 — The Hormuz Yuan Demand: Mechanism and Precedent

Iran's demand — price oil in yuan as the cost of Hormuz passage — is more sophisticated than it first appears. Here is the mechanism:

**Layer 1 (Commercial):** Any tanker captain who agrees to yuan settlement for cargo is creating a precedent that non-dollar settlement is acceptable in the Hormuz corridor. Each voyage creates a data point.

**Layer 2 (Structural):** If the practice scales — even to 10-15% of Hormuz traffic — it normalizes yuan settlement for Persian Gulf oil trade. Refiners that accept yuan terms for Hormuz passage will adapt their treasury operations accordingly. This is how monetary transitions happen: not through formal agreements but through commercial necessity repeated at scale.

**Layer 3 (Geopolitical):** Iran is demonstrating that control of a physical chokepoint can be weaponized to force compliance with a non-dollar architecture. This is the inverse of the US sanctions tool — instead of threatening exclusion from the dollar system, Iran threatens exclusion from the physical corridor unless you use an alternative system. The leverage is geographic rather than financial.

**The precedent risk:** If Iran's yuan-access model persists — even in a frozen conflict — it establishes that sovereign actors controlling chokepoints can demand payment system compliance as the price of passage. Russia could apply a similar model to Arctic shipping routes. China could theoretically apply it to South China Sea passages. The geopolitical logic is transferable.

### 3.2 — China's Blocking Statute: The Legal Infrastructure of De-Dollarization

The May 2026 blocking statute invocation is architecturally significant. By ordering Chinese firms to ignore US secondary sanctions on Iranian oil trade, China has:

1. **Created a legal shield** for Chinese financial institutions participating in yuan oil settlement — they cannot be sued or sanctioned domestically for complying with Chinese law, even if they violate US regulations
2. **Forced a choice** on Chinese firms: comply with US law and exit Iranian trade, or comply with Chinese law and stay. Most chose to stay.
3. **Established that Chinese regulatory sovereignty can override US extraterritorial reach** within the Chinese corporate universe

This matters because secondary sanctions — US sanctions on non-US firms for doing business with sanctioned entities — were the primary mechanism by which the US extended dollar hegemony beyond its borders. If Chinese firms are effectively immune to secondary sanctions enforcement (because the Chinese government will shield them), then the sanction's deterrent effect on the China-Iran-Russia trade axis collapses.

**The long-term implication:** US secondary sanctions were always dependent on the assumption that foreign firms would prefer US market access over sanctioned-country business. China has made that tradeoff explicit — and has chosen to protect its own firms from the choice. This is not a temporary crisis response; it is the establishment of a parallel legal architecture for international commerce.

### 3.3 — The Petrodollar and the 2024 Saudi Non-Renewal

Saudi Arabia's decision not to formally renew the 1974 petrodollar agreement in June 2024 was underreported and largely misread at the time. The conventional interpretation was that it was a technical lapse — the original agreement had run its course and Saudi Arabia simply didn't bother renewing a formal instrument that had become informal practice.

The more accurate interpretation: Saudi Arabia was deliberately preserving optionality. By not renewing, the kingdom retained the ability to conduct yuan-settled oil sales without being in formal breach of an agreement. This is consistent with the UAE's and Saudi Arabia's participation in the mBridge CBDC project (a multi-central-bank digital currency platform for cross-border settlement that explicitly operates outside SWIFT).

In a frozen conflict scenario (lesson 210's base case), Saudi Arabia will continue pricing the majority of its oil in dollars for Western buyers while gradually expanding yuan settlement for Chinese buyers. This dual-track model is already operational in practice.

## Part 4: Investment Implications — Positioning for the Bifurcated Monetary World

### 4.1 — The Gold Case Strengthens

In both Bretton Woods I (commodity anchor) and the emerging Bretton Woods III (commodity-anchored bilateral trade), gold is the neutral asset that sits outside either currency's sphere. Central bank gold purchases — which hit record highs in 2022, 2023, and 2024 — reflect a structural shift: central banks in the non-dollar zone (China, Russia, India, Türkiye, the Gulf states) are diversifying reserves away from US Treasuries into gold.

**The investment thesis:** Gold is not just a safe-haven hedge in the traditional sense — it is the reserve asset of the bifurcated world. As the dollar zone and the non-dollar zone expand and compete, gold is the one asset both zones accept as neutral. Every dollar of US Treasuries that a non-G7 central bank sells is potential demand for gold.

**Data point:** Global central bank gold purchases in 2023: 1,037 tonnes — the highest since 1967. Emerging market central banks drove the majority. This trend has continued in 2024-2026.

### 4.2 — Currency Plays: Short the Dollar vs. Commodity-Linked EM FX

The bifurcation model creates a structural pressure on the dollar's reserve share:
- As more trade settles in yuan, fewer dollars are needed as trade finance
- As central banks diversify reserves (selling Treasuries, buying gold and yuan assets), marginal demand for dollars declines
- As US fiscal deficits remain elevated (the fiscal dominance thesis from Lesson 149), the supply of dollars continues to rise while structural demand stagnates

**The trade:** Long commodity-producing EM currencies (BRL, CLP, ZAR, CAD) against the dollar on a 12-18 month horizon. These currencies benefit when commodities are priced in alternative architectures — their exporters receive yuan or local currency payments for raw materials, reducing dollar dependency and supporting their currency.

**Caveat:** This trade is directional over 12-18 months, not a near-term position. Dollar short trades have punished investors in every cycle for 20 years. The structural thesis must be time-matched to a structural position.

### 4.3 — The Non-Dollar Settlement Infrastructure Play

The companies and infrastructure enabling non-dollar settlement are a legitimate structural investment theme:

| Infrastructure Type | Instrument | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Gold storage and custodianship | Sprott Physical Gold Trust (PHYS), Royal Canadian Mint gold | Growing institutional demand |
| Cross-border payment alternatives | No direct equity play; monitor CIPS volume as proxy | Indirect |
| EM commodity-linked bonds (yuan-denominated) | China's Panda bonds; yuan dim sum bonds | Illiquid but growing |
| Digital gold settlement platforms | Underdeveloped; watch for institutional CBDC platforms | Early stage |
| Gold mining in non-Western jurisdictions | Barrick (GOLD), Newmont (NEM), Franco-Nevada (FNV) | Direct exposure |

**CEO Recommendation:** The clearest investable manifestation of the Bretton Woods III thesis is gold — both physical (through ETFs or direct) and producers (through gold mining equities). The more speculative play on EM FX and commodity currencies is appropriate for a small position (5-10% of FX allocation) with a 12-18 month minimum hold.

### 4.4 — The US Treasury Risk — The Asset Nobody Is Pricing

Here is the uncomfortable implication of dollar bifurcation that most portfolios are not positioned for:

**If the dollar's reserve share continues declining from 57% toward 45-50% over the next decade, the buyers of US Treasury securities decline commensurately.** The US issues approximately $2-3 trillion in net new debt per year. Historically, a significant fraction was purchased by foreign central banks accumulating dollar reserves. If those central banks are accumulating gold and yuan assets instead, domestic buyers (US banks, insurance companies, money market funds) must absorb a larger fraction of Treasury issuance — at higher yields.

**Higher yields = lower existing bond prices = compressed government capacity to borrow cheaply.**

This is the fiscal dominance loop that Lesson 149 identified: fiscal deficits are themselves undermining the dollar's credibility as a reserve asset, which reduces demand for Treasuries, which raises interest costs, which worsens deficits. The Iran war has accelerated one side of this loop (foreign demand decline) while the US fiscal situation worsens the other side (supply increase).

**Investment implication:** Underweight long-duration US Treasuries relative to historical allocations. Overweight TIPS (inflation-linked) if you must hold US government bonds. The gold overweight partially substitutes for the traditional "safe haven" role that long-dated Treasuries once provided.
