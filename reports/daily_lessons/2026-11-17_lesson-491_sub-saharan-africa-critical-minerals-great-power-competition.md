## 1. The Structural Position

Sub-Saharan Africa is not one story. It is 46 countries, 1.2 billion people, dozens of languages, and geopolitical dynamics ranging from sophisticated middle-power diplomacy (South Africa, Kenya) to fragile state collapse (DRC, Sudan, Mali) to resource-funded autocracy (Equatorial Guinea, Gabon).

What unites the continent as a **single investment thesis** is not political cohesion — it is geology.

**The mineral endowment is extraordinary:**

| Mineral | Africa's Global Share | Primary Countries |
|---|---|---|
| Cobalt | ~70% of global reserves | DRC (dominant) |
| Chromium | ~44% | South Africa, Zimbabwe |
| Manganese | ~90% of seaborne supply | South Africa, Gabon |
| Platinum Group Metals | ~90% | South Africa |
| Copper | ~40% of future supply growth | DRC, Zambia, Congo |
| Uranium | ~20% | Namibia, Niger, Zambia |
| Lithium | Growing fast | Zimbabwe, DRC, Mali |
| Rare earths | Significant underdeveloped deposits | Multiple |
| Bauxite | ~25% | Guinea (40% of world's known bauxite) |

The energy transition — EVs, wind turbines, solar panels, grid-scale batteries — requires a step-change increase in every one of these minerals. No credible energy transition scenario works without Africa's geology.

This is the structural argument. The investment question is: **who captures the value?**

## 2. The Great Power Competition: Who Is Actually Winning

### China: The Infrastructure-for-Access Model

China's presence in Africa is 20 years deep and structurally dominant. The strategy:
- Build infrastructure (railways, ports, roads) using Chinese state financing, Chinese companies, and Chinese labor
- Secure preferential access to mining concessions and offtake agreements in exchange
- Price out Western competitors by accepting lower returns, tolerating political risk, and moving faster

**The DRC case is the clearest example.** China — primarily through CMOC (formerly Freeport's DRC partner), Zijin Mining, and other SOE-linked operators — controls over 72% of Congolese copper and cobalt production. The Sicomines deal (copper/cobalt for infrastructure) is the template: China built roads and hospitals; China got the mines.

**In 2026, Chinese lending to sub-Saharan Africa has become net negative** — more debt is being repaid to China than new lending is flowing in. This reflects two things simultaneously:
1. China's own fiscal constraints (domestic real estate crisis, fiscal pressure)
2. African countries' debt distress (Zambia, Ghana, Ethiopia defaulted; debt restructuring negotiations ongoing)

China's Africa model is maturing past its peak lending phase into an **operational phase** — Chinese companies now run the mines, the ports, and significant portions of the logistics infrastructure. The loans are being paid off (or restructured); the operational control remains.

### United States: The Late Entry

The US approach to Africa has been episodic and inconsistently funded. The Biden era produced **Lobito Corridor** (a US-backed rail corridor from DRC copper belt to Angolan port of Lobito, in competition with China's TAZARA railway), **Mineral Security Partnership (MSP)**, and the December 2025 US-DRC strategic partnership giving US companies preferential mining access.

The Trump administration pivot: **transactional rather than developmental**. The February 2026 Critical Minerals Ministerial in Washington brought 54 African leaders to negotiate supply security — not development. Africa heard the signal clearly: Washington wants minerals, not allies in any robust sense.

**The honest assessment from multiple analysts:** The US is significantly behind China in African mineral access, operational presence, and diplomatic capital. The gap cannot be closed by offtake agreements alone. China has 20 years of relationship infrastructure; the US has 20 months of renewed interest.

**What the US does have:** capital markets access, technology, and a consumer base that Chinese companies need. The leverage exists — it is just not being used strategically.

### Russia: The Security-for-Access Model

Russia's strategy is simpler and more cynical. Following France's withdrawal from the Sahel (Mali, Burkina Faso, Niger expelled French forces 2022-2024), Russia's Africa Corps (formerly Wagner Group) stepped in as the security guarantor of choice for military governments.

The model:
- Provide armed protection to coup-installed governments facing jihadist insurgencies
- Extract payment in gold mining concessions, timber rights, and diplomatic alignment
- Provide political cover in the UN Security Council (blocking Western pressure)

**Countries with Russia/Africa Corps presence (2026):** Mali, Burkina Faso, Niger, Central African Republic, Sudan, Libya, Mozambique (limited), Zimbabwe (security cooperation).

**The investment angle:** Russia's Africa presence creates a risk category — **sanctioned supply chains**. Gold from CAR and Sudan, cobalt from DRC via middlemen, uranium from Niger — all risk touching sanctioned entities. This creates a **compliance premium** for Western companies sourcing minerals through transparent chains.

### France: The Strategic Retreat

France's humiliation in the Sahel is one of the most significant geopolitical shifts in Africa in decades. Operation Barkhane (counter-jihadist military operation) was France's presence in Mali since 2013. The 2021 coup (and subsequent 2023 coup) expelled France entirely. Niger, Burkina Faso, Guinea — the Sahel has become a French-free zone.

This matters for investment because **the CFA franc zone** (14 West African countries peg their currency to the euro, administered by the French Treasury) is under political pressure. Mali, Burkina Faso, and Niger announced in 2023 they would leave the BCEAO/ECOWAS CFA framework. If the CFA pegs dissolve, it creates one of the largest EM currency regime changes in decades — affecting monetary policy, inflation dynamics, and trade across West Africa.

**The EU's response:** Attempting to build a separate relationship with Africa through the EU-AU summit framework and development funding, independent of France. Too early to assess effectiveness.

### Gulf States: The Quiet Capital

The least-discussed but increasingly significant player. Saudi Arabia, UAE, and Qatar are deploying sovereign wealth capital into African agriculture, infrastructure, and minerals — focused on **food security** (Saudi Arabia and UAE import nearly all food) and **diversification** (post-oil positioning).

DP World (UAE) has acquired port operations across Africa (Berbera in Somaliland, Dar es Salaam, Lagos Apapa). The UAE is also a critical node in African gold trading — over 400 tonnes of gold flowed through Dubai annually from African sources, some from conflict zones.

**This is under-reported and underweighted in Western analysis.** Gulf capital is long-horizon, tolerates political risk, and has no democratic governance requirements. It competes with China in some areas while coordinating in others.

## 3. The DRC: The Single Most Important Mineral Story on Earth

The Democratic Republic of Congo deserves its own section because no asset in the energy transition is more concentrated in a single jurisdiction than cobalt — and the DRC is that jurisdiction.

**The numbers:**
- DRC produces approximately 70% of global cobalt supply
- Cobalt is essential for lithium-ion battery cathodes (NMC, NCA chemistries)
- The DRC also holds the world's second-largest copper reserves (critical for electrification and EVs)
- And significant lithium deposits (Manono lithium project — one of the world's largest hard-rock lithium deposits)

**The governance reality:** The DRC state has effectively governed perhaps 30-40% of its territory for most of its post-colonial history. The east — where most of the minerals are — has been in continuous conflict since the 1990s. The M23 rebel group (backed by Rwanda according to UN reports) controls significant territory in mineral-rich North Kivu. Artisanal mining (ASM) accounts for 15-25% of cobalt production, often involving child labor.

**The December 2025 US-DRC deal:** A strategic partnership giving US companies preferential mining access in exchange for infrastructure and security cooperation. This is a direct challenge to Chinese operational dominance. It will take 5-10 years to operationally matter — but the signal is significant.

**The investment implication:** Cobalt supply concentration in the DRC is **unresolvable in the near term**. Any supply disruption (conflict escalation, export ban, nationalization) spikes cobalt prices globally. Meanwhile, battery chemistry innovation is slowly reducing cobalt intensity — LFP (lithium iron phosphate) batteries use zero cobalt and are capturing market share. The long-run cobalt thesis is **shorter horizon and more volatile** than the copper thesis.

## 4. South Africa: The Swing State of the South

South Africa is the continent's most sophisticated and most watched economy. With a population of 62 million, GDP of ~$400B, and the most liquid capital markets in Africa, it is the entry point for most institutional Africa exposure.

**The 2024-2026 political landscape:** The ANC's coalition government with the DA (Democratic Alliance) and other parties has stabilized the political center — but governance challenges remain severe. Load-shedding (rolling power outages) has damaged industrial output for three consecutive years. The state utility ESKOM's debt restructuring is ongoing. Unemployment is persistently above 32%.

**South Africa's geopolitical position in 2026:**
- BRICS+ member (hosted the 2023 summit, admitted as founding BRICS+ member)
- ICC proceedings: Charged Israel with genocide at the ICJ — earning it both condemnation from the US and significant credibility across the Global South
- Non-aligned on Russia-Ukraine
- G20 presidency (2025) used to push African debt relief and Global South agenda

**The platinum thesis:** South Africa produces ~75% of global platinum group metals (PGM). PGMs are critical for hydrogen fuel cell catalysts. The **green hydrogen thesis** depends heavily on platinum catalyst costs — and those costs are determined in South Africa's Bushveld Complex. If green hydrogen becomes commercially viable (which requires platinum prices to stay manageable), South Africa's mining economy gets a significant long-horizon catalyst beyond traditional automotive catalysts.

**The ZAR:** The South African rand is the world's most liquid emerging market currency after the BRL and MXN. It trades as a **proxy for EM risk appetite and commodity confidence.** A ZAR position is simultaneously a bet on commodity cycles, South African politics, and global risk sentiment. It is volatile and often mispriced in the short term — which creates opportunity for the patient investor.

## 5. The Sahel Crisis: Implications Beyond Africa

The collapse of French-backed security architecture across the Sahel has investment implications that extend beyond Africa.

**The uranium case:** Niger produces approximately 5% of global uranium. France's nuclear fleet (which produces ~70% of France's electricity) has historically sourced significant uranium from Niger. The 2023 coup expelled France; Niger's military government signed a uranium deal with Russia in 2024.

**The implication for European energy security:** France has been scrambling to diversify uranium sourcing (Kazakhstan, Canada, Australia). EU electricity market exposure to Niger uranium disruption is an underappreciated risk factor for European energy prices.

**The gold smuggling network:** Sahel gold — from Burkina Faso, Mali, Sudan — flows through Russian-linked intermediaries to Dubai and then into the global market. This is a **sanction evasion network at commodity scale**. Western financial institutions processing African gold exposure without full supply chain auditing are carrying unpriced compliance risk.

**Long-horizon thesis (6–18 months):**

| Theme | Asset / Instrument | Direction | Condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Copper demand structural | Freeport-McMoRan, Ivanhoe Mines (DRC/Kamoa-Kakula), Zambia copper royalties | Long | EV adoption + electrification continues |
| Cobalt supply volatility | Cobalt futures, Glencore (single largest cobalt producer) | Volatile long with hedge | DRC stability, LFP substitution pace |
| PGM / Green hydrogen optionality | Anglo American Platinum (Amplats), Sibanye-Stillwater | Long-horizon speculative | Green H2 cost curves |
| South African ZAR | ZAR/USD trade | Long on commodity upcycle; short on political deterioration | Load-shedding resolution, ESKOM trajectory |
| African sovereign debt distress | Zambian bonds (restructured), Ghanaian bonds (IMF program) | Selective long (risk/reward post-restructuring) | IMF program compliance |
| Nigeria — oil and reform | Nigerian equities, Nigerian Eurobonds | Long on naira reform progress | Tinubu government fiscal reform execution |
| Kenya — tech and financial hub | Nairobi-listed equities; Kenya shilling | Long on regional tech hub | East Africa political stability |
| CFA franc dissolution risk | West African sovereign bonds | Hedge / avoid until clarity | BCEAO political trajectory |

**The macro overlay:** Africa's mineral wealth is **long-horizon** and structurally bullish for the right commodities. The near-term risk is governance and supply chain compliance. The medium-term risk is China's operational dominance being dislodged slowly (or not at all). The long-term opportunity is capturing mineral supply that the energy transition *must* have.

## 7. Databricks Angle

**Pipeline Idea: Africa Critical Minerals Monitor**

Build a country-risk-adjusted supply disruption monitor for the five most strategically important minerals (cobalt, copper, PGM, lithium, uranium) using African source country data.

| Dataset | Source | Signal |
|---|---|---|
| GDELT event data by country | GDELT Project | Conflict intensity in mining regions |
| ACLED (Armed Conflict Location & Event Data) | ACLED | Fine-grained conflict events by province |
| World Bank governance indicators | World Bank API | Governance quality score per country |
| Commodity futures prices | Yahoo Finance / Quandl | Price signal for supply tightness |
| UN Comtrade mineral exports | UN Comtrade | Actual export volumes by country |
| DRC mining permit database | DRC mining ministry (where available) | Concession activity |

**Alpha signal:** Build a **Mineral Supply Risk Index (MSRI)** per commodity: weight it by country share of global supply × country ACLED conflict intensity × governance score. When MSRI spikes for cobalt (DRC conflict escalation + governance deterioration), model predicts 60-90 day cobalt price lag response. Historical validation available from 2016-2026 data.

**Pipeline architecture:**
1. GDELT + ACLED → daily conflict intensity score per mining country
2. World Bank governance → quarterly update layer
3. Comtrade → monthly export volume baseline
4. Futures prices → real-time signal
5. Composite MSRI → alert when index crosses 2σ threshold
6. Lag-correlate MSRI against futures price movement (target: 30-90 day lead time)

**Key gotcha:** ACLED data has province-level granularity. DRC's Lualaba Province (copper/cobalt) and North Kivu (conflict) must be tracked separately — they are geographically distinct but commercially linked via supply chain.

## 8. The CEO Verdict

Sub-Saharan Africa is **the most structurally important and operationally difficult** investment geography for the next decade.

**The bull case:** The energy transition creates demand for minerals that only Africa can supply at scale. Rising African populations (the continent will be 2.5 billion by 2050, nearly all of the world's demographic growth) create a consumer market that will matter. Infrastructure gaps mean enormous capital return potential. And governance is slowly improving in the second tier of markets (Kenya, Ghana, Tanzania, Rwanda).

**The bear case:** Chinese operational dominance is 20 years deep and not dislodged by Western ministerials. Governance risk is real — DRC, Sudan, Mali, Burkina Faso are not investable in any conventional sense. The resource curse is not a myth; it is documented across dozens of African commodity economies. And the energy transition timeline itself is uncertain — LFP batteries reducing cobalt demand, geothermal and other minerals entering the picture.

**What the market underweights in 2026:**
1. The **CFA franc dissolution risk** — if West Africa moves off the euro peg, it's a larger EM currency event than most model
2. The **Kamoa-Kakula copper mine in DRC** (Ivanhoe Mines/Zijin joint venture) is coming online as one of the world's largest copper producers — this is a structurally positive copper supply story that the market treats as a risk story because of DRC governance
3. The **US-DRC strategic partnership** will take years to operationalize but signals a medium-horizon shift in Western engagement that will gradually compete with Chinese access

**Portfolio note:** African exposure should be held through liquid proxies — Freeport, Glencore, Anglo American Platinum — rather than direct EM country exposure, unless sovereign debt distress plays are specifically the thesis. Direct DRC equity or mining concession exposure requires specialist risk management that retail investors cannot replicate.

## Reflection Questions

1. **The resource curse mechanism:** Oil and mineral wealth has historically weakened African governance (the resource curse hypothesis). But Indonesia used resource nationalism (nickel export bans) to successfully industrialize its battery supply chain. What is the structural difference between Indonesia's outcome and DRC's outcome? What does that difference imply about the conditions required for resource nationalism to work?

2. **The China operational lock-in:** China controls 72% of DRC copper/cobalt production. Even if the US-DRC deal succeeds and US companies win new concessions, Chinese companies will still own the majority of existing production for decades. What investment thesis survives — and what gets disrupted — if the US-China DRC competition ends in a **managed coexistence** rather than a displacement?

3. **The compliance premium:** As ESG frameworks and supply chain legislation (EU Critical Raw Materials Act, US Inflation Reduction Act sourcing requirements) tighten, cobalt and gold from DRC artisanal mining and Wagner-linked gold from the Sahel become harder to sell into regulated markets. Who benefits from a supply chain compliance premium — and can you construct a long position based on that benefit?
