## Section 2: The Concept — The International Law of Straits, Transit Passage, and Why Iran's Toll-to-Fee Rebranding Is a Genuine Legal Move

### 2a. The Architecture: UNCLOS Part III

The United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), finalized in 1982 and entering into force in 1994, codified a regime for international straits in Part III (Articles 34–45) that represented the first time this body of customary law was formalized in a binding multilateral treaty. The core principle is **transit passage** (Article 38): all ships and aircraft enjoy the right of continuous and expeditious passage through straits used for international navigation. This right cannot be suspended by the bordering state. The bordering state cannot "hamper transit passage" (Article 44) and cannot impede it or impose conditions on it. These provisions are unconditional in their language.

But there is one notable gap. **UNCLOS contains no explicit prohibition on fees for services in international straits.** Compare this to the territorial sea provisions: Article 26 states explicitly that "No charge may be levied upon foreign ships by reason only of their passage through the territorial sea" — and then Article 26(2) carves out an exception: "Charges may be levied upon a foreign ship passing through the territorial sea as payment only for specific services rendered to the ship." The strait articles (37–44) contain no equivalent fee-prohibition language. They focus entirely on what bordering states cannot do (suspend, hamper, impede, require authorization for passage) — not on whether they may charge for services incident to passage.

This is Iran's legal wedge. The regime that prohibits tolls explicitly is the territorial-sea regime. The transit-passage regime doesn't transplant that prohibition directly. A state could argue — and Iran's legal team is arguing — that fees for navigation assistance, vessel traffic services, maritime accident response, and environmental protection are services rendered to ships, not charges for the right of passage itself, and thus fall into the Article 26(2) services carve-out by analogy, without being prohibited by the strait articles.

### 2b. The UNCLOS Non-Ratification Problem

The argument above depends on which body of law actually governs. Here the situation becomes structurally unusual: **neither the United States nor Iran has ratified UNCLOS.** The US Senate has refused ratification since 1982, largely over objections to the deep-seabed mining provisions; successive administrations have treated most of UNCLOS as reflecting customary international law while objecting to the deep-seabed chapter. Iran's non-ratification predates the US dispute and relates to different political dynamics. Both countries operate under pre-UNCLOS customary law and the 1958 Geneva Convention on the Territorial Sea and Contiguous Zone (which Iran has ratified), which does not resolve the transit-passage fee question with the clarity of the UNCLOS text.

The practical consequence: the US cannot use UNCLOS dispute-resolution mechanisms (Part XV, including the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea) to challenge Iran's fee regime. Iran is not bound by UNCLOS in a treaty sense, so it cannot be held to the text as treaty law. The dispute has to be argued on customary international law grounds, which are both less precise and slower-moving than treaty adjudication. Iran has almost certainly gamed this out: by operating in a legal gray zone that will take years to adjudicate (if it reaches adjudication at all), it can establish a de facto fee regime while the legal question remains formally open.

### 2c. The Oman Factor: Joint Administration as Legal Cover

By announcing "joint sovereignty" over the strait with Oman — and institutionalizing this claim through the Muscat Joint Hormuz Committee — Iran has taken a second legal step that is strategically significant. Oman ratified UNCLOS in 1989 and has a record of constructive maritime diplomacy (it was a key mediator in the 2015 and 2022 US-Iran prisoner exchanges). By positioning the fee regime as a joint Iran-Oman governance arrangement — not a unilateral Iranian action — Iran makes it structurally harder for the international community to characterize the fees as a rogue state's extortion. If Oman's name is on the governance framework and Oman has committed to "applicable international law," the regime acquires a veneer of multilateral legitimacy. Note the language in the joint statement: both countries reaffirmed commitment to "safe passage through the Strait in line with applicable international law, while emphasizing their sovereignty and sovereign rights over their territorial waters." The phrase "applicable international law" is doing a great deal of work — it is not a concession that UNCLOS governs, only that whatever law applies will be respected. A careful reader will note that both sides can agree to this sentence while completely disagreeing about what "applicable international law" requires.

### 2d. The 400-Year Precedent: Danish Sound Dues (1429–1857)

The one genuine historical case of a functional strait toll is instructive precisely because it challenges the assumption that international law inevitably wins over a sovereign's revenue interest.

From 1429, Denmark charged tolls on every foreign ship passing through Øresund — the strait between the Danish island of Zealand and the Swedish province of Scania, connecting the Baltic to the North Sea. The regime was initially tied to lighthouse and safe-passage services (a proto-fees-for-services framing that predated modern international law by 500 years), but it evolved into a flat revenue mechanism. At peak, the Sound Dues generated roughly two-thirds of the Danish Crown's total revenue. Ships that refused to pay were boarded. Major trading nations — the Dutch Republic, England, Sweden, the Hanseatic cities — complained, negotiated, and sometimes went to war over the dues, but none succeeded in abolishing them unilaterally. The regime persisted for **428 years**, until the 1857 Copenhagen Treaty (Sound Dues Convention), in which a coalition of 63 nations collectively paid Denmark a one-time buyout of approximately 35.7 million rix-dollars to permanently abolish the tolls. The key lesson: the mechanism that ended the Sound Dues was a **financial buyout by a coalition of users**, not legal adjudication, military coercion, or bilateral negotiation. Denmark agreed to abolish tolls because the buyout gave it capital it valued more than the revenue stream. The regime ended when the commercial and diplomatic cost of maintaining it was priced higher than the alternative — not when it was declared illegal.

The Sound Dues precedent is imperfect (Øresund is not the sole alternative route as Hormuz effectively is; Denmark was a medium European power, not a sanctioned regional actor). But it establishes a structural point: a state controlling a strait with no realistic alternative route can maintain a toll/fee regime for as long as it values the revenue more than the political costs, regardless of what international law says. Iran's nuclear negotiating team has almost certainly noted that the US framing — "a nuclear deal is worth more than the fees" — is precisely the Sound-Dues-buyout logic applied to the 21st century. The US is arguing Iran should take a higher-value offer; the question is whether Iran believes the offer is real.

### 2e. The Modern Precedents: What Bosphorus and Suez Tell Us (and What They Don't)

**Suez Canal:** A man-made canal running through sovereign Egyptian territory — not a natural strait covered by the transit-passage regime. Egypt can charge fees because it owns the infrastructure; the relevant legal question is about the 1888 Constantinople Convention (which guaranteed free use in time of war and peace) rather than transit passage. Nasser's 1956 nationalization of the canal was about replacing British-French management with Egyptian management; fees continued before and after. Suez tells us nothing about the legal permissibility of strait fees — it confirms canal fees are clearly legal, which is a different question.

**Bosphorus/Turkish Straits:** Governed by the 1936 Montreux Convention, not UNCLOS. Turkey has historically levied modest fees for pilotage, tugboat assistance, and lighthouse services. These have generally been treated as legitimate under Montreux because they are genuinely tied to services that make passage safer in a narrow, high-traffic waterway. Turkey has never successfully levied fees for passage itself. What Turkey has done — and continues to do — is use Montreux's explicit warship-transit rules as a geopolitical tool, blocking Russian warships from entering the Black Sea after the February 2022 invasion. This is the more directly comparable action: using a strait-governance regime as geopolitical leverage, within the bounds of an agreed convention, to constrain an adversary's military options. The lesson for Hormuz: legal regimes governing straits can serve as foreign policy tools without constituting outright illegality.

## Key Concepts Covered

- **UNCLOS Part III (Articles 34–45) and the transit-passage regime**: the right is unconditional, but the prohibition on fees is in the territorial-sea articles (Article 26), not the strait articles — creating a legal wedge Iran has now formally occupied by rebranding "tolls" as "fees for services"
- **The UNCLOS non-ratification problem**: neither the US nor Iran has ratified UNCLOS, making ITLOS dispute-resolution unavailable and pushing the legal dispute onto slower-moving customary international law — a structural advantage for the party that wants to establish a de facto regime while the legal question remains formally open
- **The Oman factor**: joint-sovereignty framing converts a unilateral Iranian action into a bilateral Iran-Oman governance arrangement, acquiring multilateral legitimacy cover and leveraging Oman's constructive diplomatic reputation and UNCLOS ratification
- **Danish Sound Dues (1429–1857)**: the only historical case of a functional strait toll maintained for centuries, ending via a 63-nation financial buyout rather than legal adjudication — the precedent that establishes that financial negotiation (not law) is the mechanism for resolving strait-revenue disputes, and that the US "a nuclear deal is worth more than the fees" framing is precisely this buyout logic
- **Bosphorus vs. Suez as comparison cases**: canal fees (Suez) are clearly legal and irrelevant to this dispute; strait-governance regimes (Bosphorus/Montreux) have historically permitted modest service fees while prohibiting passage charges — the distinction the Iran-Oman framework is attempting to claim
