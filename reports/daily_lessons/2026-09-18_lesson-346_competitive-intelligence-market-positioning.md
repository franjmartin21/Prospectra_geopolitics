## I. The Institutional Geopolitical Intelligence Market — Structure First

The geopolitical intelligence market for institutional clients is smaller, more concentrated, and more relationship-driven than most outsiders assume. Understanding its structure is the prerequisite to competitive positioning.

### Market Segmentation

The market segments along two axes: **product format** (bespoke vs. standardized) and **client type** (government/defense vs. commercial/financial).

| Segment | Product Format | Primary Buyers | Key Players |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Strategic advisory** | Bespoke — client-specific, project-based | Corporations, governments, sovereign funds | Control Risks, Eurasia Group (advisory arm), Kroll |
| **Subscription research** | Standardized — weekly/monthly reports, scorecards | Asset managers, hedge funds, corporates | Eurasia Group, Oxford Analytica, Verisk Maplecroft |
| **Quantitative risk scoring** | Fully standardized — numerical scores, data feeds | Banks, insurers, credit agencies | Verisk Maplecroft, S&P Global, Moody's Analytics Country Risk |
| **Data infrastructure** | Raw data + API | Quant funds, risk systems, BI platforms | GDELT, Accenture Security, MSCI Country Risk |

Prospectra's product — the GRI plus analytical research subscriptions — competes primarily in the **subscription research** and **quantitative risk scoring** segments. These are also the most commoditized and the most legacy-dominated. Understanding why requires understanding each competitor.

## II. The Incumbents — A Precise Assessment

### Eurasia Group

**Founded:** 1998. Ian Bremmer, PhD political science (Stanford). Current revenue: estimated $70–80M annually.

**What they do:** Geopolitical research subscriptions (the GZERO brand), bespoke advisory, annual "Top Risks" publication, major events calendar, speakers bureau for Bremmer.

**Why they win:**
- Ian Bremmer is one of the few geopolitical analysts with genuine mass-market and institutional credibility simultaneously. He is a media presence (CNN, Bloomberg TV regular), which gives Eurasia Group constant free marketing and legitimacy laundering.
- Their analytical framework (Political Risk Score, G-Zero model) is well-branded and has been published and debated in mainstream outlets since 2012.
- The client base is large and loyal. Major financial institutions that have subscribed for 5+ years face real switching costs: institutional memory, workflow integration, compliance documentation.

**Structural weaknesses:**
- **The Bremmer dependency problem:** Eurasia Group's brand is inseparable from a single founder. Institutional clients are sophisticated enough to notice that the analytical quality below the Bremmer level is uneven. When Bremmer is not personally on the call, the product quality variance is high.
- **The narrative-over-quantitative gap:** Eurasia Group produces excellent prose analysis but limited quantitative infrastructure. Their country risk scores are not systematically structured or methodology-documented in the way institutional buyers increasingly demand for internal risk models.
- **The media-commercial tension:** Bremmer's media presence optimizes for provocation and accessibility. This occasionally damages analytical credibility with the most technically sophisticated institutional buyers — those who know that political risk is more granular, more probabilistic, and less quotable than TV appearances suggest.
- **Pricing:** Institutional subscriptions are expensive ($25K–$100K+ annually for full access), creating a mid-market gap for institutions that want structured geopolitical analysis but cannot justify Eurasia Group pricing.

**Prospectra's positioning against Eurasia Group:** We are not competing for Eurasia Group's core institutional clients in year 1 or year 2. We are offering a methodologically structured, quantitatively grounded alternative to institutions that find Eurasia Group's narrative format analytically insufficient — and to institutions that cannot afford their pricing.

### Oxford Analytica

**Founded:** 1975 by David Young (Oxford University network). Revenue: estimated $30–40M annually.

**What they do:** The Oxford Analytica Daily Brief — a daily publication covering global political and economic developments with a characteristic analytical format. Network-sourced analysis from Oxford, Cambridge, and other academic institutions.

**Why they win:**
- The Oxford brand and the academic network model create genuine credibility differentiation. When an Oxford Analytica brief on Indonesian political risk is sourced from a University of Indonesia political scientist with 30 years in Jakarta, that is genuinely harder to replicate than a GRI score from a general framework.
- Their daily format and breadth of coverage is unmatched. They publish on more countries, more consistently, than any competitor.
- The product has been institutionally embedded since the 1980s. Compliance teams, risk functions, and treasury departments at global banks have built processes around the Oxford Analytica format.

**Structural weaknesses:**
- **The breadth-depth tradeoff:** Covering 200+ countries with daily output requires a network model where quality is inherently variable. The Oxford Analytica brief on Venezuela may be excellent; the brief on Tajikistan may be thin. Institutional buyers who care deeply about specific countries often supplement Oxford Analytica with more specialized sourcing.
- **The format problem:** The Oxford Analytica format is verbal-analytic, not quantitative. There is no scoring system, no structured data output, no API. In a world where institutional risk teams are building quantitative models, Oxford Analytica's output requires human translation into numbers — an additional analytical layer that adds friction.
- **The academic tempo:** Academic networks produce thoughtful analysis but not fast analysis. When a risk event breaks in real time, the Oxford Analytica model produces a rigorous, well-sourced piece 24–48 hours after the fact. For institutional clients managing active positions, 24–48 hours can be too slow.

**Prospectra's positioning against Oxford Analytica:** Our GRI is a direct response to the format problem. We are offering structured, quantitative, machine-readable geopolitical risk output — exactly what Oxford Analytica cannot produce. For clients building internal risk models or integrating geopolitical risk into portfolio systems, this is a genuine structural differentiation.

### Control Risks

**Founded:** 1975 (originally as a kidnap and ransom advisory firm for oil companies operating in Colombia). Revenue: estimated $500M+ annually.

**What they do:** Broad security, political risk, and compliance advisory. Their analytical products include the RiskMap country risk database and the RiskRadar due diligence platform. Strong client base in FTSE 100, US Fortune 500, energy companies, private equity.

**Why they win:**
- Deep operational history in exactly the sectors where geopolitical risk is highest: energy, extractive industries, infrastructure projects in frontier markets. When a mining company is deciding whether to proceed with a $2B project in a country with recent government instability, Control Risks has the operational credibility to be the risk advisor, not just the research provider.
- The compliance and due diligence platform is embedded in procurement and legal processes. Corporate compliance teams use RiskRadar for third-party due diligence. This is the Bloomberg terminal effect applied to corporate compliance.
- Global field presence: unlike most analytical firms, Control Risks maintains analysts physically located in high-risk markets. This creates genuine intelligence advantages on ground-level political dynamics.

**Structural weaknesses:**
- **The security firm identity problem:** Control Risks' heritage in security and crisis management means financial buyers often perceive it as a security firm that also does political risk — rather than a financial intelligence firm. This perception gap is structural. It limits penetration into pure-play financial institutions (asset managers, hedge funds, pension funds) where the security-firm association reduces credibility.
- **The scale cost:** Control Risks' global field presence and operational infrastructure makes them expensive to sustain and expensive for clients. Their target market is large corporates and governments, not mid-market financial institutions.
- **Product complexity:** The RiskMap and RiskRadar platforms are powerful but have steep learning curves. For an investment analyst who needs a weekly directional view, Control Risks' comprehensive platform is over-engineered.

**Prospectra's positioning against Control Risks:** We are not competing in their core market (corporate security, operational risk in frontier markets). The positioning gap is with financial buyers — asset managers and pension funds — who find Control Risks' operational-security framing irrelevant to their portfolio decision process. For a macro analyst deciding on EM allocation, Prospectra's financial-lens geopolitical analysis is a better fit than a platform designed for corporate security teams.

### Verisk Maplecroft

**Founded:** Maplecroft founded 1999; acquired by Verisk Analytics in 2021. Parent company Verisk revenue: ~$2.9B annually.

**What they do:** Country and supply chain risk indices, ESG risk scoring, climate and political risk data products. Strong client base in insurance, asset management, and supply chain compliance.

**Why they win:**
- The most systematically quantitative of the major players. Verisk Maplecroft produces actual numerical indices — the Climate Change Vulnerability Index, the Political Risk Atlas, the Human Rights indices — with methodology documentation that satisfies institutional compliance requirements.
- The Verisk parent company relationship gives Maplecroft access to insurance and actuarial client bases that are deeply quantitative by nature. These buyers understand risk indices, use them in pricing models, and are comfortable with structured data outputs.
- The ESG-geopolitics intersection is a Maplecroft specialty. As ESG integration became mandatory for many institutional investors, geopolitical risk scoring became relevant to ESG due diligence — a market that Maplecroft was early to and has continued to dominate.

**Structural weaknesses:**
- **The corporate-ESG framing:** Maplecroft's products are optimized for ESG due diligence and supply chain risk, not for investment signal generation. An asset manager trying to translate a Maplecroft score into a directional view on EM bonds or commodity prices has to do significant interpretive work. The product is built for the compliance team, not the portfolio manager.
- **The data-not-insight problem:** Maplecroft produces excellent indices but limited analytical narrative. The what (the score) is excellent; the *so what* (what does this mean for your portfolio decision?) is underdeveloped. Institutional clients often find they need to supplement Maplecroft data with a separate analytical service.
- **Verisk corporate culture:** Post-acquisition, Maplecroft has become embedded in Verisk's large-company culture. Product development cycles are slower, the analytical voice is more homogenized, and the ability to produce genuinely contrarian views has diminished. The institutional knowledge that made Maplecroft distinctive is gradually diluting.

**Prospectra's positioning against Verisk Maplecroft:** Maplecroft is the closest structural competitor to the Prospectra GRI — both produce quantitative country risk scores. The positioning argument is: Prospectra builds the same quantitative rigor but integrates it with investment-grade analytical narrative specifically designed for portfolio decisions, not ESG compliance. We tell you not just the GRI score but what it implies for your position in Saudi Aramco bonds, your EM equity allocation, or your commodity exposure.

## III. The Competitive Map — Where Prospectra Lives

Having assessed each competitor precisely, the honest competitive positioning becomes clear.

### What Prospectra Is NOT

- Not a full-service advisory firm (no bespoke projects, no field teams, no crisis management)
- Not a daily brief service (we do not have the network to produce credible daily output on 200 countries)
- Not a corporate security or compliance platform
- Not a narrative-first analysis firm optimizing for Davos keynotes

### What Prospectra IS — The Genuine Differentiation

**1. Investment-native analytical framework.** Every competitor was founded with a non-financial buyer as the primary client — government agencies, corporations, security teams, ESG compliance functions. Prospectra was built from the first day with a financial investor as the primary client. Every GRI score, every analytical framing, every report structure is designed to answer the portfolio manager's question: *What does this mean for my position?* This is not a feature — it is a founding architecture choice that permeates everything we produce.

**2. Quantitative + narrative integration.** Eurasia Group does narrative without quantitative backbone. Verisk Maplecroft does quantitative without investment-grade narrative. Prospectra does both, by design. The GRI score is the anchor; the weekly briefing explains what drove the score; the deep dives build the framework for understanding why the score matters to a specific asset class. No competitor does this integration for financial buyers.

**3. Methodological transparency as a feature, not a liability.** Established players protect their methodology because it is proprietary intellectual property. Prospectra is building a competitive moat through *methodological openness* — publishing the full framework, the revision log, the calibration history. This is counterintuitive but structurally advantageous: sophisticated institutional buyers who are building their own risk models want to understand what they are integrating. A transparent methodology is a compliance-friendly, auditable input. An opaque one is a black box.

**4. Founder-as-analyst accessibility.** At this stage, Bolo is the analyst. This is a disadvantage (scale limits, single-person dependency) but also an advantage that disappears as the company grows: a founder-analyst relationship is qualitatively different from a subscription relationship. The CIO who calls Bolo gets the person who built the framework. That direct access compounds into institutional trust faster than any polished product can.

**5. The Databricks infrastructure moat (Year 2).** None of the incumbent players has built their analytical infrastructure on a modern data lakehouse architecture with real-time GDELT ingestion, structured GRI pipelines, and API-accessible outputs. As Prospectra's Databricks build matures, the ability to produce genuinely data-driven, machine-readable geopolitical risk outputs will be a structural technical advantage. This moat is not yet built — but it is being built, and that matters in the Year 2 positioning conversation.

## IV. Concrete Example — How Oxford Analytica Lost a Client Prospectra Could Win

Consider this representative institutional scenario (composite of real market dynamics observed from 2022–2025):

A $6B EM-focused asset manager had used Oxford Analytica for 11 years. Their macro analyst read the daily brief consistently and used it for background context. When the firm started building a quantitative risk overlay for their country allocation model in 2023, the analyst approached Oxford Analytica for a structured data feed. Oxford Analytica's response: the Daily Brief is available in text format; structured data feeds require a custom enterprise agreement at significant additional cost, with long implementation timelines.

The analyst instead built their own country risk composite by combining Verisk Maplecroft scores with GDELT sentiment data. The Oxford Analytica subscription shifted from a daily workflow tool to a background reference. Within 18 months, the subscription was not renewed.

What won the renewal fight was not another narrative research service. It was a quantitative, structured, investment-oriented risk data product that could be integrated into their allocation model without a large IT project.

This is exactly the gap Prospectra fills — and it is a gap that is widening as institutional risk teams get more quantitatively sophisticated.

## VI. Investment Implications

### The Information Services Sector as a Geopolitical Beneficiary

The market for geopolitical intelligence is not shrinking — it is structurally expanding. The drivers are geopolitical (multipolar transition creating more risk events requiring active monitoring) and regulatory (FDI screening laws, sanctions compliance obligations, ESG mandates requiring geopolitical due diligence).

**The investment framework:** In the information services sector, the most durable businesses are those that enter regulated or fiduciary decision processes. Moody's, S&P, MSCI, FactSet, and Verisk are all worth studying not for their products but for their business model architecture: they are embedded in decisions that are required by law or professional fiduciary obligation. This creates an entirely different renewal dynamic than discretionary research subscriptions.

**The geopolitical alpha angle:** The geopolitical intelligence firms most likely to see structurally increasing demand over the next decade are those positioned at the intersection of geopolitics and compliance. As governments expand FDI screening, sanctions regimes, and critical infrastructure protection laws, the legal requirement to perform geopolitical risk assessment before committing capital is growing. Companies at this intersection — think MSCI's ESG + country risk products, or the next generation of sanctions compliance data — are not just useful; they are legally mandated inputs.

**Directional view (6–18 month horizon):**
- Long: information services companies with exposure to ESG + country risk scoring (MSCI, Verisk) as FDI screening expands
- Long: compliance-adjacent data vendors (LexisNexis, Refinitiv, MSCI) as geopolitical compliance requirements scale
- Monitor: pure-play narrative research subscriptions (less defensible moats as quantitative competitors scale)

### The Competitor Set as Opportunity Map

The incumbents' structural weaknesses are not just competitive positioning data — they are investment hypotheses about where capital will flow in the geopolitical intelligence market over the next five years.

Eurasia Group's Bremmer-dependency creates an acquisition or succession risk (declining quality and potential founder exit). Oxford Analytica's format problem creates a market gap for quantitative geopolitical data products. Verisk Maplecroft's ESG-compliance focus creates space for investment-focused competitors. Control Risks' security-firm framing leaves financial buyers underserved.

The pattern: **the incumbent providers were all built for a pre-2020 risk environment.** In that environment, geopolitical risk was episodic, secondary to financial modeling, and primarily relevant for corporate operational decisions. In the post-2020 environment — where geopolitics is constant, macro-critical, and directly portfolio-relevant — the institutional research market is being restructured, and new entrants with the right product architecture are well-positioned to capture the marginal institutional buyer's research budget.

## IX. Reflection Questions

1. **The transparency paradox:** Prospectra's methodological transparency is identified as a competitive advantage — specifically, it makes the GRI auditable for institutional risk model integration. But consider the counterargument: a transparent methodology can be replicated by a better-resourced competitor who reads it and builds a superior version. How do you structure the methodology transparency to capture the credibility benefit without eliminating the intellectual property moat? What is transparent, what is proprietary, and where is the line?

2. **The Verisk Maplecroft threat:** Of the four incumbents, Verisk Maplecroft is structurally the most similar to Prospectra's product roadmap. They produce quantitative scores, they serve financial institutions, and they have a Verisk parent with resources to fill the investment-narrative gap Prospectra identifies as its differentiation. How quickly could Verisk Maplecroft close the gap — and what would it look like if they did? What is the pre-emptive strategic move Prospectra should make before that happens?

3. **The market positioning dilemma:** Prospectra's positioning statement identifies the mid-market financial institution as the primary target. But the mid-market is also the most price-sensitive. The clients willing to pay $50K+ for geopolitical intelligence are the large institutions already using incumbents. What is the realistic acquisition path — do you start at the bottom of the market and move up, start with a price-competitive offering that erodes margins, or find a niche within the large-institution market where incumbents are structurally failing?

## XI. CEO Portfolio Note

The geopolitical intelligence market is in structural transition. The incumbents were built for a world where geopolitical risk was episodic and corporate-operational. The new world — where geopolitical events are continuous, directly portfolio-relevant, and increasingly legally mandated inputs to investment decisions — requires a different product architecture.

Prospectra is entering this market at exactly the right inflection point. The risk is not that the market is too small. The risk is that we identify the positioning precisely but execute it too slowly — allowing a better-resourced incumbent to fill the gap we have identified before we have established the track record and client relationships that make us defensible.

**The CEO's verdict:** Prospectra's competitive position is sound but fragile at this stage. It depends on three things going right simultaneously: (1) the GRI achieving genuine analytical credibility through track record accumulation, (2) the Databricks infrastructure maturing enough to be a real technical differentiator before incumbents catch up, and (3) the first 3–5 institutional subscribers becoming genuine advocates who make competitive displacement difficult. All three are achievable on the current timeline. None is guaranteed.

Speed matters more than polish right now.
