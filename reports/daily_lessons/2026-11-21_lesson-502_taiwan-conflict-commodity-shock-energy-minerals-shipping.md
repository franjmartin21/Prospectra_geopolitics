## 1. The Scenario Frame: Why Commodities, Not Just Chips

The standard Taiwan narrative centers on semiconductors — TSMC produces ~90% of the world's most advanced chips, and a disruption would be catastrophic for tech supply chains. That story is well-covered. What is *less* covered is the broader commodity architecture that runs through the same geography.

The Taiwan Strait sits at the intersection of three critical flows:

**A. Energy flows to Northeast Asia**
- Japan imports ~92% of its oil and ~97% of its gas
- South Korea imports ~100% of its oil
- Taiwan imports ~100% of its energy
- The majority of this energy transits via sea lanes through or adjacent to the South China Sea and Western Pacific

A blockade or mining of the strait doesn't just stop chips — it threatens to strand the energy supply of three major economies simultaneously.

**B. Critical mineral supply chains**
- China controls 60–70% of rare earth element (REE) refining capacity
- DRC cobalt → Chinese refining → battery supply chains
- Chinese graphite (anode material) → ~90% of global supply
- In a conflict, China does not need to fire a missile at a mine. It simply stops exporting.

**C. Chokepoint amplification**
The Strait of Malacca, Lombok Strait, and Sunda Strait become contested routing alternatives in any Taiwan scenario. Combined, these chokepoints carry roughly 40% of global seaborne trade. Shipping insurers price conflict risk in days; the broader commodity market takes weeks. That lag is signal.

## 2. The Oil Shock Mechanism

A Taiwan conflict does not need to disrupt Middle East production to create an oil shock. The shock mechanism is **demand + route uncertainty**, not supply destruction.

**Step 1 — Insurance withdrawal.** War risk insurers (Lloyd's market, P&I clubs) pull coverage from Taiwan Strait transit routes within hours of serious hostilities. This happened in the Ukraine war for Black Sea routes in days.

**Step 2 — Tanker diversion.** Without insurance, tankers divert. The alternate route (around Australia, through the Indonesian straits) adds 10–14 days per voyage for Japan/Korea-bound cargoes.

**Step 3 — Strategic reserve drawdown.** Japan and South Korea hold 150+ days of strategic petroleum reserves (SPR). The IEA coordinates drawdowns. This caps the initial spike but doesn't eliminate it.

**Step 4 — Demand-side panic.** Asian industrial buyers scramble for spot cargoes. Spot LNG prices — already volatile — become catastrophically illiquid. In the February 2022 Ukraine invasion, TTF gas spiked 60% in 48 hours. A Taiwan blockade is a larger event in a tighter market.

**Investment implication — oil:** The directional call is *not simply "oil goes up."* It's more precise:
- **Brent-WTI spread widens dramatically** (US domestic supply less affected; Asian supply premium explodes)
- **Asian LNG spot prices spike** disproportionately to European or US natural gas
- **Middle East producers benefit** from rerouted demand and higher prices (Saudi Aramco, ADNOC-listed entities)
- **Tanker stocks spike** on day-rate explosion — but are volatile and mean-revert; the pure tanker play is a 2–8 week trade, not a 12-month thesis

## 3. The Critical Minerals Shock: The Invisible War

Here is the asymmetry that most analysts miss: **China can deliver a severe economic shock without firing a single shot, using export controls on critical minerals.**

The precedent is established. In 2010, China imposed a de facto rare earth embargo on Japan following a fishing trawler dispute near the Senkaku Islands. REE prices spiked 400–1,000% within 12 months. Japan capitulated. China learned the lesson.

In a Taiwan scenario, China's first economic move is almost certainly **export control escalation on:**

| Mineral | Chinese Market Share | Key End Use | Current Stockpile Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gallium | ~80% global production | Semiconductors, defense electronics | Low (US strategic reserve minimal) |
| Germanium | ~60% global production | Fiber optics, solar panels, infrared | Low |
| Graphite | ~90% anode material | EV batteries, lubricants | Moderate (Japanese stockpile) |
| Rare earths (NdPrDy) | ~60% mining, ~87% refining | Permanent magnets, defense motors | Very low (12–18 months for defense) |
| Cobalt | ~70% refining | Battery cathodes | Moderate |
| Tungsten | ~83% global production | Armor-piercing munitions, cutting tools | Very low |

The investment signal here is in **the upstream producers and refiners outside China** who become the alternative supply source. In a conflict, spot prices for these materials in non-Chinese markets detach entirely from Chinese domestic prices.

**Companies that benefit from Chinese export control escalation:**
- Australian rare earth miners (Lynas Rare Earths — the only significant non-Chinese REE refiner at scale)
- MP Materials (US rare earth mining/processing)
- Energy Fuels (US uranium + rare earth co-production)
- Electra Battery Materials (Canadian cobalt refining)
- Nouveau Monde Graphite (Quebec graphite processing)

None of these companies need to produce at higher volumes for the investment thesis to work. The market simply re-prices them as *the only viable alternative at any price*.

## 4. The Shipping and Insurance Architecture

Shipping is the most underappreciated transmission mechanism in a geopolitical crisis. Here is why:

**The Baltic Dry Index (BDI) and tanker day-rates respond within days.** The equity markets for shipping companies follow with a lag of 1–3 weeks. This lag has been exploited successfully around every major maritime disruption of the past 20 years (Gulf of Aden piracy, Black Sea closure 2022, Red Sea Houthi attacks 2024).

**The rerouting math for a Taiwan conflict:**
- South China Sea/Strait rerouting adds ~8,000–12,000 nautical miles for affected routes
- At 14 knots, this is 24–36 additional transit days per voyage round trip
- Effective fleet capacity drops 15–25% as vessels spend more time in transit
- Same ships, same cargo, but fewer deliveries per year — rates spike

**Insurance cascade:**
- War Risk Premiums (WRP) in the Lloyd's market can go from 0.05% of hull value to 3–5% within days
- Many charterers' contracts have *war clauses* that allow carriers to deviate or refuse, shifting cost to cargo owners
- Commodity buyers scramble to pre-purchase forward contracts — another price amplification mechanism

**Shipping equities as a tactical play:**
- Crude tankers (Frontline, DHT Holdings, Nordic American Tankers)
- LNG tankers (Flex LNG, Golar LNG)
- Dry bulk (Star Bulk, Diana Shipping)
These are *not* core long-horizon positions. They are tactical 30–90 day instruments in a crisis. Sizing must reflect mean-reversion risk — the same names that spike in a crisis can retrace 40–60% when routes reopen or diplomatic resolution occurs.

## 5. The Agriculture Dimension: Taiwan's Hidden Food Vulnerability

Taiwan is 65% food-import dependent. A blockade does not need to target food ships — but the effect is the same if commercial shipping diverts. This creates a **secondary humanitarian pressure** that accelerates the geopolitical timeline.

More broadly, a Taiwan Strait conflict would almost certainly cause China to restrict certain agricultural exports as leverage — or face domestic food security pressure from supply chain disruption on agricultural inputs.

**The agricultural investment angle:**
- Brazil soybean exporters benefit from any China-US agricultural decoupling acceleration
- Fertilizer producers (Nutrien, Mosaic) benefit from supply chain stress
- Global grain shippers benefit from rerouting demand
- Australian agricultural exports re-price upward as Asian buyers diversify away from China-adjacent supply

## 6. The Gold and Safe-Haven Dynamics

Gold's behavior in a Taiwan conflict would be unusual relative to historical crisis patterns:

**Standard crisis pattern:** Gold spikes 5–15%, retreats as crisis resolves.

**Taiwan complication:** Unlike most geopolitical crises, a Taiwan conflict directly involves the world's two largest holders of US Treasuries and foreign reserves. China holds ~$3.1 trillion in foreign reserves (as of late 2026, partially shifted from USTs to gold over the past 3 years). The US would likely freeze Chinese dollar assets, as it did Russian assets in 2022.

This creates a **dollar weaponization feedback loop:**
1. US freezes Chinese dollar assets
2. All remaining dollar-holding states accelerate diversification
3. Gold, BTC, and non-Western reserve assets re-price upward on structural demand
4. The move is not a fear spike — it is a structural repricing of reserve architecture

The gold investment thesis in a Taiwan scenario is therefore **not tactical** (don't try to time the spike). It is **structural**: a Taiwan conflict accelerates the monetary regime transition that is already underway, and gold's repricing is part of that transition. Central banks in the Global South accelerate buying. The floor moves up.

## 7. What the Market Misprices (and Why)

Three systematic mispricings in a Taiwan crisis scenario:

**Mispricing 1 — The tech = Taiwan trade**
Most investors will immediately sell Taiwan Semiconductor, Korean memory makers, and US semiconductor equipment stocks. This is the right first move. The mispricing is *how much further they fall* than the underlying economic damage warrants — and the subsequent overshoot recovery opportunity 6–12 months later when supply chain rerouting and domestic production investments are announced.

**Mispricing 2 — Commodity timing**
The commodity shock is *not priced in advance*. The market treats the probability of conflict as low until it isn't. This means commodity producers that benefit from Chinese export controls — Australian rare earths, non-Chinese graphite, Western cobalt — are chronically underpriced relative to their option value in a conflict scenario. They should be held as *permanent portfolio insurance* at a reasonable allocation (1–3% of portfolio), not as a crisis trade.

**Mispricing 3 — Japan and Korea as victims vs. beneficiaries**
The reflexive trade is to sell Japan and Korea (closest geographic proximity to conflict, most energy-dependent). The nuanced trade is more complex: Japanese defense contractors, Korean defense exporters, and both countries' energy infrastructure companies benefit enormously from the structural rearmament and energy security spending that follows even a *threat* of conflict. TSMC's new fab in Japan suddenly becomes the most strategically important industrial facility in the world.

## Databricks Angle

**Pipeline: Taiwan Strait Shipping Risk Monitor**

Build a pipeline that tracks:
1. **AIS (Automatic Identification System) vessel tracking data** — monitor commercial shipping volumes transiting the Taiwan Strait. Free AIS data is available via MarineTraffic API or AIS Hub. A drop in transit volume is a leading indicator before any formal political announcement.
2. **War risk premium feed** — scrape or API-pull Lloyd's maritime insurance pricing. Unusual premium spikes on specific routes are early warning signals.
3. **Rare earth spot price tracker** — USGS and Metal Bulletin publish non-Chinese rare earth spot prices. Build a monitor that flags anomalies against the Chinese domestic price benchmark.
4. **Tanker day-rate tracker** — Baltic Exchange publishes VLCC and LNG day-rates. Build a Z-score monitor that alerts when rates move beyond 2 standard deviations of their 12-month baseline.

**Features to engineer for the Signal Generator:**
- Taiwan Strait transit volume (7-day moving average vs. 90-day baseline)
- War risk premium spread (Taiwan Strait vs. comparable routes)
- REE price divergence (non-Chinese spot vs. Chinese domestic)
- Tanker day-rate Z-score
- Composite "Strait Tension Index" — a weighted combination of the above

This index would have predictive power for commodity volatility beyond just a Taiwan scenario — it captures any generalized Asia-Pacific maritime risk signal.
