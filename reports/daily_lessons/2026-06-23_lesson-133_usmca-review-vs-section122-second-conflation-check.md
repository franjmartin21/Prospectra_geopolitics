## Section 1: What Actually Happens July 1 — The USMCA Joint Review

USMCA Article 34.7 commits all three parties (US, Mexico, Canada) to a joint review on the agreement's sixth anniversary — July 1, 2026. This is a structural, pre-written mechanism, not a crisis deadline: the agreement itself has a 16-year term, and the six-year mark is when the parties must affirmatively decide whether to extend it for the full remaining term or trigger a fallback.

The fallback, if no extension is agreed: a 10-year countdown of annual reviews begins, with the agreement's term ending July 1, 2036 absent further action. This is a meaningfully different structure from a true "cliff" — non-renewal at the six-year mark does not terminate USMCA on July 1; it converts a 16-year guarantee into a rolling, annually-revisited one.

USTR opened a public comment period in September 2025 and held hearings in December 2025. A first US-Mexico bilateral round concluded in late May 2026, covering automotive rules of origin, steel and aluminum trade, and economic security. USTR Jamieson Greer has already said publicly that the three parties "probably will not resolve all the issues by July 1" — meaning the realistic base case for July 1 itself is not a finished renegotiation, but the start of a multi-month (or multi-year) process, with the 10-year-countdown fallback the more probable near-term outcome.

## Section 2: What Actually Expires July 24 — and the Detail Lesson 128 Already Settled

Lesson 128 already built the full Section 122 record: a 150-day statutory surcharge (capped 15%) imposed February 20, 2026, running out July 24, 2026 regardless of the pending Federal Circuit appeal. The detail that matters for today's question is buried in Lesson 128's own Databricks table (Section 122 scope row): the surcharge applies to **"All countries (minus §232-covered goods, USMCA, Annex II)."**

That parenthetical is doing the work this lesson needs. **USMCA-originating goods — products that already qualify under USMCA's existing rules of origin — are carved out of the Section 122 surcharge.** They were never inside the mechanism that expires July 24. Section 122 was built, like its 1971 ancestor, as a blunt global instrument; USMCA-compliant trade was explicitly excluded from it from the start, the same way Section 122 also excluded the EU's bilateral framework from double-counting once that framework was separately ratified.

## Section 3: Running the Test

Lesson 128's question, applied here: is the USMCA review's outcome actually contingent on, or materially affected by, Section 122's July 24 sunset?

**No — for the same structural reason the EU case failed the test.** USMCA goods are already outside Section 122's scope. Section 122 expiring, surviving on appeal, or being replaced by a successor mechanism (the Section 301 investigations Lesson 128 flagged as the most probable next vehicle) changes nothing about the tariff treatment of USMCA-qualifying trade, because that trade was never inside Section 122 to begin with. The two dates are calendar-adjacent — five weeks apart — and both involve the word "tariff," but they run on independent tracks: one is a structural treaty-renewal mechanism between three named governments with its own 16-year/10-year clock; the other is a temporary balance-of-payments authority with a 150-day statutory clock that explicitly carves out the first mechanism's beneficiaries.

**This is the same error shape as Lesson 128, confirmed on a second case.** Two mechanisms sharing a five-week window does not make them one event. The honest framing of Lesson 132's flagged topic was itself slightly miscalibrated — "the two-deadline interaction" presupposes an interaction that the evidence does not support. The corrected framing: two independent trade-policy deadlines are landing in the same five-week window, and conflating them risks the same mistake the June 16 briefing made with the EU — borrowing one mechanism's urgency to narrate the other's risk.

**Where the two tracks could still touch, and the one place to actually watch:** the US-Mexico bilateral talks reported in late May covered automotive rules of origin specifically. If the joint review tightens USMCA's own rules of origin (raising the regional value content threshold, as happened in the original 2020 USMCA-vs-NAFTA transition), that would change which goods qualify for the USMCA carve-out itself — including, mechanically, the carve-out from Section 122 or whatever succeeds it. That is a real, narrow channel of interaction. It is not "USMCA review happens, therefore Section 122 outcome changes," nor the reverse. It is "USMCA review could redefine the boundary of an exemption that a different, unrelated mechanism happens to reference." Worth tracking specifically — not as a tariff-cliff collision, but as a rules-of-origin redefinition with second-order tariff-scope effects.

## Databricks Angle

Lesson 128 proposed `prospectra.portfolio.trade_mechanism_log`, one row per active legal tariff mechanism. Today's case confirms why the `scope` column in that table needs to be a structured exclusion list, not a free-text note: Section 122's row already needed "(minus §232-covered goods, USMCA, Annex II)" to be queryable, because the question this lesson just asked — "is mechanism X's outcome relevant to goods carved out under mechanism Y" — is exactly the kind of join a flat per-country tariff-rate table cannot answer, but a mechanism-and-scope table can, with a simple anti-join between `trade_mechanism_log.scope_exclusions` and any new `usmca_rules_of_origin_log` row tracking the rules-of-origin renegotiation specifically. **Concrete build recommendation:** add `usmca_joint_review_log` as a sibling table to `trade_mechanism_log`, tracking `review_status`, `auto_rules_of_origin_threshold`, and `fallback_clock_active` (boolean, true once the 10-year countdown starts) — and explicitly do **not** add a foreign key from this table to `trade_mechanism_log`'s Section 122 row, since today's analysis found no real dependency to model.
