## Opening Frame

> **Framing question:** China produces approximately 30% of global manufacturing output but accounts for only 13% of global consumption. That 17-percentage-point gap has to go somewhere. Where it goes — and what happens to every economy it lands in — is the central economic geopolitical question of 2026.

Lesson 178 explained the *internal* logic of China's Three D's — Demographics, Debt, Deflation. Today's lesson is about the *external* consequence: when domestic demand cannot absorb China's industrial output, the surplus floods global markets. Countries that cannot absorb it — or will not — impose tariffs. And those tariffs are now reshaping global supply chains, fracturing trade relationships, and creating the most significant inflationary pivot point in a decade.

This is not just a trade story. It is a geopolitical story with direct portfolio consequences.

## Core Concept: How Overcapacity Becomes a Geopolitical Weapon

### The Arithmetic of the Problem

China's manufacturing model was designed around one assumption: growth would remain high, domestic demand would keep pace with supply, and exports would supplement rather than substitute for internal consumption. That model broke in 2021–2022 when the property crash destroyed household wealth and consumer confidence.

The result:

- **China accounts for ~30% of global manufacturing output** but only **~13% of global consumption** — an imbalance of approximately $2–3 trillion per year that must be absorbed by the rest of the world
- **Trade surplus: $1.19 trillion in 2025** — the largest national trade surplus in the history of the global economy, up 20% from 2024
- **June 2026 monthly surplus: $125.62 billion** — the second-largest single month on record, driven by booming AI data center hardware exports and persistent weak domestic demand
- **PPI: 41 consecutive months of deflation** as of mid-2026 — factory prices have not risen in over three years

**The mechanism:** Chinese factories, many state-directed, cannot reduce output — doing so would trigger unemployment, social instability, and political problems the Communist Party cannot accept. So they sell at or below cost, undercutting competitors in every market they enter. This is not dumping in the traditional legal sense (though some of it qualifies). It is a structural feature of an economy where production cannot slow down even when demand cannot keep up.

### The Involution Problem

China has a word for what happens internally when too many producers compete for too little demand: **involution (nèijuǎn)**. It describes a self-destructive race to the bottom — companies cutting prices, wages, and quality to survive, creating conditions where no one is profitable.

In EVs: BYD cut prices 12 times in 2024. In solar: Chinese panel prices fell 50% in a single year. In steel: Chinese mills produced at loss for extended periods rather than cut capacity.

**Beijing's 2026 response:** The government is now explicitly cracking down on involution — capping price wars in key sectors (EVs, battery, solar), directing industry consolidation, and restricting the most aggressive below-cost selling practices. The goal is to raise Chinese PPI back above zero by Q3 2026. This is an important signal: **if Chinese factory prices begin rising while US supply chains are still being re-routed, the world could flip from deflationary fear to cost-push inflation faster than markets expect.**

## The Trade War Response: EU and US Architecture

### United States: The Tariff Wall

The US-China trade confrontation, which began formally in 2018, has escalated to near-comprehensive tariffs. As of mid-2026:

- **Average effective US tariff on Chinese goods: ~65%** (across IEEPA emergency tariffs, Section 301 tariffs, and specific sector actions)
- Chinese goods as % of US imports has fallen sharply — but supply chains take 2–3 years to re-route; short-term, higher tariffs mean higher prices for American consumers
- Strategic sectors (semiconductors, EVs, batteries, solar) face specific tariffs in the 50–100%+ range designed to make Chinese imports uneconomical regardless of subsidy level

**The diversion problem:** Tariffs on direct Chinese imports do not solve the underlying overcapacity problem. Chinese manufacturers route through Vietnam, Mexico, Thailand, and Indonesia — a phenomenon US trade officials call "tariff evasion through transshipment." USMCA review in 2026 explicitly targets Chinese-content rules of origin in Mexican manufacturing.

### European Union: Late But Accelerating

The EU moved later than the US but is now catching up rapidly:

**EVs:** Tariffs introduced in 2024 (BYD: 17%, Geely: 18.8%, SAIC: 35%+, Tesla China-made: 9%) failed to significantly slow Chinese EV penetration of the EU market. Chinese brands gained further market share despite tariffs because their absolute cost advantage absorbed the tariff margin. The EU is considering further measures.

**Steel (July 2026):** EU cut tariff-free steel quotas by **47%** — from ~33 million tonnes to 18.3 million — and **doubled out-of-quota tariffs from 25% to 50%** through 2031. This is a structural, multi-year measure, not a tactical response.

**E-commerce:** New regulations targeting Chinese e-commerce platforms (Temu, Shein, AliExpress) over customs valuation, product safety, and de minimis exemption abuse.

**Scale of the problem:** EU–China trade deficit widened to ~€360 billion ($410 billion) in 2025 — roughly €1 billion per day — and is still rising in 2026. Global steel overcapacity alone could reach **721 million tonnes by 2027**, nearly five times total EU steel consumption.

### The Emerging Market Battleground

The most under-covered dimension: China's overcapacity is not just hitting the US and EU. It is devastating manufacturing sectors in **emerging markets** that compete with Chinese exports in global and third-country markets:

- **Vietnam, Bangladesh, Indonesia:** Textile and electronics manufacturers facing Chinese competition that is cheaper even after freight costs
- **India:** Steel producers being undercut by Chinese steel that cannot sell in the US or EU; India has imposed anti-dumping duties on 30+ Chinese product categories
- **Brazil, Mexico:** Domestic manufacturers facing Chinese flooding of their home markets through e-commerce platforms
- **Africa:** Chinese goods disrupting nascent manufacturing sectors that countries like Ethiopia and Kenya were trying to build for export

This is geopolitically significant: the Global South, which China has been cultivating through Belt and Road and diplomatic engagement, is now experiencing Chinese overcapacity as an economic threat, not a development partnership.

## Historical Case Study: Japan's Steel Dumping, 1970s–1980s

The US-Japan trade conflicts of the 1970s–80s offer a structural template.

Japan ran massive trade surpluses in automobiles, steel, and electronics, driven by an export-oriented production model that outcompeted American manufacturers. The US responded with:
- Voluntary Export Restraints (VERs) negotiated with Japan in autos (1981)
- Trigger pricing mechanisms for steel
- Eventually the Plaza Accord (1985) — forcing yen appreciation to correct the imbalance at the currency level

**What resolved the Japan-US trade imbalance:**
1. Currency revaluation (Plaza Accord forced yen up 50%)
2. Japanese domestic demand eventually growing
3. Japanese companies moving production offshore to the US
4. Japanese economic slowdown reducing surplus

**The China difference:** China will not accept a Plaza Accord equivalent. RMB appreciation is a political line Beijing will not cross at the speed required to close a $1.2 trillion surplus. Chinese companies are moving offshore, but slowly — and often to circumvent tariffs rather than genuinely relocate production. There is no equivalent political mechanism to the US-Japan relationship that can force a rapid correction.

**Conclusion:** The China trade imbalance will take longer to resolve than Japan's did, the measures required will be more comprehensive, and the spillover into geopolitics will be deeper.

## Investment Implications

### 1. The Inflation Pivot Risk: The Most Mispriced Scenario

**The setup:** Markets in mid-2026 are still broadly priced for "China exports deflation, inflation stays contained." But:
- Beijing is explicitly cracking down on involution, targeting positive PPI by Q3 2026
- EU and US tariffs are reducing the supply of cheap Chinese goods in Western markets
- Supply chains are re-routing — but re-routing is expensive

If Chinese factory prices begin rising (PPI turns positive) while Western tariff walls are still being built and re-routing is still incomplete, the world could experience a simultaneous loss of the China disinflationary effect AND higher costs from supply chain disruption.

**Implication:** Inflation expectations — especially 2–5 year breakevens — may be underpriced. This is the "reflation surprise" scenario that central banks are not fully prepared for. **Directional view:** Inflation-linked bonds (TIPS, UK linkers) represent undervalued insurance against this scenario.

### 2. EU and EM Industrial Metals: The New Beneficiaries

EU steel tariff escalation creates a protected space for European and non-Chinese steel production. With Chinese steel barred or tariffed, European steel mills (ArcelorMittal, Thyssenkrupp) and US mills (Nucor, Steel Dynamics) benefit structurally.

**But watch for:** EU carbon border adjustment mechanism (CBAM) interacting with tariffs — some steel routes may remain price-competitive even with higher tariffs.

**Implication:** Selectively long EU and US steel producers as tariff beneficiaries. Not a commodities bet — a trade policy beneficiary bet. Different risk profile.

### 3. The Mexico Nuance: Threat and Opportunity Simultaneously

Mexico is a nearshoring beneficiary of US-China decoupling (covered in Lesson 59). But Mexico is also a Chinese overcapacity conduit: Chinese EV and battery manufacturers have been building Mexican factories to access US USMCA preferences.

**The USMCA 2026 review** is tightening rules of origin, specifically to prevent Chinese-content products from claiming NAFTA/USMCA preferential access. This threatens Chinese-owned Mexican manufacturing operations but protects genuinely Mexican and North American supply chains.

**Implication:** Mexico's nearshoring story remains structurally intact but is more nuanced than simple "friend-shoring beneficiary." Companies with genuine supply chain transfer (not just assembly of Chinese components) are the real beneficiaries.

### 4. Chinese EV and Solar: The Trapped Exporter

Chinese EV and solar companies face a difficult position: domestic demand is insufficient to absorb capacity, US market is effectively closed, EU market is increasingly closed, and Global South markets are lower-margin.

**BYD's response:** Aggressive move into Latin America, Southeast Asia, and Australia — markets not yet covered by significant tariff barriers. This creates a new competitive dynamic in those markets for incumbent auto manufacturers (Toyota in Southeast Asia, Stellantis in Latin America).

**Implication:** Japanese automakers (Toyota, Honda) face Chinese EV competition in their core Southeast Asian and Latin American markets in a way that is not yet priced by equity markets. This is a structural headwind for Japanese auto equities.

### 5. The De-Risking Premium for Domestic Manufacturing

Every tariff wall creates an incentive for domestic manufacturing investment. In the US: IRA, CHIPS Act, and now tariff protection creating a domestic industrial renaissance. In Europe: EU industrial policy, defense spending, energy transition infrastructure.

**The investment consequence:** Companies building domestic manufacturing capacity in protected sectors (semiconductors, batteries, steel, solar) are effectively receiving a government-guaranteed margin premium via trade protection. This is a different kind of infrastructure play — not just construction, but protected markets.

**Directional view:** Long domestic industrial plays in both the US and EU, particularly in sectors where tariff protection and industrial policy subsidies compound. Short companies with high Chinese content exposure in regulated markets.

## Signal vs. Noise

**Signal:** China's trade surplus of $1.2 trillion is structural, not cyclical. The deficit it creates in every trading partner is a political and economic crisis that will generate protectionist responses for years. The inflection point to watch is PPI turning positive — that is when the global inflationary dynamics shift.

**Noise:** Month-to-month trade surplus data fluctuations. Any single month of weaker Chinese exports is not structural; it is weather, shipping disruption, or holiday timing.

**CEO Portfolio Note:** The China overcapacity story is at an inflection. For 18 months, it has been a deflationary force and a bearish signal on commodity-intensive sectors. In the next 12–18 months, the tariff response plus Beijing's own anti-involution policy could flip it into an inflationary force. Portfolios positioned only for the deflationary outcome are exposed. Add TIPS exposure and EU/US industrial plays as hedges against the inflation pivot scenario.
