## Core Concept

### The Electromagnetic Spectrum as Contested Terrain

Every modern weapon system is electromagnetic-dependent. GPS signals navigate missiles and drones. Radar guides air defense. Datalinks carry targeting coordinates. Communications link commanders to units. Satellite uplinks transmit imagery in real time. Electronic warfare is the systematic effort to exploit, disrupt, degrade, or deny the adversary's use of the electromagnetic spectrum — while protecting your own.

There are three pillars:

**Electronic Attack (EA):** Active disruption of adversary systems. This includes:
- **Jamming:** Flooding a frequency band with noise to overwhelm a receiver (communications, radar, GPS)
- **Spoofing:** Sending false signals an adversary system believes are real (fake GPS coordinates, false radar returns)
- **Directed-energy EW:** High-powered microwave or laser systems that physically damage electronics
- **Anti-radiation missiles:** Weapons that home on radar emissions and destroy the transmitter

**Electronic Protection (EP):** Hardening your own systems against adversary EW. Frequency-hopping radios, spread-spectrum communications, low-probability-of-intercept radar, GPS anti-jam receivers, emissions control (EMCON) doctrine. Modern military platforms spend enormous engineering effort on EP precisely because EA is ubiquitous.

**Electronic Support / SIGINT (ES):** Passive collection of electromagnetic emissions. Intercept an adversary's radar pulse and you know their radar frequency, range, capability — and potentially their location. SIGINT is the intelligence foundation of modern warfighting: you cannot attack what you cannot locate, and location is electromagnetic.

### Why This Moment Is Different

EW is not new. Nations have jammed and spoofed each other for 80 years. What is different now is the convergence of three forces:

1. **Software-defined everything:** EW systems used to be hardware: a physical jammer tuned to specific frequencies. Now they are software-defined radio (SDR) systems that can be reprogrammed in the field. This creates an *algorithm race* — the side that updates its EW algorithms faster wins, independent of the original hardware investment.

2. **Proliferation below the state level:** EW once required nation-state investment. Today, commercial GPS jammers cost under $50. The GPS-spoofing of civilian aircraft across the Baltic and Black Sea (documented from 2022 onward) has been attributed to Russian military EW operating at scale — but the technology is increasingly accessible.

3. **AI-enabled cognitive EW:** Next-generation EW systems use machine learning to identify and characterize adversary emissions in milliseconds, automatically determine optimal countermeasures, and adapt faster than a human operator can respond. The US Air Force's Advanced Battle Management System (ABMS) and the Army's Multi-Function Electronic Warfare (MFEW) program both incorporate AI-driven spectrum management. This is the domain where AI meets kinetic warfare most concretely.

## Historical Grounding

### The Wizard War (1939–1945)

Winston Churchill called it "The Wizard War." German bombers navigated to British targets using intersecting radio beams (Knickebein, then X-Gerät). British scientist R.V. Jones — working from first principles and captured equipment — decoded these systems and bent the beams, causing German bombers to drop their payloads in fields. This was the first systematic EW campaign in history. The lesson still stands: EW is a cognitive competition, not just a technical one. The side that understands the adversary's system first wins the engagement before a bomb drops.

### Vietnam and the Wild Weasels

Soviet-supplied SA-2 SAMs decimated US aircraft in Vietnam. The US response was the "Wild Weasel" program — specialized aircraft that flew directly toward SAM radar emissions to trigger a lock, then destroyed the radar with anti-radiation missiles before it could fire. The Wild Weasels developed the operational doctrine still used today: Suppress Enemy Air Defenses (SEAD) through EW. That doctrine made the 1991 Gulf War possible.

### 1991 Gulf War: Spectrum Dominance as Decisive Advantage

Before the first ground forces crossed the line in 1991, the Iraqi air defense network was methodically dismantled through EW. US EA-6B Prowlers jammed Iraqi radar and communications. F-4G Wild Weasels killed radar sites with HARM missiles. SIGINT aircraft mapped the entire order of battle. When ground forces crossed, Iraqi commanders were blind, unable to communicate, unable to coordinate. The ground war lasted 100 hours. EW dominance made it a rout rather than a battle.

## Concrete Example: Ukraine as the EW Laboratory (2022–2026)

No conflict in the precision-weapon era has tested EW capabilities at greater scale or produced more doctrinal learning than Ukraine. By 2026, every military intelligence service on earth has updated its EW doctrine based on what happened in that theater.

**Russian EW at scale:** Russia deployed its most sophisticated EW systems — the Krasukha-4 (ground-based broadband jammer), Murmansk-BN (long-range HF suppression), and Borisoglebsk-2 (tactical communications jammer). These systems suppressed Ukrainian communications and degraded drone operations significantly in contested areas. Russia also conducted GPS jamming at unprecedented geographic scale — documented spoofing affected civilian aircraft across the Baltic, Black Sea, and Eastern Mediterranean, disrupting commercial aviation from Helsinki to Istanbul.

**Ukrainian adaptation — and the algorithm race:** Early in the war, Russian EW systems were effective against Ukrainian commercial drones (DJI Mavics used for reconnaissance). Ukraine's response was a masterclass in rapid adaptation: switching drone navigation to optical flow and terrain-following (reducing GPS dependence), deploying anti-radiation drones that home on Russian jamming sources and destroy them, and updating firmware at a pace Russian EW operators could not match. This is the defining insight: in software-defined EW, **update cadence is a military capability**.

**Starlink's EW resilience:** Starlink demonstrated far greater jam-resistance than its commercial origin suggested, using phased-array terminals that can steer narrow, directional beams and change frequencies dynamically. Russia worked throughout the conflict to develop Starlink-specific countermeasures. The result was a grinding EW adaptation cycle with neither side achieving permanent advantage — which is itself the lesson. Spectrum dominance is not achieved once. It must be sustained in real time.

**The investment signal:** Every defense procurement office in NATO updated its EW requirements after Ukraine. The result is a multi-year procurement acceleration that began in 2024 and is still running. The US EW market was $7.98 billion in 2026 and is projected to reach $13.92 billion by 2032 — a 9.7% CAGR — driven entirely by lessons from this laboratory.

## Investment Implications

### The Structural Thesis

EW investment is a structural growth story with two independent drivers:

**Driver 1 — Great-power competition:** The US, China, and Russia are all in an EW arms race. Each country's military platforms — aircraft, ships, ground vehicles, drones — require EW self-protection suites. The more contested the spectrum, the more every platform needs electronic hardening. This is a multiplier on all defense spending: every new weapons program creates derivative EW demand.

**Driver 2 — Software-defined upgradeability:** Unlike hardware platforms (a destroyer takes 10 years to build), EW systems can be upgraded via software updates. This creates a recurring revenue model for defense software companies — a rarity in a hardware-dominant industry. Companies with software-first EW architectures command a premium for this reason.

### Key Players

**L3Harris Technologies (LHX):** The largest US EW-focused defense company. Electronic warfare systems across all domains — airborne jamming (ALQ-218 wideband receiver), ground-based, shipborne. Significant exposure to EW upgrade programs for existing platforms.

**Northrop Grumman (NOC):** Operates the EA-18G Growler's jamming systems, runs major SIGINT programs, and is the prime contractor on several classified airborne EW platforms. The Electronic Systems sector is one of Northrop's fastest-growing divisions.

**RTX / Raytheon (RTX):** Prime contractor for the Next Generation Jammer (NGJ) — the replacement for the Cold War-era ALQ-99 on Navy EA-18G Growlers. The NGJ-MB (Mid-Band) contract is worth multiple billions over its lifecycle. Raytheon also produces the HARM anti-radiation missile (heavily used in Ukraine) and the High-Power Microwave Tactical Vehicle Demonstrator.

**CACI International (CACI):** Software-defined EW and spectrum management. Won a $250M Army task order in January 2026 for spectrum dominance capabilities. CACI is the purest expression of the software-defined EW investment thesis — it wins by writing better algorithms faster, not by pouring steel.

**Mercury Systems (MRCY):** Processing subsystems for EW — the AI/compute layer inside EW platforms. As EW becomes more AI-driven, Mercury's signal processing modules become more critical. High gross margins relative to prime defense contractors.

**Thales (France), Leonardo (Italy), Rheinmetall (Germany):** European EW investment has accelerated dramatically since 2022. These are the primary beneficiaries of European defense budget expansion in the EW domain. Thales in particular is positioning heavily in cognitive EW.

### Directional Views (6–18 month horizon)

**Overweight:** Software-defined EW companies (CACI, Mercury Systems). The algorithm-race dynamic rewards companies that can deliver software updates faster than the procurement cycle. Recurring revenue, high margins, structurally growing addressable market.

**Accumulate on weakness:** L3Harris, RTX. Both have underperformed broader defense indices in 2026 due to program delays. The underlying demand signal — driven by NDAA appropriations and NATO procurement — is intact. Weakness is an entry opportunity.

**Monitor:** European defense EW (Thales, Rheinmetall). European defense budgets are rising but procurement cycles are long. Thales's EW division is a 2027–2028 story; near-term revenue recognition will lag the headlines.

**Risk flag — the SIGINT-AI intersection:** Companies building AI-enabled SIGINT platforms (Palantir, Leidos) will benefit from EW budget growth indirectly through intelligence fusion systems. This is not pure EW but is adjacent and worth tracking.
