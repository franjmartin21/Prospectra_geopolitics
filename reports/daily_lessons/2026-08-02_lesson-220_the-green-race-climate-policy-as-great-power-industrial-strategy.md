# Lesson 220: The Green Race — Climate Policy as Great Power Industrial Strategy

**Date:** 2026-08-02
**Session Type:** Daily Lesson
**Lesson Number:** 220 / Extended Curriculum
**Arc:** The Green Race (Lesson 1 of 4 — Arc Open)
**Topic:** The energy transition is no longer principally an environmental project. It is a great power competition — fought with subsidies, tariffs, domestic content requirements, and critical mineral supply chains — in which industrial dominance over the technologies of the next energy system is treated as a national security objective. This lesson examines the architecture of that competition, its primary combatants, and what the collision between their strategies means for investors.

## Opening Framing Question

> *In August 2022, the United States passed the Inflation Reduction Act — a $369 billion commitment to subsidize American clean energy manufacturing over 10 years, the largest climate investment in U.S. history. It contained a provision called the "Foreign Entity of Concern" (FEOC) exclusion: any electric vehicle that uses battery components manufactured by a Chinese, Russian, North Korean, or Iranian company is ineligible for the $7,500 consumer tax credit that makes EVs affordable for middle-income buyers. The exclusion was not written for environmental reasons. It was written to force U.S. automakers to build battery supply chains that exclude China.*

> *Meanwhile, China was producing 57% of the world's solar panels, 77% of its lithium-ion batteries, and nearly 70% of the rare earth elements used in wind turbines and EV motors. BYD had overtaken Tesla as the world's largest EV seller. Chinese solar panels were 80% cheaper than American-made equivalents, making them structurally dominant in every market that didn't impose tariffs to exclude them.*

> **Here is the question that opens this arc:**

> **If the cheapest path to a global energy transition runs through Chinese manufacturing — and every major Western power is simultaneously subsidizing domestic alternatives specifically to exclude Chinese supply chains — what is the energy transition actually optimizing for? Is this a race to decarbonize the planet, or a race to dominate the industries that will power the next century? And for investors: what happens when the two goals are in direct conflict?**

## 1. The Historical Context — Why Industrial Policy Is Back

### 1.1 The Washington Consensus Interregnum (1989–2008)

For twenty years after the Cold War, the dominant economic doctrine — the "Washington Consensus" — held that industrial policy was a form of market distortion to be eliminated, not a legitimate tool of statecraft. The WTO's subsidy rules, IMF conditionality, and the discipline of global capital markets systematically pressured governments away from picking industrial winners.

This produced a specific outcome in clean energy: **the technologies were developed primarily by private capital in market economies, but manufactured at scale in China** — which never fully subscribed to Washington Consensus orthodoxy and continued deploying industrial policy aggressively throughout this period.

The result by 2020:
- Solar PV: invented in the U.S. (Bell Labs, 1954); commercialized by German and Japanese firms; manufactured at competitive scale almost entirely in China
- Wind turbines: European technology (Vestas, Siemens Gamesa) but with Chinese rare earth supply chains (NdFeB magnets)
- Lithium-ion batteries: Japanese and Korean chemistry; Chinese manufacturing scale (CATL, BYD)
- Electric vehicles: American (Tesla) and Chinese (BYD) leadership; European brands dependent on Chinese battery supply

The Washington Consensus period produced the technologies. China captured the manufacturing value chain.

### 1.2 The Inflection Point — COVID and the Supply Chain Reckoning

COVID-19 exposed the fragility of globally integrated supply chains in a visceral way. Semiconductor shortages shuttered auto plants. PPE shortages became a national security crisis. The political consensus in both the U.S. and EU shifted decisively toward "strategic autonomy" — the idea that critical industrial capabilities must be domestically anchored even at higher cost.

Clean energy manufacturing was immediately framed in this context. If lithium-ion batteries are the "oil of the 21st century," and China controls 77% of global battery manufacturing capacity, that is a strategic dependency analogous to European dependence on Russian gas — visible, comprehensible, and politically actionable.

The IRA (2022), the EU Green Deal Industrial Plan (2023), and the UK Net Zero Industrial Strategy (2024) were all responses to the same diagnosis: **the energy transition cannot be outsourced to a geopolitical rival.**

## 2. The Three Combatants — Architecture of Each Strategy

### 2.1 The United States: The IRA and Its Industrial Logic

The Inflation Reduction Act's $369bn commitment is structured almost entirely as **production and investment tax credits** — not grants, not subsidies paid upfront, but tax advantages that accrue as clean energy is manufactured and deployed. This design has three implications:

**Implication 1 — It is self-scaling with investment.** The Congressional Budget Office estimated $369bn over 10 years; independent analysts now project $800bn–$1.2 trillion as investment flows exceeded forecasts. The IRA "unlocks" private investment at 3-5x the public commitment.

**Implication 2 — It requires domestic manufacturing.** The full $7,500 EV tax credit (Section 30D) requires:
- 50%+ of battery components manufactured in North America
- 40%+ of critical minerals sourced from North America or FTA partners (rising to 80% by 2027)
- Final assembly in North America
- The FEOC exclusion (no Chinese battery supply chain participation)

This is industrial policy disguised as environmental policy. The domestic content requirements are not necessary to reduce carbon emissions — they are necessary to build an American battery industry.

**Implication 3 — It creates "friend-shoring" by treaty.** The "FTA partners" eligible for the critical minerals sourcing requirement include Canada, Mexico, Australia, Japan, South Korea, and the EU (under a targeted critical minerals agreement). This is a geopolitically constructed supply chain — explicitly designed to include democratic allies and exclude China.

**Key IRA sectors as of mid-2026:**
| Technology | IRA Credit Structure | Domestic Requirement |
|-----------|---------------------|---------------------|
| EV consumer tax credit (30D) | Up to $7,500/vehicle | North American assembly; FEOC exclusion |
| EV commercial fleet (45W) | Up to $7,500/vehicle | Less restrictive |
| Battery production (45X) | $35/kWh for cells | North American manufacturing |
| Solar module manufacturing (45X) | $0.07/watt | U.S. manufacturing |
| Wind turbine component (45X) | Variable | U.S. manufacturing |
| Clean hydrogen (45V) | $3/kg | Lifecycle GHG intensity test |
| Nuclear (45J) | $15/MWh operating credit | Existing plants |

### 2.2 China: The Platform Economy of Clean Energy

China's clean energy industrial dominance did not emerge from a single legislative act. It is the product of 20 years of continuous industrial policy — cheap land, subsidized financing from policy banks, managed competition among domestic champions, mandatory domestic sourcing requirements for state enterprises, and strategic patience.

**The scale of Chinese dominance by 2026:**
- **Solar PV modules:** 80-85% of global production capacity
- **Lithium-ion batteries:** CATL alone has ~37% of global market share; China total ~75%
- **Electric vehicles:** BYD's global sales (~3.7m vehicles in 2025) exceeded Tesla (~1.8m). Chinese brands (BYD, SAIC, NIO, Li Auto) collectively account for ~65% of global EV unit production
- **Rare earth processing:** China controls ~85% of global rare earth processing, even for ore mined elsewhere (Australian ore routinely shipped to China for processing)
- **Polysilicon (solar feedstock):** ~95% of global production

**The cost advantage is structural, not temporary.** Chinese solar module prices are $0.09-0.12/watt vs. $0.25-0.35/watt for non-Chinese production. This gap reflects: economies of scale; 20 years of manufacturing learning-by-doing; integrated supply chains (polysilicon → wafer → cell → module manufactured in adjacent facilities); policy bank financing at near-zero real interest rates.

**China's 2026 response to Western tariffs:** Rather than retreating, China accelerated:
- **Offshore manufacturing:** BYD, CATL, and others building plants in Hungary, Morocco, Mexico, Southeast Asia — jurisdictions inside or adjacent to Western tariff walls
- **Belt and Road energy:** Exporting clean energy technology to BRI recipients who are not subject to U.S./EU tariffs, capturing the Global South market
- **Vertical integration:** Acquiring mining assets in Africa (lithium, cobalt, nickel) to secure upstream supply chains beyond Western reach

### 2.3 The European Union: From Green Deal to Industrial Reality

The EU's green transition strategy has evolved in a distinctive way:
- **2019–2022 (Green Deal era):** European Green Deal was primarily regulatory — setting 2050 net-zero targets, 2030 intermediate targets, carbon pricing through the EU ETS. It assumed the market would find the cheapest technology (including Chinese).
- **2023 (Post-IRA panic):** The IRA's domestic content requirements threatened to pull European clean energy investment to the U.S. The EU responded with the **Green Deal Industrial Plan** — relaxing state aid rules so member states could subsidize clean energy manufacturing, fast-tracking permitting, and net-zero industry targets.
- **2024–2026 (Clean Industrial Deal):** Under pressure from deindustrialization fears and auto industry lobbying, the EU launched the **Clean Industrial Deal** — industrial policy in all but name, with explicit technology targets for European manufacturing in batteries, solar, heat pumps, and wind.

**The EU's fundamental tension:** The EU is not one country. Its industrial policy is the aggregate of 27 national policies, coordination failures, and competing interests (German auto industry vs. French nuclear vs. Polish coal). China imposed 45%+ tariffs on EU electric vehicles in 2024; the EU retaliated with tariffs on Chinese EVs — but the tariff levels reflected political negotiation among member states, not an optimal strategic response.

**The EU carbon border adjustment mechanism (CBAM):** From 2026, the EU charges importers for the carbon embedded in steel, cement, aluminum, fertilizers, and electricity. This is both a climate tool and a competitiveness tool — it prevents "carbon leakage" to less regulated producers and makes European producers more competitive against lower-carbon-priced imports.

## 3. The Critical Minerals Chokepoint — Where Geography Re-Emerges

The energy transition is, at its physical level, a materials transition. The hydrocarbon economy runs on oil and gas (largely fungible commodities traded in global markets). The clean energy economy runs on:
- **Lithium** (EV batteries): 55% of production from Chile and Australia; 75% of processing in China
- **Cobalt** (NMC batteries): 70% of mining from the DRC; 80% of processing in China
- **Nickel** (batteries, stainless steel): Indonesia emerging as dominant producer; significant Chinese processing
- **Rare earth elements** (EV motors, wind turbines): ~60% of mining in China; ~85% of processing in China
- **Copper** (everything electrical): Chile, Peru, DRC — more geographically diversified but processing increasingly Chinese
- **Silicon/polysilicon** (solar): ~95% Chinese production

The geopolitical implication: **the energy transition routes through China's processing infrastructure regardless of where raw materials are mined.** 

The U.S., EU, and allied responses:
- **The Minerals Security Partnership (MSP):** U.S.-led coalition of 14 countries committed to developing alternative critical mineral supply chains. Progress has been slow — processing capacity takes a decade to build.
- **Canada and Australia as alternative sources:** Both have significant lithium, rare earth, and nickel deposits. Both are receiving U.S. and EU investment to develop processing (not just mining) capacity.
- **Recycling:** Battery recycling at scale could eventually reduce virgin material dependence — but the recycling base doesn't exist at the scale needed until 2030+.

**The investor implication:** The minerals chokepoint is not going away quickly. Chinese processing dominance will persist for 5-10 years regardless of how aggressively the West invests in alternatives. This creates a structural tension in the "friend-shoring" strategy — you can pass domestic content requirements, but if 85% of rare earth processing happens in China, compliance is either impossible or extremely expensive.

## 4. The Collision Scenarios — What Happens When Strategies Conflict

### 4.1 The Tariff-Subsidy Spiral

Western tariffs on Chinese clean energy products (U.S.: 100%+ on Chinese EVs, 50% on solar cells by 2024; EU: 45%+ on Chinese EVs) have not stopped Chinese market penetration in third countries. They have:
- Raised domestic clean energy costs in the U.S. and EU (a clean energy transition tax on consumers)
- Accelerated Chinese investment in third-country production platforms
- Created a "tariff arbitrage" industry in which Chinese companies build factories in non-tariffed jurisdictions

The **Mexico EV plant question** crystallized this: BYD's planned Mexico plant (since 2023) would produce EVs for the U.S. market — qualifying for USMCA tariff exemptions and potentially IRA tax credits. The FEOC rule was tightened specifically to close this loophole. The result is a continuous legal/regulatory arms race between Chinese market entry strategies and Western exclusion mechanisms.

### 4.2 The Global South Bifurcation

The U.S./EU "friend-shoring" strategy does not include most of the developing world. Countries in Africa, Southeast Asia, and Latin America that are not part of the Minerals Security Partnership or U.S. FTA networks are essentially left to choose:
- **Western finance:** IFC, DFC, bilateral development banks — with governance and environmental conditions
- **Chinese finance:** BRI, Chinese policy banks — with procurement conditions (Chinese contractors, equipment)

In clean energy, this bifurcation is already visible. Chinese solar panels dominate in Africa and South Asia — they're simply cheaper, and Western restrictions don't apply. This is actually good for carbon emissions (cheaper solar = faster deployment) but is also a vector of Chinese industrial influence into markets Western firms are priced out of.

### 4.3 The Technology Convergence Risk

All three major powers are subsidizing overlapping technologies:
- **Batteries:** U.S. (IRA 45X), EU (IPCEI batteries), China (policy bank financing)
- **Hydrogen:** U.S. (IRA 45V), EU (REPowerEU), China (hydrogen strategy)
- **Solar:** U.S. (IRA 45X), EU (net-zero industry act solar targets), China (dominant existing base)

This creates a risk of **massive global overcapacity** in specific technologies — already visible in solar (Chinese module prices crashed 50% in 2023-24 due to overcapacity) and beginning to appear in batteries (CATL has been producing at 85% capacity vs. 95%+ previously).

For investors: overcapacity in the technology itself is bad for technology manufacturers (margin compression), but **good for deployers** of those technologies (solar developers, EV fleet operators, utility-scale storage projects).

## 5. Investment Implications

### 5.1 The Core Framework: Winners and Losers in a Subsidy War

The green race creates a clear investment structure:

**Winners in a subsidy-driven buildout:**
- **Deployers of subsidized technology** (not manufacturers): Solar project developers, wind farm operators, EV fleet operators. They buy cheap technology (benefiting from overcapacity price drops) and deploy it with subsidy support. This is the "picks and shovels" play without the mining risk.
- **Critical mineral producers** with assets in "friend-shored" jurisdictions: Lithium miners in Australia and Chile; nickel producers in Indonesia (complexity — partially Chinese-controlled); rare earth producers in Canada and Australia developing non-Chinese processing.
- **Power infrastructure:** Grid modernization, transmission, substations. All three great powers are simultaneously expanding electricity grids for electrification — this is the one sector where there is no geopolitical conflict, only investment need.
- **Nuclear:** The IRA's nuclear production credit (45J) made existing U.S. nuclear plants profitable for the first time in a decade. New nuclear (small modular reactors) is receiving state support across the U.S., UK, France, and Japan.

**Losers / Danger zones:**
- **Western clean energy manufacturers competing against Chinese incumbents:** U.S. and EU solar module and battery manufacturers trying to build up against Chinese cost structures with subsidy support. These businesses are viable only while subsidies persist and tariffs hold — both are politically fragile.
- **Automakers with unresolved battery supply chains:** Legacy auto OEMs (Ford, GM, Stellantis, Volkswagen) face enormous capital expenditure requirements to build U.S./EU-compliant battery supply chains. Returns are uncertain; timelines are slipping.
- **Commodities in the "overcapacity zone":** Polysilicon, standard solar modules — prices will remain structurally depressed.

## 7. Reflection Questions

1. **The optimization question revisited:** If the fastest and cheapest path to global decarbonization is to buy Chinese solar panels and batteries — and the Western strategy is to make alternatives that are 2-3x more expensive — how many additional tons of CO₂ are emitted during the 10-year period it takes to build Western manufacturing capacity? Is there a version of this calculation where the "green race" strategy makes climate change *worse* in the critical 2025-2035 decade, even if it produces a more resilient supply chain in 2035+?

2. **The Global South problem:** The IRA's domestic content and FEOC requirements make the subsidies available only to vehicles manufactured in North America. The result is that the cheapest EVs (made by BYD) are excluded from U.S. subsidies while the most expensive (made by Ford and GM in U.S. factories) receive them. Is there a version of clean energy policy that serves both decarbonization and supply chain security objectives simultaneously? What would that look like, and why hasn't any major government designed it?

3. **The copper signal:** Of all the critical minerals, copper is the least geopolitically contested and the most clearly demand-driven by electrification regardless of who wins the green race. Design a simple thesis for a copper investment based on the analysis in this lesson. Include: what you're buying (miners vs. ETF vs. futures), your 18-month price target thesis, what would make you wrong (the falsifiable condition), and what Databricks signal would tell you if that condition is materializing.
