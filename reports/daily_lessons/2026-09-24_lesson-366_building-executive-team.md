## Opening Question

*You have just raised capital. You have a small, high-trust founding team that has gotten the company to this point through speed, improvisation, and shared context that was never written down. Now you need to hire your first VP of Sales. Or your first Head of Research. Or a CFO.*

**How do you evaluate someone for a role you have never held — and how do you know when the "best person for the job" will actually destroy the culture that got you here?**

This is one of the most underestimated inflection points in a company's life. Most founding teams get it wrong not because they hire bad people, but because they hire the wrong *archetype* of person for where the company actually is. This lesson is about how to do it right.

---

## Part I — The Fundamental Mistake: Hiring for the Company You Want, Not the Company You Have

### Stage-Role Mismatch

The most common executive hiring mistake is stage-role mismatch: hiring a leader whose skill set is calibrated to a stage of company that yours hasn't reached yet.

Consider the VP of Sales archetype:

| Archetype | Best fit | Wrong fit |
|---|---|---|
| **The Hunter** | 0 → 1 stage, no repeatable motion, needs to find the buyers by trial and error | Series B+, where the motion is defined and you need someone to execute it |
| **The Builder** | Series A, motion is emerging, needs to be codified into process | Pre-seed, where process will slow you down |
| **The Scaler** | Series B+, repeatable motion exists, needs to hire and train a team to execute it | Seed, where the motion doesn't exist yet and you need someone to discover it |

Hiring a Scaler at Seed stage is very common. The candidate has an impressive resume, has built and managed 20-person sales teams, knows Salesforce deeply, and writes a 90-day plan that looks rigorous. And then they spend 6 months building process for a motion that doesn't exist yet, while the business needs someone willing to personally make 50 cold calls and figure out why prospects aren't buying.

**The discipline:** For every executive role, explicitly define the stage of the company the role needs to serve for the next 18 months — and hire for that stage, not the stage you hope to be at by then.

### The "Amazing but Wrong" Problem

Some of the most damaging executive hires are people who are genuinely excellent — just at something you don't need right now. A world-class enterprise sales leader who has scaled teams from 10 to 100 will likely struggle to be an effective individual contributor at a 12-person company. Not because they're not talented, but because the skill of building systematic sales process at scale and the skill of personally finding product-market fit in an unproven market are different skills, developed by different experiences, and rewarded by different environments.

This is not about intelligence or work ethic. It is about which muscles are developed and which have atrophied.

## Part II — Role Identification: What Does the Business Actually Need?

### The Bottleneck-First Framework

Before posting a job description, answer one question: *What is the single constraint on the company's progress right now?*

The answer to that question is the role you should hire first. Not the role that sounds most impressive. Not the role your investors are suggesting. Not the role that fixes a personal weakness you're tired of managing. The role that removes the binding constraint on the company's growth.

At Prospectra's current stage, ask:

1. **Is the constraint on the product?** — We don't have enough engineering bandwidth to build what customers are asking for. → Hire a technical lead or senior engineer.

2. **Is the constraint on revenue?** — We have a product and some customers but can't find more of them predictably. → Hire a commercial lead (first sales hire, or VP Sales if the motion is emerging).

3. **Is the constraint on research quality?** — Our geopolitical analysis is good but inconsistent; we can't hire analysts fast enough to maintain quality at scale. → Hire a Head of Research who has managed analyst teams.

4. **Is the constraint on operations?** — The founding team is spending too much time on things that shouldn't require founder attention (finance, HR, vendor management). → Hire an ops/finance lead.

The binding constraint changes over time. Reassess every quarter. The correct hire six months from now may be different from the correct hire today.

### Writing the Role Scorecard (Not a Job Description)

Most job descriptions describe the *inputs* of a role (experience required, skills listed) rather than the *outputs* the role is accountable for. This produces interviews that evaluate credentials rather than fit.

Use a **role scorecard** instead. For each executive hire, define:

1. **Mission:** One sentence. What is this person hired to accomplish in the next 18 months? (e.g., "Build the repeatable commercial motion that takes Prospectra from $200K ARR to $1M ARR with a team of 3.")

2. **Outcomes:** 3–5 specific, measurable results expected at 3, 6, and 12 months.

3. **Competencies:** The 5–8 behaviors that predict success in this specific role at this specific stage. Not generic competencies like "strong communicator." Specific ones: "builds trust with institutional buyers who have never heard of Prospectra" or "comfortable operating with no playbook and writing the first one."

4. **Red flags:** The specific patterns that would indicate the wrong archetype. Write these before interviewing anyone.

The scorecard is a forcing function. If you can't write it clearly, you don't know what you need — and you will hire someone impressive who solves the wrong problem.

## Part III — Hiring Leaders for Roles You've Never Held

### The Knowledge Gap Problem

Founders hiring their first VP of Engineering (when the founder is not an engineer) or their first Head of Research (when the founder is not a researcher) face a structural disadvantage: they cannot easily evaluate whether the candidate is genuinely excellent at the job, or merely sounds excellent in an interview.

This is a solvable problem, but it requires explicit process.

**Strategy 1: Reference-led evaluation**

The most information-rich signal about an executive candidate is what their former direct reports say about them — not what their former bosses say. References that former bosses give are almost always positive (that's what references do). References that former direct reports give are the ground truth.

Conduct structured reference checks with 3–5 people who reported directly to the candidate. Ask:

- "What did [candidate] do better than anyone else you've worked with?"
- "What did [candidate] struggle with that you'd want their next employer to know?"
- "If you were starting a company, would you hire [candidate]? For what role specifically?"
- "Tell me about a moment when something went badly wrong and how [candidate] handled it."

The answer to that last question is almost always the most informative.

**Strategy 2: Domain-expert evaluation**

For roles you cannot evaluate yourself, bring in someone who can — before you make an offer. This might be an advisor, a board member, or a trusted executive in your network who has held this role before. Ask them to conduct one of the interviews, evaluate the technical or domain-specific claims the candidate is making, and give you a calibrated view.

The cost is one meeting. The benefit is avoiding a 12-month mistake that costs you $400K in salary and another 12 months to recover from.

**Strategy 3: Work samples and structured problems**

Give candidates a real problem to solve — not a manufactured case study, but an actual challenge the company is facing. Evaluate not just the quality of the answer but how they approached the problem: Did they ask the right questions? Did they want to understand the context before proposing a solution? Did they show intellectual humility when challenged?

The work sample evaluates the skill. The process evaluates the judgment. You need both.

## Part IV — The Culture Continuity Problem

### What "Culture" Actually Is

Culture is not ping-pong tables, company values posted on a wall, or the adjectives people use in their LinkedIn profiles. Culture is the sum of the actual behaviors that are rewarded and tolerated within an organization — especially under pressure.

At a 10-person company, culture is essentially identical to the personality of the two or three founders. It propagates through daily decisions, through who gets praised and who gets corrected, through what gets written down and what gets assumed.

When you hire an executive — especially your first ones — you are importing a foreign operating culture and giving it formal authority. If the executive's implicit beliefs about how decisions get made, how conflict gets resolved, how ambiguity gets handled, and what "good work" looks like are incompatible with the founding culture, you will create fractures that are extremely difficult to repair.

### The Cultural Due Diligence Interview

Add one structured interview to every executive hiring process specifically for cultural alignment. Not "do you like working in startups?" (everyone says yes). Probe actual operating beliefs:

- "Tell me about a time you disagreed strongly with a decision your CEO made. What did you do?"
- "How do you handle a situation where two of your direct reports have a serious conflict? Walk me through what you actually do."
- "What does a good first week look like for a new hire joining your team?"
- "Tell me about a decision you made that turned out to be wrong. How did you handle it internally with your team?"

The answers to these questions reveal the candidate's real operating model — not their professed values.

### The Onboarding Architecture: The First 90 Days

Most companies onboard individual contributors with reasonable care and onboard executives with almost none. This is backwards: executives are network nodes, not just individual workers. The beliefs and behaviors they establish in their first 90 days propagate to everyone who reports to them.

**A 90-day executive onboarding framework:**

**Week 1–2: Listen first.** The executive's first priority is to understand the business as it is, not to impose the playbook from their last company. Build an explicit expectation: for the first two weeks, your job is to ask questions and form hypotheses. Not to fix things.

**Week 3–6: Diagnosis and relationship-building.** Meet every person in the company in a 1:1. Interview every customer you can reach. Build a picture of the constraints as they actually exist. At the end of week 6, write a short document: "Here is what I understand about the business; here is what I think needs to change; here is what I think should stay exactly as it is."

**Week 7–12: First real work.** Now begin executing. The decisions made in this window should be visible and collaborative — with regular check-ins with the founder/CEO to calibrate whether the direction is right.

**The trap to avoid:** Letting a new executive hire move too fast. The speed that impresses in month 2 often produces wreckage by month 5, because the executive made changes before they understood the business well enough to change it correctly.

## Part V — Special Case: Prospectra's First Key Hire

Prospectra's binding constraint, given the current arc (post-raise, moving toward commercial scale), is most likely in one of two places:

**Option A — Commercial lead.** The research product exists. The challenge is finding more institutional buyers at scale. The first commercial hire would be someone with a track record of selling research, data, or geopolitical intelligence products to institutional investors. Not a generic B2B SaaS sales rep — the domain knowledge matters for credibility with the buyer.

**Option B — Research lead.** The analytical framework exists. The challenge is producing it consistently at scale — more signals, more geographies, with institutional quality. The first research hire would be someone who has run an analyst team at a research firm or asset manager, understands what institutional clients expect from a research product, and can both produce work and manage junior analysts.

The right choice depends on where the actual constraint is. If Prospectra has more demand than it can fulfill with research quality, hire Option B first. If the product is strong but the pipeline is thin, hire Option A first.

This is a CEO decision that requires honest introspection about where the bottleneck actually is — not where it's most flattering to say it is.

## Key Concepts

- **Stage-role mismatch:** The most common executive hiring failure mode — hiring a scaler when you need a hunter
- **Binding constraint analysis:** Identify what actually limits the company's growth before writing a job description
- **Role scorecard:** Define mission, outcomes, competencies, and red flags before interviewing
- **Reference-led evaluation:** Former direct reports, not former bosses, are the ground truth
- **Culture continuity onboarding:** Listen-first, diagnosis, then execution — never reverse the order

## Investment Implications

Executive team quality is a primary driver of venture returns — arguably more predictive than market size or product quality at the early stage. For investors, executive hiring signals are real:

- **First executive hires as thesis validation:** Who a company hires first signals what they believe their binding constraint is. A seed-stage company that hires a CFO as their first executive is either managing complexity that requires it (good) or avoiding the harder commercial challenge (bad signal).

- **Hiring from competitors vs. adjacent industries:** Research shows that the highest-performing early-stage executive hires often come from adjacent industries rather than direct competitors — they bring transferable skills without the baggage of a "this is how we did it at my last company" mentality that can be culturally destructive.

- **Speed of executive turnover as a signal:** Executive tenure in the first 18 months post-raise is a market-observable signal of cultural health. Companies that cycle through their first VP Sales in under a year are either hiring badly or have an unfixable product problem. Either way, it's a yellow flag for the next round.

**Macro context (2026):** In the current environment of higher rates and longer fundraising timelines, capital efficiency in headcount is more important than ever. Companies that hired aggressively in 2021–2022 and are now managing the consequences of that over-hiring are an object lesson. The discipline of hiring slowly, for the right stage, for the binding constraint, is not just a culture argument — it is a survival argument in a tighter capital market.
