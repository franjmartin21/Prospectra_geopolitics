## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The week of May 25–31 is best described as **the oscillation trap made visible**. Iran fired ballistic missiles toward Kuwait on May 29 while its negotiators were telling Washington a deal was "mostly agreed." The US conducted "self-defense strikes" in southern Iran on May 25–26, briefly pushed Brent back above $100, then walked it back with Rubio signaling diplomatic patience — and Brent fell 5% in a day. The month ended at $92.56, down 19% from its April peak, driven entirely by deal optimism rather than any change in physical reality: the Strait of Hormuz is still at 5% of pre-war traffic. Meanwhile, Russia launched one of its largest attacks on Kyiv since 2022 and hit a populated area in Romania — the first time a Russian drone has injured civilians in NATO territory. And at the Shangri-La Dialogue in Singapore, the US Defense Secretary delivered a notably softer China message than last year, revealing the depth of strategic bandwidth consumed by Iran.

The CEO's read: the market is pricing what it wants to be true. The framework says to price what is constrained to be true.

## EVENT 1 — IRAN/HORMUZ: THE OSCILLATION TRAP

### What Happened

Three distinct sub-events in seven days that together reveal the true structure of the crisis:

**May 25–26 — US "Self-Defense" Strikes:** The US military conducted strikes in southern Iran against missile launch sites and boats it said were attempting to lay mines. American jets killed four IRGC troops. Iran's IRGC claimed to have downed an MQ-9 Reaper drone and fired on an F-35. Brent jumped above $100/bbl on the news. Oil markets saw this as evidence the ceasefire was collapsing.

**May 27 — Rubio De-escalation:** Secretary Rubio stated the US would give Iran talks "every chance to succeed." Oil dropped more than 5%, settling at $94.29 — a single-day reversal of the gains from the strikes.

**May 29 — Kuwait Ballistic Missiles:** Iran launched ballistic missiles toward Kuwait, successfully intercepted. On the same day, negotiators confirmed the 60-day MOU was "mostly agreed" but required Trump's approval. Brent closed the month at $92.56.

**May 31 close:** Brent -19% for May, the largest monthly decline since the crisis began — on no physical change whatsoever.

### Geopolitical Significance

The oscillation pattern is not noise. It is the structural signal. What the week reveals:

1. **The IRGC and the political negotiators are not operating under unified command.** Political officials signal a deal; the IRGC fires ballistic missiles at Kuwait. This is the split-principal problem — the organization responsible for physical Hormuz disruption is not the organization agreeing to stop it. A signed MOU between Trump and Iranian political leadership does not bind the IRGC.

2. **The market is pricing deal probability, not physical reality.** Hormuz traffic at 5% of pre-war levels is indistinguishable from last week. Insurance premiums remain elevated. Suezmax rerouting through the Cape of Good Hope continues. The 19% May decline in Brent occurred while the physical supply disruption was unchanged.

3. **The deal sequencing problem is structural.** Iran wants sovereignty over the Strait acknowledged before nuclear concessions. The US wants nuclear concessions before Hormuz normalization. These are incompatible starting positions. The MOU framework can address a 60-day ceasefire extension — it cannot resolve the sequencing dispute.

**CEO determination:** The war premium is compressed but not eliminated. The structural risk premium — insurance, rerouting costs, non-Gulf supply reliability discount — survives any MOU signing. The falsification condition on all energy long positions (formal deal signed + structural normalization + non-Gulf supply expansion) remains far from triggered.

## EVENT 2 — SHANGRI-LA DIALOGUE: THE STRATEGIC BANDWIDTH SIGNAL

### What Happened

The 23rd annual Shangri-La Dialogue security forum ran May 29–31 in Singapore. US Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth delivered the keynote. China's Defense Minister Dong Jun skipped for the second consecutive year. Key Hegseth remarks:
- Called China's military buildup a source of "rightful alarm" but said the US was "not seeking needless confrontation"
- Confirmed "no change in our status" toward Taiwan
- Urged Asian allies to increase defense spending
- Said the US-China relationship should be one of "strategic stability based on fairness and reciprocity"
- Notably toned down vs. 2025 when he warned the China threat "could be imminent"

India's Defense Minister used the forum to deepen defense ties with the US and NATO.

### Geopolitical Significance

The tonal shift from Hegseth is not reassuring — it is revealing. The US is signaling strategic restraint in the Indo-Pacific at precisely the moment it is consumed by Iran. The message received by Asian allies: Washington's bandwidth is finite, and it is currently allocated to the Persian Gulf.

China's second consecutive no-show confirms Beijing's reading: the Shangri-La forum is a US-managed venue that serves no purpose when China's strategic position is strengthening. Beijing does not need to show up to a forum to defend its position in the South China Sea — its island fortifications speak for it.

**The Taiwan implication:** Hegseth's "no change in our status" formula was notably opaque. He declined to confirm future arms sales, deferring to Trump personally. This creates an ambiguity premium in Taiwan risk that was not present 12 months ago. The US deterrence signal toward China is weaker in May 2026 than it was in May 2025 — not because policy changed, but because the US is visibly stretched.

**The India implication (bullish for INDA):** India's deepening of defense ties with the US and NATO at Shangri-La is the non-alignment thesis in action. India is simultaneously buying Russian oil and strengthening its US security partnership. No other country can do this. The Shangri-La appearance confirms India's role as the indispensable Indo-Pacific partner for US strategy — and validates the Tranche 1 INDA position opened May 30.

## EVENT 3 — RUSSIA HITS KYIV AND ROMANIA: ESCALATION THRESHOLD TESTED

### What Happened

Russia launched one of its largest attacks on Kyiv since the start of the war during the May 25–31 window — hundreds of drones and missiles, including a strike that devastated Kyiv's Chornobyl Museum. Separately and more strategically significant: a Russian drone struck a densely populated area in southeastern Romania, injuring two people. This was confirmed as the first time a Russian weapon has caused injuries in a populated civilian area within NATO territory during the conflict.

In the diplomatic sphere, Putin reiterated his claim that the war was "coming to an end" and expressed willingness to meet Zelensky in Moscow or a neutral location. Zelensky rejected the Moscow framing.

Ukraine and Sweden finalized a deal: Ukraine to purchase up to 20 Gripen fighter jets while Sweden donates 16 from its current fleet — the most significant Western combat aircraft transfer of the conflict outside F-16 deliveries.

### Geopolitical Significance

The Romania drone incident is the most significant escalation marker of the week. For the first time, Russian munitions have injured civilians in a NATO member state. This is not accidental: Russian drone trajectories over Romanian airspace are not random navigation errors at this stage of the conflict. This is a probe of NATO's Article 5 threshold — a test of whether the alliance will treat this as an act of war or absorb it as collateral.

The alliance's non-response (strongly worded statements; no Article 5 invocation) tells Russia what the threshold is. That information has strategic value for Moscow.

The Gripen deal is the other signal: Western combat aircraft are now flowing to Ukraine as a matter of policy. This is a structural escalation in Ukrainian capability regardless of ceasefire negotiations.

**CEO reading on the ceasefire:** Putin's "war is ending" language is, as the CEO assessed in the May 25 briefing, audience management — not an operational signal. The massive Kyiv strikes during the same week reveal the actual operational disposition. The frozen conflict thesis (extended ceasefire + no structural resolution + sustained European defense spending) remains the most likely 18-month outcome.

## EVENT 4 — US FISCAL TRAJECTORY: THE SLOW-MOTION SIGNAL

### What Happened

US debt held by the public as of May 31, 2026 stands at approximately $31.27 trillion — exceeding nominal GDP ($31.22 trillion) for only the second time since World War II. The first was the brief COVID spike; this time, there is no crisis end-date. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (signed July 4, 2025) adds an estimated $3.4–6.0 trillion to the 10-year deficit trajectory depending on scoring methodology. The Supreme Court's ruling striking down bulk IEEPA tariffs removes ~$1.7 trillion in projected revenue. Fitch projects the 2026 deficit at 7.9% of GDP.

Moody's downgrade (Aaa → Aa1, covered in the May 26 briefing) has now settled into market pricing with minimal yield reaction — 10-year Treasuries remain in the 4.00–4.50% range. The absence of a yield spike is not comfort; it is evidence of the market's inability to price a slow-moving structural deterioration.

### Geopolitical Significance

The US fiscal trajectory is geopolitically consequential in two ways that compound each other:

1. **Dollar reserve currency credibility:** The US requires foreign buyers of Treasuries (Japan, China, Gulf oil exporters) at precisely the moment its relationships with those buyers are most adversarial. Japan is a carry-trade-dependent holder. China is in a structured decoupling. Gulf states are watching the Hormuz management with concern. The demand for Treasuries is deteriorating structurally while supply is increasing structurally. This is the Triffin Dilemma made real.

2. **Fiscal dominance of monetary policy:** As defense spending ($150bn new via OBBBA) and entitlement spending compound the deficit, the Fed's ability to tighten without triggering a sovereign debt crisis shrinks. This is the fiscal dominance thesis (Lesson 73) in live operation. The structural inflation consequence: the Fed's 2% target becomes a political aspiration, not a monetary achievable.

## EVENT 5 — INDIA-US DEFENSE DEEPENING: THE NON-ALIGNMENT DIVIDEND

### What Happened

India's Defense Minister used the Shangri-La Dialogue to publicly deepen defense ties with the United States and NATO allies — the highest-profile multilateral defense engagement India has participated in at this level. The engagement follows India's successful management of the Operation Sindoor aftermath (May 10 anniversary, ceasefire holding) and India's continued purchase of discounted Russian crude oil under the informal Hormuz-response framework. India's GDP growth for 2026 is confirmed at 6.6–7.4%, the highest of any major economy.

The India-Pakistan ceasefire (one year anniversary, May 10) holds — fragile but intact. Backchannel diplomatic talks exist. The structural nuclear deterrence calculation between both states has shifted post-Sindoor: India is more assertive, Pakistan more reliant on nuclear signaling.

### Geopolitical Significance

India is executing the most sophisticated non-alignment strategy of any nation in 2026. The elements:
- Buying Russian oil at $15–20/bbl discount (energy security)
- Deepening US and NATO security ties (geopolitical positioning)
- Emerging as the US's preferred Indo-Pacific manufacturing partner (economic integration)
- Maintaining strategic autonomy on Taiwan, Russia-Ukraine, and Iran

No other country can simultaneously occupy all four of these positions. The Shangri-La defense deepening moves India from "non-aligned" to "selectively aligned when it suits" — which is the correct strategic posture for a rising power navigating multipolar transition.

The India-Pakistan nuclear risk (tail risk, not base case) remains the single most important falsification condition on the INDA thesis. A terrorist attack triggering rapid escalation remains the low-probability, high-impact event to monitor.
