## EVENT 1: SAUDI EAST-WEST PIPELINE SHUTDOWN — THE LAST HORMUZ BYPASS IS GONE

### What Happened
On September 10–11, 2026, Iraqi-origin drones attacked Saudi Arabia's East-West Crude Oil Pipeline (also called the Petroline) at pumping stations in the Riyadh and Medina provinces. Saudi Arabia shut down the pipeline on September 11 as a precaution; satellite imagery confirmed fires at multiple pumping stations along the route. The East-West Pipeline was Saudi Arabia's *only* meaningful Hormuz bypass route — it carried up to 5 million barrels per day from the eastern Ghawar fields to the Yanbu Red Sea terminal, enabling Saudi crude to reach export markets without transiting Hormuz. With Hormuz at 23% of pre-war throughput and the East-West Pipeline now offline, Saudi Arabia's export capacity is at its most constrained in the country's history. Brent hit $110 intraday on September 11, settled at $107.46 on September 15, and has traded in the $103–108 range through the week as markets try to price the duration of the shutdown.

### Geopolitical Significance
This attack changes the strategic architecture of the oil crisis in three critical ways. First, the drone launch from Iraq is a signal that Iran's proxy network is activating beyond the Hormuz theater — Iraq-based Shia militia groups (Kataib Hezbollah, aligned with IRGC Quds Force) are now attacking Saudi Arabia's domestic energy infrastructure. This is a significant geographic expansion of the conflict. Second, the strategic logic is clear: Iran calculated that attacking the Hormuz bypass removes the remaining Saudi oil export capacity, maximizing price pressure on the US-led coalition and accelerating pressure for a settlement. Third, Saudi Arabia's position is now extraordinarily uncomfortable — it is a US security partner being attacked by Iran's proxies, but its own oil infrastructure is more exposed than at any point since the 1991 Gulf War. Watch for Saudi signaling on whether Riyadh will accelerate quiet Iran talks separately from the US diplomatic track.

Fed Chair Kevin Warsh held the shortest post-FOMC press conference on record for a regularly-scheduled meeting, approximately 30 minutes. He framed the hike as offering "good news for Americans who don't own financial assets" by protecting real take-home pay. No explicit signal on November meeting direction, but the framing was unmistakably hawkish.
