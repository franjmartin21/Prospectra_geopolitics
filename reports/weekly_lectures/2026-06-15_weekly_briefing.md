## EVENT 1 — G7 ÉVIAN OPENS: THE IRAN WAR SUMMIT

### What Happened
The 52nd G7 Leaders' Summit opens today in Évian, France, running June 15–17 with France's Emmanuel Macron as host. The agenda, originally designed around development finance, global aid commitments, and digital governance, has been entirely overtaken by the US-led war against Iran and its energy-market consequences. Alongside the G7 seven, Kenya, India, Brazil, Egypt, and South Korea attend as invited guests — a configuration that is itself a signal: Macron is using the summit to demonstrate that middle powers and the Global South cannot be excluded from decisions that affect global energy supply. Trump, attending in person, faces sharp pressure from European leaders over surging fuel costs, burden-sharing asymmetries, and the lack of a clear diplomatic off-ramp on Iran.

### Geopolitical Significance
This is the first G7 since the Hormuz closure became structurally entrenched — and the gap between the US posture (maximize military pressure on Iran while managing the oil-price fallout) and the European posture (recover economic normalcy as fast as possible even if it means a diplomatic concession to Tehran) is genuinely wide. Macron's invitation of India, Brazil, and South Korea is not coincidence — it is a demonstration that middle powers are building coordination mechanisms *around* the US-Europe binary rather than inside it. The World Economic Forum observed before this summit that "this could be a middle power moment." The CEO agrees: if Évian produces a joint communiqué on Iran that the middle-power guests co-sign, it changes the diplomatic geometry of the crisis in ways that reduce the IRGC's ability to frame Hormuz as purely an "Anglo-Zionist" conflict. Watch the communiqué language very carefully.

## EVENT 2 — OIL AT $112: THE ENTRENCHMENT PREMIUM ARRIVES

### What Happened
Brent crude crossed $112/bbl this week — up roughly 18% from the $93–95 range where it settled at the time of last week's briefing — as the US military announced that mine-clearance operations in the Strait of Hormuz are expected to take **six months**, extending any credible resolution timeline to late-2026 at the earliest. Approximately 2,000 commercial vessels remain stranded in the Gulf, tanker insurance has been cancelled by major underwriters since early March, and all major carriers have suspended Strait transits. United Airlines CEO Scott Kirby publicly warned that oil could reach $175/bbl and remain elevated through 2027 if the standoff persists. European governments are now formally planning for fuel-shortage scenarios over the coming winter.

### Geopolitical Significance
Last week's briefing used the phrase "quiet crisis" and warned it was exactly the phase when markets misprice. The $112 print is the market waking up from that mis-pricing. The six-month mine-clearance announcement is analytically crucial: it reveals that the US military does not expect a near-term Hormuz reopening even in a best-case diplomatic scenario. This converts the Hormuz closure from a "crisis event" (duration uncertain, priced as transient) to a **structural regime change** (duration bounded from below, now priced as entrenched). The distinction matters for portfolios: in "crisis event" pricing, the correct trade is mean-reversion. In "structural regime" pricing, the correct trade is staying long and adding on the dips that optimistic headlines produce. The CEO made this call four weeks ago. This week's price action validates the analytical framework, not just the trade.

## EVENT 3 — OPEC+ FRACTURES: UAE EXITS, THE BLOC VOTES ANYWAY

### What Happened
The June 7 OPEC+ meeting — the most consequential since the 2020 price war — produced two decisions that pull in opposite directions: (1) the **UAE formally departed the bloc**, the most significant defection since Venezuela's marginalization, stripping OPEC+ of its largest Gulf producer outside Saudi Arabia; and (2) the remaining seven core members (Saudi Arabia, Russia, Iraq, Kuwait, Kazakhstan, Algeria, Oman) voted to increase collective output by **188,000 barrels per day** in June — slightly below the 206,000 bpd raise from the prior month, and effectively symbolic against the scale of Hormuz-driven disruption. The increase is framed as part of the "adjustment schedule" introduced in April 2023, and members stressed flexibility — increases can be "paused or reversed" depending on market conditions. The next formal meeting is in July.

### Geopolitical Significance
The UAE's departure is the structural story that the headline-focused market is likely underweighting. Abu Dhabi has spent years building spare production capacity and an independent energy-sector strategy (ADNOC expansion, refinery investments, LNG diversification) precisely to reduce its dependence on the OPEC+ quota framework. Its exit signals that the Gulf's second-largest producer now believes it can extract more value outside the cartel than inside — a calculation that directly undermines Saudi Arabia's ability to manage prices through production discipline. With UAE gone, OPEC+ is increasingly a Saudi-Russian coalition with five smaller partners, not a pan-Gulf bloc. Saudi Arabia is simultaneously facing its own fiscal constraint: Vision 2030 requires elevated oil revenues, but Riyadh also faces US pressure not to constrain supply into an already-tight market during the Hormuz crisis. This is the "OPEC+ fracture" thesis that Lesson 4 flagged — it is now operating in real time.

No clean directional call |

## EVENT 4 — FOMC TOMORROW: WARSH'S INAUGURAL TEST IN AN OIL SHOCK

### What Happened
The Federal Open Market Committee convenes June 16–17 for the **first meeting chaired by Kevin Warsh**, the new Fed Chair confirmed earlier in 2026 following Powell's departure. The context could hardly be more complex: Brent at $112/bbl injects direct inflationary pressure via energy costs; the Section 301/122 tariff architecture adds goods-price pressure; the DXY reclaimed 100 last week on rate-differential and safe-haven flows; and Warsh arrives with a reputation for fiscal hawkishness that markets will be testing against the reality of an economy being squeezed from multiple directions simultaneously. Current Fed target range is 3.50–3.75%. Futures markets have been cycling between "one cut in 2026" and "no cuts until 2027" as the data — and the geopolitical backdrop — keep refusing to give a clean signal.

### Geopolitical Significance
This meeting is the textbook **"geopolitical constraint on monetary policy"** dynamic from Lesson 91 — but with an added dimension: Warsh is a *new* chair, which means his communication choices this week will set the credibility baseline against which all future statements are measured. If he signals that the Fed will look through oil-driven inflation (prioritizing growth), he validates fiscal-dominance fears and weakens the dollar. If he signals that oil-driven inflation is as real as any other (implying rates stay higher for longer), he creates conditions for a growth slowdown that further strains an economy already paying $112/bbl at the pump. There is no clean exit — which is exactly the "impossible position" the CEO described in the June 2 briefing. The meeting is tomorrow. The CEO's positioning is already set (short-duration, long real assets, long gold, structurally USD-bearish); the FOMC is a data point to refine, not a trigger for repositioning.

## EVENT 5 — US-CHINA: 12.5% SECTION 301 PROPOSED, RARE EARTH DÉTENTE HOLDS (FOR NOW)

### What Happened
Two signals from the US-China tech and trade contest this week point in opposite directions but describe the same structural dynamic:

**Escalation:** The USTR proposed a new **12.5% Section 301 tariff on Chinese goods** via a forced-labor investigation — a framing that (a) carries a legal rationale independent of the broader trade war, (b) is applicable to Chinese supply-chain adjacents, not just direct Chinese imports, and (c) layers on top of existing Section 301/232/122 tariff architecture to create a compounding friction regime.

**Détente (temporary):** Chinese rare earth and magnet export controls — the April 2025 retaliatory escalation that threatened US defense supply chains — are **paused until November 10, 2026**, per an agreement reached in October 2025 and extended through a deal apparently struck in recent weeks. However, China's export control architecture — including the "0.1% Chinese-origin content" rule that brings foreign-made products into the licensing regime — remains in place structurally. Australia responded this year by ordering Chinese investors to divest from domestic rare earth mining; the US-Australia Critical Minerals Framework is active.

### Geopolitical Significance
The rare-earth pause is the most interesting signal. Détentes of this kind are not resolutions — they are breathing space purchased by mutual self-interest, and they do not alter the underlying strategic architecture. China's Ministry of Commerce built the export-control system as a permanent strategic tool (the licensing bureaucracy, the end-use verification requirements, the institutional dependencies) that will outlast any single diplomatic cycle. This means every November is a potential cliff edge where the détente either re-extends or doesn't. The CEO's read: **November 10, 2026 is a major event-risk date to calendar now** — if the détente expires without renewal (especially if US-China tensions re-escalate between now and then, which is the base case), the impact on defense and clean-energy supply chains will be swift and severe. The new 12.5% Section 301 tariff proposal, meanwhile, confirms that the US side is *also* adding to the structural friction regime even during the détente window — this is not a de-escalation track, it is two parallel tracks operating simultaneously.
